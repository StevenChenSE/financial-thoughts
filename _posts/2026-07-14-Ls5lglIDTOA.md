**Mortgage rates climb, Canada faces condo bailout backlash, and population shifts reshape the housing outlook.**

In a comprehensive analysis of the current housing landscape, Ron Butler, host of the *Angry Mortgage Podcast* and principal mortgage broker at Butler Mortgage, issued a stark warning to market participants: those hoping for a quick housing rebound should prepare to wait at least 18 months. With mortgage rates trending upward, a controversial government bailout of condominium developers in British Columbia, and a significant demographic shift in Canada, the housing market is navigating a period of prolonged correction and structural change.

## Mortgage Rates and Inflationary Pressures

Butler highlighted that the housing market is already feeling the strain of elevated borrowing costs. Weekly mortgage demand is dropping as rates remain stuck in a narrow, high range. Recent data shows the average contract interest rate for a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage with conforming loan balances increasing to 6.58%. Prediction markets on Kowala suggest a 64% probability that the 30-year rate will exceed 6.7% by the end of the year.

Butler agrees with this upward trajectory, attributing rate movements primarily to supply and demand dynamics rather than central bank policy alone. He noted that once the 6% plateau was crossed, the market began to "wilt," particularly given record-high price points in many regions. Several factors support continued rate increases:

*   **Geopolitical Tensions:** Ongoing conflicts in the Middle East and a lack of de-escalation contribute to global inflationary pressures.
*   **Oil Prices:** Butler emphasized that oil prices remain a critical indicator. While major economies like the U.S. and China appear to be managing supply to prevent extreme spikes, any sustained price above $80 to $90 per barrel is inherently inflationary. If WTI crude remains elevated, it will drive up the 10-year yield, mortgage rates, and the Fed funds rate.
*   **Absence of Softening Factors:** There are currently no clear catalysts that would drive rates down. Butler pointed out that even the U.S. Federal Reserve may face hawkish pressure, potentially influenced by new leadership such as Kevin Warsh, who does not appear to be a dove on rates.

## Housing Market Correction and Foreclosure Trends

Despite reports from organizations like the Toronto Regional Real Estate Board (TRREB) suggesting market improvement, Butler dismissed such narratives as "real estate propaganda." He argued that a rise in sales from 32-year lows to 15-year lows does not constitute a rebound, as activity remains well below 10-year averages.

The correction is intensifying in key Canadian markets. Butler noted that foreclosures and power of sales are rising in a "hockey stick" pattern from historically low bases, signaling growing distress among homeowners. The weakness is pervasive, with strong markets in Quebec and Alberta joining British Columbia and Ontario in rolling over.

**Advice for Market Participants:**
*   **Sellers:** Butler advises sellers to execute moves immediately rather than waiting for a rebound. He warned that waiting three to six months for better results is unrealistic, as prices have consistently declined over the past year. A rebound is unlikely before 18 months, and prices could remain flat for years.
*   **Buyers:** Patience is key. In British Columbia, Butler sees more runway for price declines. In Ontario, buyers should start getting serious in the fall, monitoring the market for opportunities that fit their budget.

## Canada's Condo Developer Bailout Controversy

A significant portion of the discussion focused on Prime Minister Mark Carney's announcement to spend up to $1.4 billion to purchase underwater condominium units from developers in British Columbia. The plan aims to convert over 2,200 vacant units into affordable housing. Butler, who previously bet this would never happen due to political optics, called the initiative a "communications disaster" and a misstep for the government.

Butler criticized the bailout on several grounds:
*   **Market Interference:** He argued that the government is derailing the free market process. In a capitalist system, developers making severe business mistakes should face receivership, where assets are auctioned to find the true market price. Government intervention with taxpayer money prevents this discovery mechanism.
*   **Job Creation Myth:** The justification that the bailout saves jobs was rejected by Butler, who noted that no new condo projects are likely to start in Vancouver regardless of the bailout. The construction sector is already in a downturn, and workers are ready to move on as soon as viable projects emerge.
*   **Product Quality:** Butler highlighted the public's disdain for high-rise condo developers, citing astronomically high prices and poor product quality, such as buildings without garbage chutes.

The plan reportedly targets lower-cost units in the Fraser Valley rather than luxury towers in Vancouver or Burnaby. Butler predicted the bailout will proceed quietly out of the limelight, though he questioned the efficiency of using taxpayer funds when the government already has programs like Build Canada Homes dedicated to affordable housing.

## Population Decline and Economic Divergence

Butler underscored a dramatic shift in Canada's demographics as a headwind for the housing market. Under former Prime Minister Justin Trudeau, immigration surged by 3.2 million people over three years, overwhelming healthcare, housing, and rental systems. The new government under Carney has halted new immigration, leading to a population decline as some newcomers depart due to a lack of jobs and permanent residency pathways.

This shrinking population makes a housing rebound increasingly difficult. Butler contrasted Canada's "profound economic malaise" with the resilience of the U.S. economy. While the U.S. faces a K-shaped recovery but is not struggling, Canada is experiencing manufacturing job losses and construction pressure.

Monetary policy divergence is also emerging. Governor Tiff Maklem of the Bank of Canada is reluctant to raise rates due to the weak domestic economy, though rising inflation could force his hand. In contrast, the U.S. economy shows signs of overheating in certain sectors, increasing the likelihood of Fed rate hikes. Butler noted that Canada will likely hold off on rate increases as long as possible, even if the U.S. moves first.

## Conclusion

Butler concluded that the ongoing housing correction, while painful for construction workers, is ultimately beneficial for the broader Canadian economy. He emphasized that affordability has deteriorated significantly, with the average first-time homebuyer age rising from 27 thirty years ago to 40 today. The market is finally waking up to fair value, but the path forward requires patience. With rates likely to remain elevated, government interventions creating distortions, and demographic trends shifting, the housing market is in for a prolonged period of adjustment.
