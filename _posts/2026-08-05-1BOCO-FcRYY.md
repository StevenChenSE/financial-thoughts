## The Skyscraper Curse and the New AI Bubble

Markets are currently hitting all-time highs across the board. As of August 4th, the S&P 500 and the Dow Jones have reached new peaks, driven significantly by massive capital expenditure (capex) in the artificial intelligence sector. Companies like Palantir and Caterpillar have seen substantial gains on the back of expectations that AI-related capex spending will jump by 77% to reach $725 billion by 2026.

However, Dr. Mark Thornton, Senior Fellow at the Mises Institute, suggests that these record highs may signal the top of a boom cycle rather than sustainable growth. Referencing his work on "The Skyscraper Curse," Thornton explains a historical pattern where record-setting skyscrapers coincide with the onset of global economic crises. From the Singer Building during the Panic of 1907 to the Chrysler Building during the Great Depression, and the Burj Khalifa following the 2008 financial crisis, these mega-projects are born from artificially low interest rates.

According to Austrian economic theory, these ultra-low rates distort the capital structure of firms and accelerate technology adoption unnaturally. Today, Thornton argues, AI data centers are functioning as the modern-day equivalent of skyscrapers. While data centers do not require the same vertical construction technology, they demand immense capital and rely on the same speculative credit environment. Thornton warns that this rapid cluster of investment will eventually lead to a cluster of "entrepreneurial errors" when profitability fails to match the massive spending, signaling a potential bubble burst.

## Tariffs as Political Theater and Economic Hostility

The economic landscape is further complicated by aggressive tariff policies. In a recent development, a coalition of 25 Democratic-led states sued the Trump administration over new tariffs ranging from 10% to 12.5% on imported goods. Thornton views these tariffs not just as economic policy, but as political theater and acts of hostility.

History offers a grim parallel. In his 2004 book, *Tariffs, Blockades, and Inflation*, Thornton detailed how protectionist policies in the 1860s created antagonism between Northern manufacturers and Southern farmers, contributing to the American Civil War. Today, he argues that tariffs are a "political act of hostility" that hurts American consumers who ultimately pay the costs, as evidenced by recent refund demands and corporate windfalls for companies like Amazon and Apple.

Thornton warns that such protectionism fosters an "economic Napoleon complex," potentially leading to military confrontation similar to the escalations seen before World War I. By targeting oil suppliers and engaging in aggressive trade posturing, the administration risks spiraling into conflicts that voters and politicians may eventually lose control over.

## The Looming Agricultural and Energy Crisis

Beyond the stock market and trade wars, Thornton highlights severe risks in the global supply chain, particularly regarding energy and agriculture. The conflict in the Persian Gulf threatens to exacerbate bottlenecks in diesel and jet fuel production. Diesel is critical for transporting raw materials, farm production, and final goods; shortages or price spikes could ripple through the entire economy.

Underlying this is a structural energy problem. For decades, the deemphasis on fossil fuels and nuclear power has reduced global output from older oil and gas fields, while investment in new exploration has lagged. This scarcity extends to essential byproducts like sulfuric acid, which is vital for mining copper—a metal currently in tight supply due to demand from AI data centers and solar panel manufacturing.

Thornton predicts a looming agricultural crisis. Farmers worldwide, particularly in developing economies, are facing dwindling supplies of fertilizer and rising diesel costs. This combination threatens to diminish crop yields in coming seasons, leading to food shortages and higher grocery prices for consumers globally.

## The Federal Reserve’s Interest Rate Trap

The Federal Reserve finds itself in a precarious position, caught between high inflation and a massive national debt of $40 trillion. With the 30-year Treasury yield hovering around 5.2%, the Fed faces a difficult choice. Cutting short-term rates could spur further inflation, driving long-term yields even higher.

Thornton suggests the Fed may resort to "financial repression," a strategy last used during World War II, where the central bank directly purchases government bonds to suppress interest rates. This would likely involve an expansion of quantitative easing targeting the long end of the yield curve. Additionally, Thornton notes that central banks often resort to redefining inflation statistics to appear closer to their 2% target, despite consumers experiencing much higher price increases.

The appointment of Kevin Walsh as Fed Chairman was interpreted by the market as a hawkish move, temporarily depressing precious metal prices. However, Thornton argues that political events, including the appointment and geopolitical actions in Iran, were used to distract from the underlying devaluation of the dollar.

## Why the Economy Is Ripe for a Crash

Drawing on the Austrian Business Cycle Theory, Thornton explains that the economy is "ripe for a crash" due to malinvestments fueled by years of artificially low interest rates. While the Fed has managed to delay the inevitable by injecting liquidity—such as transferring $2.5 trillion from repo holdings into the economy—this merely extends the boom cycle without solving the structural issues.

Thornton emphasizes the "knowledge problem" identified by Friedrich Hayek: no central authority can accurately aggregate decentralized economic data to make perfect policy decisions. Consequently, the Fed is prone to both small and catastrophic errors.

Looking ahead, Thornton is bearish on traditional assets. He expects zero or negative real returns on stocks and bonds over the next decade due to high valuations and inflation. Conversely, consumer prices for food and energy are likely to rise, eroding real wages. For long-term investors, understanding these cycles and the role of assets like physical gold and silver is crucial for navigating the potential economic turbulence ahead.
