## A candid conversation about motherhood, artistry, and the making of the new record.

Gracie Abrams and Olivia Rodrigo recently sat down at the Cherry Lane Theater for an intimate conversation celebrating the release of Abrams' highly anticipated new album, *Daughter from Hell*. With early access to the record, Rodrigo offered a fan's and peer's perspective on the project, while Abrams opened up about the album's deeply personal themes, her creative evolution, and the enduring friendship that has grown between the two songwriters since their early days connecting via Instagram DMs.

### The Title Track and a Love Letter to Her Mother

The album's title, *Daughter from Hell*, is drawn from a track of the same name, which Abrams reveals was one of the last songs written for the project. Recorded in London with producer Aaron Dessner, the song emerged during a moment of profound emotional clarity. Dessner played a baritone guitar in an open tuning that Abrams describes as visceral, prompting her to step away from the studio to write the lyrics in silence.

"Daughter from Hell is a love letter to my mom," Abrams explains. "It's an apology. It's the big reveal that I don't know if I will ever live up to the woman that she is."

Abrams notes that the title felt inevitable upon writing the song, a sensation she hadn't experienced with previous projects. She reflects on a shift in perspective, acknowledging that she was "brutal" to her parents during her youth. Now, she values time with her family deeply, recognizing the fragility of life. The song captures a complex mix of intense love, shame, and the ache of wanting to embody the strength and character of her mother.

### Songwriting Evolution and Restraint

Rodigo praises the album for its restraint and layered storytelling, noting that multiple reveals new details with each listen. Abrams discusses her growth as a songwriter, emphasizing a desire to write from a place of honesty without cruelty. She aims to inhabit both the protagonist and antagonist roles in her narratives, acknowledging that she has been both the "unreliable friend" and the one left behind in various relationships.

This maturity is evident in tracks like "Afflictions," which Rodrigo describes as a gut-wrenching yet mature love song that avoids superficiality. Abrams admits the difficulty of writing about love without diminishing its depth, but she feels this track captures the safety and purity of a profound connection.

Other key tracks discussed include:
*   **Hit the Wall:** Written at Electric Lady Studios while Abrams was missing a close person named Audrey. The song features samples of Dessner sampling his own piano playing. Abrams recalls writing the lyrics in a state of "cute loneliness," describing the process as a cathartic release of intrusive thoughts.
*   **Men Like You:** Noted for its "vocal gymnastics," this track is a fan favorite for its energetic and playful nature.
*   **Death Wish:** Cited by Rodrigo as a standout for its poignant lyrics and lack of filler, evoking strong emotions.
*   **Cold Goodbyes:** A track that marks the album's origins, written two years prior during the *Secret of Us* tour. Abrams recalls a productive day where she also recorded "That's So True" and "I Told You Things."
*   **Mini Bar:** Created during a collaborative week with Audrey, showcasing a zippy attitude.

### Production and Sonic Exploration

The album's sonic landscape was shaped significantly by Abrams' time on tour and her collaboration with Dessner. During the *Secret of Us* tour, Abrams utilized an OP1 synthesizer to explore sounds distinct from her acoustic guitar work, leading to unique textures that made their way into the new record.

Abrams describes Dessner as "medicine" during a period where she felt disconnected and unsure of her voice after the relentless pace of touring. His patience and musical intuition helped her navigate the creative process. Additionally, Dan contributed to the track "Look at My Life," adding to the album's diverse production palette. Abrams emphasizes that having uninterrupted time to hibernate and focus on writing was crucial, marking the first time in four years she could remain in one place to develop her art.

### Visuals, Glastonbury, and Personal Growth

Abrams discusses the visual identity of the album, which she aimed to keep raw and free of artifice. Working with stylist Spencer Singer and photographer Julie Grieves, she sought an aesthetic that felt authentic to her current self. Abrams notes that cutting her hair shorter has helped her feel more like herself, moving away from the dress-heavy aesthetic of her previous tour toward a style that feels more aligned with her evolved identity.

The conversation also touches on Abrams' recent performance at Glastonbury, which Rodrigo attended. Abrams describes the experience as magical, admitting to a panic attack backstage before feeling a transcendent connection with the audience once on stage. "I am the vessel," she reflects, highlighting a moment where the performance felt effortless and joyful.

Rodigo expresses admiration for Abrams' growth, noting how the album reflects a woman who is deeply in tune with her emotions and her art. She praises Abrams for maintaining dignity and empathy in her songwriting, even when addressing conflict.

### Friendship and Future Projects

Beyond the music, the duo celebrates their friendship, which began when they were teenagers communicating through Instagram. They share laughs over past memories, including a tour incident involving norovirus, and acknowledge how rare it is for artists to find such genuine connection in the industry.

Looking ahead, Abrams mentions her involvement in a film project directed by Halina Reijn, currently filming in Europe. She expresses excitement about collaborating with artists who challenge her and expand her creative horizons.

As the conversation concludes, both artists reflect on the joy of their work and the importance of surrounding themselves with people who inspire growth. *Daughter from Hell* stands as a testament to Abrams' evolution, capturing the complexities of her twenties with honesty, vulnerability, and a newfound sense of peace.
