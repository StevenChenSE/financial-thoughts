## The Night's Split: Microsoft vs. Meta

The evening session revealed a new market philosophy regarding capital expenditure. Both Microsoft and Meta are spending aggressively, but their outcomes were opposite.

### Microsoft: The Power of a Ceiling

Microsoft delivered strong numbers: $90 billion in revenue, beating the $87.6 billion forecast, with 18% year-over-year growth. Net profit reached $35.7 billion.

However, there was a red flag that should have punished the stock. Microsoft’s capital expenditures, including finance leases, surged 69% year-over-year to $41 billion in a single quarter. Based on recent market behavior—where TSMC was sold off for spending $8 billion extra and Alphabet was punished for raising guidance—this 69% spike should have triggered a sell-off. Instead, Microsoft rose over 7%.

Why? Because Microsoft provided three critical assurances:

1.  **A Ceiling:** Their 2026 capital expenditure plan remains unchanged. They are not raising their spending targets further.
2.  **A Commitment:** They pledged to maintain positive cash flow for fiscal year 2027. They are burning cash, but not burning through their profitability.
3.  **An Explanation:** The CFO noted strong demand signals and explained that extending equipment lifespans would help mitigate depreciation pressure.

In short, Microsoft said: "I am still spending, but I will tell you when it stops, and I will tell you why it is worth it."

Earnings per share came in at $6.18, missing the $7.13 expectation by 13%. Net profit fell 14% year-over-year. The most glaring metric was the operating profit margin, which collapsed from 43% a year ago to just 31%.

The final blow was Meta’s guidance. They raised the lower bound of their 2026 capital expenditure from $125 billion to $130 billion and increased their full-year expense guidance, which included $2.4 billion in legal fees. Meta earned more revenue but less profit, while planning to spend even more next year.

The most telling statistic from Meta’s report was its cash flow. Operating cash flow was $31.86 billion, but free cash flow was a mere $784 million. Meta generated $31.86 billion in cash from business operations but spent 97.5% of it, leaving less than $800 million in the pocket. This is the definition of burning cash.

## The Market’s New Rules

This evening established a new market law. It is not that burning cash is a crime; both companies are spending heavily. The new rules are:

1.  **Is there a ceiling on your spending?**
2.  **Has your profitability been burned through?**
3.  **Where is the money coming from?**

Meta’s answer to the third question is concerning. Just before the earnings call, Meta announced a partnership with BlackRock to build a $14 billion data center, with 80% of the funding coming from BlackRock’s funds. This signals that Meta’s internal cash flow is tight, forcing it to bring in external capital to fund today’s compute power with future returns. This is a move typical of the late stages of an arms race.

## A Note on Circular Financing

There is a subtle detail worth noting. Of Microsoft’s $35.7 billion net profit, $3.2 billion came from valuation adjustments on its stake in Anthropic. While this is fully compliant and normal, it raises eyebrows when viewed alongside market concerns about "circular financing."

When companies in an ecosystem invest in each other and those valuations feed back into their profit reports, the integrity of the balance sheet becomes a metric to watch closely.

Each shift has been stricter. The standard set tonight is practical: the market does not fear spending; it fears spending without an end date. Expenditure with a ceiling is investment; expenditure without a ceiling is hemorrhaging.
