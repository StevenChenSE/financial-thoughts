## A Comprehensive Analysis of the Middle East Conflict and Its Worldwide Implications

---

In a wide-ranging interview that touched on everything from the collapse of global energy markets to the psychological profile of American leadership, Colonel Douglas McGregor offered one of the most sobering assessments of the current Middle East crisis and its potential consequences for the United States and the world.

"The global energy complex consists of effectively thousands of oil tankers, refineries, production facilities, not simply in the Persian Gulf, but all over the world," McGregor explained. "And we're discovering some things that we didn't fully appreciate before."

## The Energy Domino Effect

The colonel paints a stark picture of interconnected vulnerability. Remove approximately 15 million barrels of oil from daily global supply—some analysts put the figure closer to 20 million—and the effects ripple far beyond the immediate combatants.

"Australia has almost no petroleum strategic reserve. They are going to run out of diesel in an estimated 12 to 13 days," McGregor noted with evident disbelief. "They have no refining capability, although they do have some oil left, and that means that their entire economy is effectively going to grind to a halt."

This, McGregor suggested, is just the beginning. One of the most alarming consequences of the current conflict is the disappearance of 35% of the world's fertilizer supply—a development the colonel describes as "practically the green revolution in reverse."

"Very few people today know anything about the green revolution that happened 50, 60, 70, 80 years ago, where people finally learned how to grow great quantities of food, which has contributed enormously to the growth in population," McGregor observed. "Right now, even if everything stopped right now with that 35% shortfall in fertilizer, the world would be staring at famine over the next two years in various parts of the world."

The United States, while better positioned than most nations due to its domestic oil production, will not escape these pressures entirely. "Even in the United States, where we have a fertilizer shortage, we are going to face in the future some food shortages."

## The Petro-Dollar's Fragile Foundation

Beyond the immediate humanitarian concerns lies an economic structure that McGregor argues has underpinned American prosperity for decades—the petrodollar system.

"This is the agreement that stemmed from the 1970s, where the agreement was essentially that all trade in energy would be in dollars, and that those dollars, once that had been accomplished, those dollars would find their way back into our economy," McGregor explained. "They would go into our treasuries and help finance our debt and essentially finance debt-based consumption, which is what we're in. We're in debt-financed consumption. We're living far beyond our means. All of that is about to come to a crashing end."

The evidence, according to McGregor, is already visible. "Iran has said you can buy whatever you want here, but you can't do it in dollars. It has to be done in preferably yuan, but we'll take rubles, we'll take other forms of currency."

This shift away from dollar-denominated oil trade strikes at the heart of American financial flexibility. "Most people don't understand that," McGregor said, "not just because of the closure of the straits, but because of this fundamental restructuring of how energy is traded."

The implications extend to America's closest allies in the region. "The major oil infrastructure facilities for refining and producing and so forth are in Al-Jabal, which is an enormous port that's on the Gulf Coast. If that's destroyed, then I would say you're looking at perhaps as much as 10 years to recover inside the Gulf."

## Trump's Strategy: A Roy Cohn Legacy

When asked about presidential decision-making, McGregor offered a psychological framework drawn from Trump's formative influences. "We have to go back and look at his great mentor, the man who most profoundly shaped Trump's character, education, and upbringing. That wasn't his father. It was a man named Roy Cohn."

McGregor outlined what he describes as Cohn's three rules for life:

**First: Attack, attack, attack.** "Never stop attacking. In other words, be relentless. Regardless of whether or not you think you can win, regardless of whether or not your cause is just, those things don't matter. There's no morality involved. If you want something, you just attack, attack, attack till you get it."

**Second: Deny everything.** "If you've done something wrong, if you made a mistake, don't worry about it. Just deny, deny, deny."

**Third: Declare victory at all times.** "Whatever happens, you're a success. Whatever happens, you are victorious. Everyone else failed. Everyone else is irrelevant. Everyone else is stupid. You're the genius. You win. Declare victory. Move on."

McGregor drew a direct line from this philosophy to the current conflict: "I think if you understand those things, and if there is anything called a strategy, it falls under those three points, and he decided he was going to attack Iran in support of Israel."

The influence of what McGregor calls "Zionist billionaires" on Trump's decision-making is substantial. "These people wanted war with Iran for at least 30, some people would say 40 years. The notion was always that Israel will never be safe until all of its enemies are vanquished. And the most serious enemy is Iran."

"Remember, Iran's the state sponsor of terrorism in the world and all this," McGregor added dismissively. "It's all crap. It's not true. Doesn't make any difference. Truth doesn't matter. Remember, rule number one: we're going to attack."

## Military Reality Versus Strategic Fantasy

The gap between administration expectations and operational reality emerges as a central theme in McGregor's analysis.

"The intelligence community was consulted. The Israeli friends who are the ones putting you up to this anyway were consulted. And eventually it comes down to: well, it probably won't last more than a few days, maybe a week. They'll collapse. We'll get what we want," McGregor recounted. "Well, that's failed. Any low-grade [analyst] would have concluded such a thing were even possible. I think the Israelis just lied to him, and he chose to believe the lie."

The current military situation differs dramatically from administration talking points. "They really haven't had an air force since the Shah left the country—nothing to destroy. Navy? Well, they had seven old frigates, which were easy to target and sink. Not a problem."

The real Iranian capability lies elsewhere: "Then they have lots of these small boats. They have unmanned submersibles. They have unmanned craft on the surface. We haven't hit all of those. It's very difficult to do so. And they've got probably pens full of these damn things that they can send out at will."

"I think the others are very simply, you've got to dismantle most of your ballistic missile arsenal because that threatened us and we want you to be vulnerable to attack in perpetuity by us."

More significantly, McGregor emphasizes Iran's preparations over two decades: "Iran's been preparing for this for at least 20 years. They have built massive underground complexes. The amount of damage inflicted on them is only visible on the surface, and it doesn't seem to have had much impact on their ability to continue launching missiles at all kinds of ranges, up to theater ballistic missiles."

"There is no evidence that Iran is starving. There's no evidence that Iran is about to disintegrate. There's no evidence that Iran is about to suffer tremendous unrest. If anything, there's a lot of evidence that we've forced a great deal of cohesion on Iranian society."

## The Ground Forces Question

American military capabilities face serious questions in the current operational environment.

"The troops that have been shipped over there, flown in, whatever you want to call them, they are largely light infantry now," McGregor observed. "They're good soldiers, good Marines. They're organized, trained, and equipped for missions that went away 30, 40 years ago, but we cling tenatiously to the past when it comes to the American military."

He references historical precedent from the Gulf War: "When the 82nd [Airborne Division] did ultimately get in, they rode on school buses. Kind of a humiliating experience for a paratrooper, but they rode on school buses into the country."

The assumptions behind current planning remain questionable. "None of the ships that want to move through that strait are going to be underwritten by insurance. Lloyd's of London won't do it. Won't have anything to do with it under any circumstances, as long as it's a war zone."

Seizing islands in the Persian Gulf would not resolve this insurance crisis: "If anything, assuming they could get to those islands without being shot out of the sky, then they've got to stay there. That means they've got to protect themselves from the tactical ballistic missiles and the unmanned systems, specifically the Shahed drones and all these things that come pouring in with warheads on them with pinpoint accuracy."

## Regional Tensions Converge

The interview expands beyond Iran to the broader Middle East dynamic, identifying multiple pressure points.

"Israel has taken a terrible beating," McGregor stated flatly. "And this next round, I think, could be fatal to Israel in many ways."

Turkey emerges as a significant factor that Western planners may have underestimated. "From the very beginning, Ottoman Turkey and then subsequently the Turkish state we have today has been the 500-pound gorilla in the room. They are a natural strategic power. In fact, if you go back over the last 2,000 years, you can argue we have two great powers: one is Turkey and the other is Persia."

"The hatred today for the Jewish state in the region is greater than at any point in its entire history," McGregor observed.

Egypt presents another volatile element. "Egypt is a boiling pot. And our friend General Sisi is swimming uncomfortably in the boiling water. He could be gone any minute. He's afraid to do anything for fear that whatever he does may backfire. But Egypt and its population and its armed forces are up in arms and want desperately to punish the Israelis for what they've done to their Palestinian Arab brethren."

Turkey's military capabilities are substantial: "They have the largest army in NATO. They have exceptionally competent ground forces, well-armed, well-equipped. They have missiles, tactical ballistic missiles, theater ballistic missiles, a lot of unmanned systems. They have a substantial navy that can do a lot of damage from the sea."

## The Nuclear Shadow

Perhaps most alarmingly, McGregor addresses the potential use of nuclear weapons.

"The Russians have made some threatening statements warning the Israelis not to even think about it," he noted. "The Pakistanis have promised that if the Israelis use a nuclear weapon, they will see to it that Israel is nuked. And of course, the Chinese have said this would be the demise of Israel."

"One hopes that that will not happen," McGregor concluded, "but we don't seem to have any control over Israel. Israel seems to have a great deal of control over us."

## China's Strategic Position

McGregor identifies China as a critical wildcard in the unfolding situation.

"China has interests in the region. Now, one of the things we need to keep in mind is that the Iranians do have other ways to export oil. They have overland pipelines. They have access to the Caspian Sea. Between those two and the railroads and everything else, they can move oil to China."

"I can't tell you how much, but I can tell you that they've done that. They have that system in place, and they will do it."

China's calculus involves regional dominance considerations: "If you're sitting in Beijing and you're watching us, you may be saying, these people are maniacs. The next thing you know they're going to station missiles on Taiwan and dare us to attack."

"Remember, this is back to the evolutionary program that was already in place before we decided to challenge China's interests on China's turf. Maybe the Chinese decided to become involved now. I don't know. They could."

## Russia's Opening

Russia, meanwhile, finds itself in a strategically advantageous position.

"The Russians have just made—it's estimated—a trillion rubles as a windfall profit above and beyond what they were getting for their oil," McGregor noted. "Lavrov was just in Beijing. He says, 'We're going to drill, baby, drill, and we're going to supply everything we possibly can to you and the Japanese and the Koreans and everybody else, but we're going to make sure you, China, get what you need.'"

Military developments in Ukraine suggest Moscow may be preparing decisive action: "We are seeing that there are buildups of troops inside Ukraine on two principal axes. One is from Sumy that goes due west towards Kyiv, and the other is in the south that would lead up through Zaporizhzhia, cross the river, and move down into Odessa."

"I would not be surprised if we see Vladimir Putin say, 'Well, at this point, let's get this over with once and for all.'"

## The Path Forward: Escalation or Diplomacy?

McGregor offers a candid assessment of likely escalation.

"I think on Monday they will commence the mother of all bombing campaigns," he predicted. "It'll be another attempt at a war-winning air and missile campaign to bring Iran to its knees. And that won't work. After four or five days of intensive bombing and missiles and everything else, the Iranians aren't going to show up to surrender. The Iranians are going to continue to launch missiles."

The critical question becomes: "Do we get to the point where Donald Trump says, 'Well, that's all I can do. I can't do anymore. I'm declaring victory'? Can he leave at that point?"

McGregor believes a different approach is needed: "The intelligent thing is first of all, don't go through another bombing, which is a waste of time. The intelligent thing is to say, 'Look, we've done as much as we can do, and we have not been successful. There is only one way forward, and it is not military power. We need an international conference of great powers and small powers that will sit down and discuss how to end this thing, how to restart the energy complex and get it back on its feet.'"

He references successful precedents: "If you go to the Dardanelles, the strait between Turkey and Europe in the Bosporus, as we call it, the Turks are in charge of managing it. And that Montreux Convention was achieved in 1926. And the Turks have handled it very efficiently and very competently. There's never been a problem."

The logical conclusion, McGregor argues, is similar: "Who has most of the coastline in the Persian Gulf? Iran. Who has the greatest stake in what happens in the Gulf? Iran. Then Iran can be made responsible for the management of the strait."

However, he acknowledges the political obstacles: "You're asking Donald Trump to turn on Netanyahu, turn on the Zionist billionaires and say, 'Look, we can't do this anymore. This is harming the United States. We've gone too far.' Is he the sort of character who's going to do that? I don't think so."

## Leadership and Accountability

On the question of presidential fitness, McGregor was direct.

"I think that's true," he acknowledged regarding concerns about Trump's mental capacity. "There is a way to rectify that. That's the 25th Amendment. And that involves the cabinet meeting and deciding to remove him from office. As far as I can tell, everybody in the cabinet lives in fear of Donald Trump. Probably JD Vance most of all. So I don't see anything happening there."

The broader political class shares responsibility: "Remember, these same people control the Congress. How many people have stood up and spoken out forcefully for an end to the conflict, immediate end to the conflict on the basis of the Constitution or congressional preferences or anything else? How many have said we will not fund this any longer? None of that's happened."

"Congress thinks they're going to skate and walk away from this," McGregor predicted. "I don't think so. I think what's coming economically and financially for us is disastrous. And people are going to say, 'How could you have done this to us?'"

## Conclusion: A Catastrophe in Progress

McGregor concluded with a comprehensive summary of the situation's trajectory.

"I think at the end of the day, this is going to be a catastrophe. He'll be punished for it. He'll be the scapegoat, so to speak. But he's a principal actor in his own tragedy."

The path to resolution requires acknowledging reality, McGregor argued, but the current leadership appears incapable of such acknowledgment.

"I don't pay too much attention to all of these two in the morning tweets and things that come out or exposts," he said. "I think a lot of it is just imaginary nonsense, and some of it is outrageous. But the fact that he would pretend to be a Christlike figure suggests a certain instability of mind that we cannot ignore."

"The man should go, and I think he will ultimately be relieved. And the sooner they do that, the better."

But the structural problems run deeper than any individual leader. "The Epstein class is still in power," McGregor observed. "It's not going to last. They're all going to go down for this. We're headed into terrible territory, not just globally, but in our own country."

"The only thing that I can see on what I would call the hard left is hatred for white people, hatred for this country, for the white civilization they despise. I mean, that's pretty obvious. They'll do whatever they can to undermine the rule of law and promote unrest in the country."

"The rest—what difference is there? They're all basically going to do the same thing. It's all about self-enrichment. They are all part of what I call the larger Epstein class. What part of the Epstein class do you want to vote for in November?"

For McGregor, the fundamental problem is America's failure to adapt to 21st-century realities: "This is the 21st century way of war. We're using World War II as the template. It's not going to work."
