## Jim Bianco on Interest Rates, Inflation, and Market Implications for Late 2026

The Federal Reserve stands at a critical crossroads. With inflation stubbornly remaining above target for 63 consecutive months and core PCE sitting at 3.4%, the Fed appears ready to resume rate hikes—potentially as early as October. According to Jim Bianco, president of Bianco Research, this isn't just an American phenomenon but part of a synchronized global hiking cycle that will reshape fixed income markets and challenge conventional wisdom about how rising rates impact equities.

### A Global Rate Hiking Cycle Is Already Underway

While markets focus on the Federal Reserve's next move, many central banks have already begun their tightening campaigns. The Bank of Japan started hiking rates in 2024 and has raised rates five times. The Bank of Australia began its cycle in January, implementing three rate increases. The European Central Bank hiked just last week, and the Bank of New Zealand appears ready to join. The Bank of England remains in the mix, though it has yet to pull the trigger.

"We're in a rate hiking cycle," Bianco states definitively. "The Fed is going to be raising rates at least one time, probably October at this point."

### Where Will Interest Rates Go?

Bianco expects the 10-year Treasury yield—currently hovering around 4.42%—to trend toward 5% by year end. This represents another 50 basis points or so in yields, but he cautions against panic.

"The perfect interest rate is zero. That's how every real estate guy thinks," Bianco explains. "But interest rates are a lot more nuanced than that. If they're too low, you create speculative bubbles. If they're too high, you choke an economy's growth."

The key insight is that the "fair value" range for interest rates is shifting upward alongside inflation expectations. As long as yields remain in that sweet spot, equities should remain supported. The real concern emerges only if rates move meaningfully beyond 5%.

### The Bond Vigilante Paradox

One of Bianco's most counterintuitive observations centers on bond market dynamics. He offers a memorable framework: "As a bond investor, I can stop panicking when the Federal Reserve starts panicking."

Consider 2022: despite inflation reaching 9%, the 10-year yield never exceeded 4.23% because the Fed was raising rates aggressively with 75 basis point moves at every meeting. In contrast, when inflation moderated to 4% and the Fed appeared less aggressive, the 30-year yield spiked to 5.20% in May.

"If Powell had acted like a sock puppet yesterday and signaled rate cuts, the bond market would have collapsed on itself and yields would have soared," Bianco suggests. "You don't want to cut rates in a 4% inflation environment. If you're going to do that, I don't want anything to do with your bond market."

This dynamic points to yield curve flattening—short-term rates rising faster than long-term rates—as the likely scenario.

### Why Higher Rates Don't Necessarily Kill Stocks

The conventional narrative holds that rising interest rates are always bearish for equities, particularly growth stocks. Bianco pushes back on this oversimplification.

"Trump has done a really good job of making everybody think like a real estate guy—down on interest rates is always good, up is always bad," Bianco notes. "But I've been saying if rates drift toward five, equities will be fine in that environment."

The critical distinction lies in distinguishing between rates that are genuinely restrictive versus rates that simply reflect a more normalized inflation environment.

### The Inflation Reality: A Regime Change

Perhaps the most significant insight from Bianco's analysis concerns the nature of inflation itself. We've now experienced 63 consecutive months above the Fed's 2% target. For context, from 2010 to 2020, inflation exceeded 2% for only about four months total.

"For those who talk about AI being deflationary, about a long-term deflationary pull—that's over," Bianco declares. "This is a different inflation cycle. You cannot expect us to go back to that other cycle we had from 2010 to 2020."

The current inflation picture is more complex than energy prices alone. Core PCE at 3.4% represents what Bianco calls "the thing moving the needle." Add headline inflation and the economy faces 4% or higher readings—numbers that simply cannot be ignored.

### The Fed's Shift in Focus

The FOMC statement released yesterday revealed a significant pivot in the Fed's priorities. The statement emphasized high inflation and included nothing about the labor market. When pressed in the press conference, Powell referred to the employment report as "an echo of the past"—a statement Bianco interprets as a clear signal about where Fed attention now lies.

"He basically told you that you could schedule a vacation on the first Friday of the month because he's not paying attention to the payroll report," Bianco observes. "The Fed is not very good at predicting these things, and they need to stop with this forward guidance and models based on data that's old."

This represents a fundamental shift in the Fed's reaction function—from a dual mandate that balanced employment and inflation to something approaching a single-mandate focus on price stability.

### Is the Bond Bull Market Over?

When asked whether investors should rule out bonds entirely, Bianco offers a nuanced response.

"The secular bull market for bonds ended in August 2020 when the 10-year yield hit 50 basis points," he confirms. "But no, you don't want to rule out bonds."

Bianco sees investors as having become "entitled" over recent years, expecting randomly picked investments to deliver 20% annual returns. This expectation—whether applied to stocks, gold, crypto, or AI—is simply unrealistic.

"Bonds still have a role in portfolios," Bianco suggests, though the era of capital appreciation in fixed income appears to have concluded.

### Oil, Iran, and the Strait of Hormuz

The geopolitical landscape has shifted dramatically with news that the Iran conflict—Bianco's self-described "Strait of Hormuz War"—appears over. Oil prices plummeted from $95 on June 8 to $75 by June 18, a $20 decline in just 10 days, following a peace deal and memorandum of understanding signed at Versailles.

The location choice raised eyebrows. "If you're not familiar with your history, that's where people go to surrender," Bianco notes with characteristic bluntness. "Bad optics on the White House's part to pick Versailles."

### What This Means for Investors

The confluence of factors—global central bank tightening, persistent core inflation, a Fed willing to prioritize price stability over employment, and a geopolitical landscape that may ease energy pressures—creates a complex backdrop for markets.

Bianco's core thesis suggests investors should prepare for a 10-year yield drifting toward 5%, a flattening yield curve, and an equity market that may prove more resilient to rising rates than conventional wisdom suggests. The key is recognizing that we're in a new inflation regime, not the disinflationary environment that shaped the previous decade.

The Fed's credibility now rests on demonstrating that 63 months above target was an aberration, not the new normal. Whether Powell's new approach succeeds remains to be seen—but the era of easy money and below-normal interest rates appears firmly behind us.
