**March 25, 2026** – In the complex world of global finance and energy politics, the most significant threats to the established order often emerge from the shadows. According to analyst Eric Young, a relatively obscure Chinese financial institution—the Bank of Kunlun—is playing a pivotal role in chipping away at the decades-long dominance of the U.S. petro-dollar.

The discussion, hosted by Mario on *Monaco 64*, delved into Young’s Substack research, which connects recent geopolitical events to a quiet but profound shift in how oil is traded.

### The Bank of Kunlun: China’s Islamic Financial Bridge

At the heart of this shift is the Bank of Kunlun, a small Chinese bank with a unique profile. As Young explained:

> “The Bank of Kunlun is basically China’s Islamic bank… It has two headquarters. One is in Beijing and one is in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region in China… the westernmost province in China that is closest to Iran.”

This geographical and cultural positioning is strategic. By operating through an Islamic bank in a Muslim-majority region, China facilitates trade with Iran while demonstrating cultural respect. Crucially, Young notes the bank is controlled by the China National Petroleum Corporation (CNPC), the parent company of the globally known PetroChina.

“So PetroChina is essentially controlling a bank that is facilitating the purchase of crude oil from Iran,” Young said.

### The Mechanics of Bypassing the Dollar

For years, analysts suspected China was buying Iranian oil using Chinese yuan (RMB), bypassing U.S. dollar systems and sanctions. These transactions, long denied by some Western commentators, are now coming into clearer focus.

According to Young’s research, China has historically purchased about 80% of Iran’s seaborne oil, paying in RMB through the Bank of Kunlun. The mechanism doesn’t end there.

> “What Iran does is they don’t even apparently take the majority of the RMB out of the system. They keep it within the Kunlun bank and then they use that current account… to buy whatever they need—Chinese electronics, missile components, drone components, etc.”

This creates a closed-loop financial system. Iran sells oil for RMB, then immediately uses that digital currency to purchase goods from China, all within the same sanctioned-proof bank.

### The Gold Connection and Surplus Recycling

A critical question arises: what happens when Iran runs an RMB surplus from high oil sales? Young points to the Shanghai Gold Exchange.

> “Some of the RMB surplus, they are probably buying physical gold from the Shanghai Gold Exchange, but not through the Shanghai Gold Exchange International because that would ring alarm bells… They use local, domestic accounts to purchase those gold bars and potentially they can just ship that gold bar by land route directly back to Iran from Xinjiang, China.”

This move from petro-yuan to petro-gold further insulates Iran from the dollar-based financial system and Western oversight.

### The Gulf War Catalyst and Expanding Yuan Usage

The ongoing conflict in the Middle East has accelerated these trends. Iran now controls the Strait of Hormuz and has declared that oil passing through must be paid for in RMB. Furthermore, Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries like Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE are reportedly paying Iran a “toll fee” of $2 million per tanker—in RMB—to ship their oil through the strait.

Perhaps more significantly, Young shared intelligence from his Chinese sources:

> “Starting in January 2026… the oil that Saudi Arabia is selling to China, 50% of it is being settled in RMB.”

This marks a substantial increase and a direct blow to dollar hegemony. The financial repercussions are severe. GCC countries historically recycled dollar oil revenues into U.S. Treasury bonds. With oil flows from the region reportedly down to 30% of pre-war levels and an increasing share settled in RMB, their demand for Treasuries has likely plummeted.

“The Treasury has about $10 trillion to roll over in the next 12 months,” Mario noted, highlighting the potential fiscal crisis for the U.S. if traditional buyers retreat.

### Gold Market Volatility: A Tell-Tale Sign

Young presented a gold price chart from December 2025 to March 2026, suggesting market insiders foresaw these shifts. A major gold dump in late January 2026 coincided with Saudi Arabia’s move to 50% RMB oil settlement.

> “Somebody knew like beforehand that the war was going to happen and they dumped gold back in January… The ramp up was that everybody knew that the GCC countries are buying physical gold because they have that RMB surplus.”

The volatility, Young argues, is less about central banks dumping gold and more about wealthy individuals in the Gulf moving assets. He speculates that affluent residents, possibly relocating to financial hubs like Hong Kong, sold gold in Dubai and repurchased it in Asia, creating price swings.

### The Domino Effect: Who’s Next?

Looking ahead, the analysts see the petro-dollar’s erosion spreading. South Korea, under a leadership described as “very pro-China,” is a prime candidate to start settling oil trades in RMB. Japan may already be doing so quietly to secure oil transit through the Strait of Hormuz.

“Maybe Japan is going to be the last holdout,” Young said, “But maybe Japan’s already doing it.”

### Geopolitical Stalemate and Financial Reality

The conversation turned to the grim military reality in the Middle East. Citing analysis from former Navy SEAL Matt Bracken, Young described a potential U.S. landing on Iran’s Qeshm Island as a “quagmire” and a “massacre,” given Iran’s proximity and defensive capabilities.

A protracted war of attrition, they agreed, would force the U.S. to print more money, further debasing the dollar and boosting the appeal of gold and silver.

### Navigating the Uncertainty

In closing, both hosts emphasized a neutral, analytical approach to these intertwined geopolitical and financial storms.

> “We’re not trying to take a side here by any means,” Mario stated. “We’re just trying to help people navigate through all this by telling them the truth.”

For investors, Young’s advice was straightforward: dollar-cost average into gold and silver.

> “Nobody knows where the actual bottom is… The best way to do this is to dollar cost average over a period of time.”

Mario concurred, sharing that he bought gold during a recent $400 drop: “Looking a year or two [ahead], you’re going to probably regret that you didn’t buy on this dip.”

The message is clear: while kings and queens maneuver on the geopolitical chessboard, the silent work of institutions like the Bank of Kunlun is steadily changing the rules of the game, making tangible assets a prudent harbor in the storm ahead.
