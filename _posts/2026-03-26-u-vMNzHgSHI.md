In a recent conversation on the All-In podcast, billionaire investor and Bridgewater Associates founder Ray Dalio returned to discuss the economic and geopolitical landscape. Reflecting on a prior discussion just after President Trump’s inauguration—where Dalio emphasized the need to cut the deficit-to-GDP ratio to about 3% for a smoother transition—he noted that the Congressional Budget Office now estimates the 2026 deficit will be roughly 6%.

“Are we on a good path?” the host asked. Dalio responded by outlining the five intertwined forces that, based on his study of 500 years of history, determine our trajectory:

1. **The debt and money cycle**
2. **Internal conflict** stemming from wealth and values gaps
3. **Great power conflict** internationally
4. **Technology**
5. **Acts of nature**, such as pandemics and climate events

“All orders change,” Dalio stated, referring to monetary, domestic political, and international geopolitical systems. “We have 250 years here, but… they always change.”

## The Debt Crisis: A “Very Unhealthy Condition”

Dalio broke down the U.S. government’s financial position in stark terms: it spends about $7 trillion, takes in $5 trillion, and thus runs a 40% deficit. Debt stands at six times annual revenue.

“The problem with debt cycles,” he explained, “is almost like the circulatory system of the body.” When credit is used productively, it’s healthy. But when debt service grows faster than income, “it’s like plaque in the system… and it squeezes out spending.”

Currently, half of the $2 trillion deficit goes to interest payments, while the government must also roll over $9 trillion in maturing debt. “If you were to look at a company like that or an individual like that, you have that problem,” Dalio said. Achieving a 3% deficit-to-GDP ratio would help stabilize the situation, but we are far from that.

The supply-and-demand challenge is acute: the U.S. must find buyers for massive debt issuance. About one-third of buyers are foreign, but Dalio warned that geopolitical risks—such as potential conflicts with China or sanctions—could make them hesitant. “The things that I’m describing have happened repeatedly through history,” he noted, pointing to the 1929–1945 period as an example.

## Can Government Be Made Efficient? “A Hell of a Trick”

The discussion turned to the Department of Government Efficiency (DoGE), an initiative led by Elon Musk to cut waste and fraud. Its limited success raised a question: Is meaningful change structurally impossible at this stage?

Dalio tied the issue to broader political dynamics. “In a society in which no matter what you do, you’re criticized and torn down… does democracy and our system lend itself toward the sort of executive leadership that both makes it efficient and makes it acceptable for all people?” He cited controversial cuts to programs like school lunches as an example of the tension. “I think that’s a hell of a trick to pull off.”

When asked about recent reports of large-scale fraud in public programs, Dalio replied, “Is that a surprise to you?” He compared it to the experience at the DMV: the system is simply too big, complex, and poorly managed.

## Gold: “The Most Established Money”

Since their last conversation, gold has surged from about $2,900 to $5,200 per ounce. Dalio attributed this to the “big cycle” dynamics he outlined.

“Gold is not a precious metal that’s speculated on… It is the most established money,” he said. Central banks have been accumulating gold due to economic, political, and geopolitical concerns. “Individuals and others are looking for an alternative money.”

He explained the core issue: “Money is debt. If you’re holding money, you’re holding a promise from somebody to deliver you money.” With central banks able to print money to manage debt, the safety of that promise comes into question. “What money do you think is safe?” Dalio asked. “I want an asset… that’s got some physical known limitation to it.”

Gold, he argued, is uniquely suited as a store of value and medium of exchange that can be transferred across borders. “They can’t print a lot of it.”

On portfolio allocation, Dalio advised, “If you didn’t know what gold was likely to do… one should have between five and 15% of their portfolio in gold because… it’s a diversifier when the hits the fan.”

## Bitcoin vs. Silver: Why Haven’t They Performed Like Gold?

Bitcoin has fallen about 25% over the same period gold rose 80%. Dalio highlighted key differences: Bitcoin lacks privacy, is not held by central banks, and is correlated with tech stocks. “It’s a relatively small market that’s a relatively controllable market.”

As for silver, which has also risen, Dalio called it “a residual commodity” that “has taken on a speculative life of its own.”

## Interest Rates, the “K-Shaped” Economy, and the Fed’s Dilemma

With so much debt, interest rate policy becomes a delicate balancing act. “You can’t make interest rates severely artificially low because one man’s debts are another man’s assets,” Dalio said. Too low, and you fuel bubbles; too high, and debtors get squeezed.

This is complicated by what he called the “K-shaped economy”: a booming top tier—where “the question is who will be the first to be a trillionaire”—contrasts with the bottom 60%, where many struggle with basic literacy and face displacement from AI. “This is a challenging situation,” he admitted.

With global central banks shifting from U.S. Treasuries to gold, Dalio believes the Fed will likely have to expand its balance sheet again. “It’s likely down the road.”

On the new Fed chair, Kevin Wars, Dalio offered: “He’s a practical man. He understands both sides of the pros and cons. I think it’s a tough job.”

## Tariffs and the “Hollowing Out” of America

Discussing recent tariffs, Dalio challenged conventional economic thinking. “All economists make the mistake of not including taxes in inflation.” If taxes or tariffs rise, he argued, that is effectively inflation—it takes money out of people’s pockets.

Historically, tariffs were a major revenue source. More importantly, he tied them to the larger cycle: the U.S. has run unsustainable trade deficits, which reflect dependence on foreign capital. “We’ve had a hollowing out of manufacturing [and] the middle class,” Dalio said. “What is the plan to rectify that?”

---

**In summary**, Ray Dalio sees the U.S. navigating a perilous confluence of forces: a debt spiral, deep internal division, rising geopolitical tensions, technological disruption, and natural threats. His historical perspective suggests that these cycles have played out before—and that their resolution will reshape the global order. In the meantime, he urges investors to ask themselves a fundamental question: “What is safe?”
