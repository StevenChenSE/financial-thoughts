The global energy landscape has shifted dramatically since the world last checked in with Rick Rule in early January. What was once a straightforward case for oil and gas investments—rooted in systematic underinvestment in sustaining capital—has been complicated by the outbreak of war in the Middle East. For Rule, longtime financial analyst, investor, and founder of Rule Investment Media, this creates both an opportunity and a challenge for resource investors navigating an increasingly volatile commodity complex.

"Energy is a very complex topic," Rule admits during a recent appearance on Palisades Gold Radio. "I remember the last time you and I talked, and I was able to say, because nobody liked oil and gas stocks, that oil and gas was a no-brainer. I, of course, wasn't prescient enough to have anticipated war."

What Rule did anticipate, however, was troubling. At their previous meeting, he noted that the world was underinvesting by approximately one billion dollars per day in sustaining capital investments—and suggested that oil could easily trade at $90 per barrel by 2029. The war, it seems, has simply frontloaded that move.

## The Straits of Hormuz: Reality Versus Anticipation

The headlines tell one story: 20% of the world's crude flows through the Straits of Hormuz. But the more troubling figure, according to Rule, is that well over 50% of the world's export crude passes through those same straits. The implications for global energy markets—and particularly for nations on the eastern rim of the Pacific basin—are severe.

"The WTI market, which is commonly quoted at $95 a barrel, is a futures price," Rule explains. "Cargos that are loaded today on tankers that are south of the Straits of Hormuz are commanding as much as a $40 a barrel premium. Which is to say the futures market at $95 doesn't correspond well with a tanker loading in Pakistan or Sri Lanka at 140 or 145 a barrel."

This discrepancy between futures and physical markets reveals a critical insight: the world hasn't yet experienced an actual shortage. The price rise to date reflects anticipation of disruption, supported by floating inventory and strategic reserves. But if the conflict continues for two to three more weeks, that changes.

"I have no way of telling you how this conflict is going to end," Rule says. "That's above my pay grade. Neither the Iranians nor the Israelis nor President Trump have me on their speed dial."

But he can tell investors this: the moment the world must actually ration oil by price, things get "uglier very quickly."

## The Two-to-Three Week Tipping Point

In the near term, there are approximately 200 tankers north of the Straits of Hormuz that cannot get out—a fair bit of supply effectively trapped. If the war were to end tomorrow, Rule suspects the anticipation of disruption would fall rapidly, and the reality of actual shortage would prove different than the market expects. The Biden administration has already taken the unusual step of anticipatorily desanctioning Russian crude in response to the crisis.

"I would suspect that if the war ended, you would see a fairly rapid decline in oil prices," Rule notes.

But the deeper structural problem remains unchanged. The destruction of productive capacity in the Gulf—both Qatari liquefied natural gas infrastructure and Iranian infrastructure on Kharg Island—will take as much as five years to repair. The systematic underinvestment in oil supply that Rule identified nine months ago hasn't been cured by conflict; it's been accelerated.

"The circumstance that caused me to be an oil stock buyer—which had nothing to do with the war, which had everything to do with systematic underinvestment in sustaining capital—hasn't changed," Rule says. "In fact, it has worsened. So while in the very near term—2026—you might see some relaxation in price, the circumstance we talked about for late 2028 and 2029 gets exacerbated."

Most commodity investors, Rule observes, don't think in the two-to-five year time frame, "which is why most commodity investors don't make money."

## A Warning From History: The 1970s Oil Embargo

The current situation invites historical comparison. Rule points to the Arab oil embargo of the early 1970s—a political act in response to the Israeli conflicts with neighboring states—that so dramatically disrupted oil trade. Decades of underinvestment combined with constriction of Persian Gulf supplies, along with inflation, drove crude from $3 per barrel to $30 over the course of that decade.

"I'm not suggesting that a move on that order of magnitude is present today," Rule cautions. "But I am suggesting to you that, irrespective of the war, the circumstance we're facing in late 2028 and 2029 is very real—and has merely gotten worse."

Venezuela provides a cautionary tale. PDVSA, the Venezuelan national oil company with the largest reserves in the world, saw production decline by 85% as a consequence of deferred sustaining capital investments. Two decades of underinvestment and two decades of input inflation have coalesced—leaving Venezuela with less free cash flow to invest precisely when it needs billions more to close the gap, unable to do so on an inflation-adjusted basis.

"This will come to bite us," Rule says flatly. "The input costs are rising between 5% and 8% per year compounded. A billion dollars deferred today will likely cost you $1.2 to $1.3 billion to make up three years from now—after you've already experienced the production declines when it's more difficult to make that investment."

## Oil Equities: Pricing, Opportunity, and the Question of Profits

When asked how oil equities are currently pricing the situation into their valuations, Rule's honest answer is characteristically direct: "I don't know. I haven't done that work."

What he does know is that both the oil price and oil equities reflect significant anticipatory pricing. With the exception of Saudi Aramco—now publicly traded—all oil equities could reasonably be expected to fall in price if the conflict finds reasonable resolution.

"This would be, in my own opinion, the calm before the storm," Rule says. "I'm hoping—I have no particular interest in selling my Exxon. It's a machine. But the basket of Canadians I bought partly as a consequence of speculation—if I were a rational actor, I would probably be selling half."

Yet Rule hasn't sold a share. He remains hopeful that the Middle East crisis might give Canadian political leadership the courage to allow the building out of Canadian infrastructure and kickstart that economy with latent assets that aren't currently active.

"I'm probably giving too much credit for Canadian politicians. They're probably incapable of rising to the moment," he admits. "But I'd like to believe that they're capable of it. I'm very fond of that country and its people, and very fond of the resource inventory."

The challenge of selling is compounded by the fact that, in inflation-adjusted terms, oil is nowhere close to its all-time high. And with the world underinvesting a billion dollars per day in sustaining capital, the consequences are real. If companies aren't recompleting wells, drilling makeup wells, or replacing and repairing infrastructure, their ability to produce declines.

## Regional Producers: Winners and Laggers

The United States presents a stark exception to the global underinvestment story. "They are right back to drill baby drill," Rule observes. "They are going absolutely crazy in the Permian Basin—which is why you're seeing the Henry Hub natural gas price fall so quickly. The byproduct gas in the Permian Basin, co-produced with oil, is overwhelming the infrastructure."

But that drilling isn't sustaining capital—it's growth capital. The situation differs markedly in Canada, where companies are taking surplus cash flow and either paying down debt or returning it to shareholders. "You are not seeing a great increase in sustaining capital expenditure in Canada, which I suspect is a consequence of the political leadership in Canada, who despite their kind words to the contrary have proven to be continually anti-oil and gas."

For investors seeking exposure, Scandinavian producers offer interesting geology but face political headwinds. "The geological endowment in Scandinavia is spectacular, and there is still at least the vestige of the rule of law," Rule says. "The challenge is that Scandinavian governments, traditionally, although they've enjoyed spending the social rents from energy, have been anti-energy."

Australia's Woodside, meanwhile, represents a compelling alternative for East Asia. The development of Australia's northwest shelf has opened a real alternative source of LNG supply for the region—prolific enough that a ship of LNG from Australia recently landed in Atlantic Canada, a fact that strikes Rule as one of the "political absurdities of the world."

"Why the Canadians couldn't figure out the business case for supplying natural gas to Eastern Canada and instead chose to import it from Australia," he says with audible frustration. "I collect absurdities."

## Helium: A Niche Opportunity in a Disrupted Market

Beyond oil and gas, the conflict has disrupted other commodity markets. Helium, specifically, presents an interesting speculative opportunity—though one that requires sophisticated analysis.

"I recently made a helium investment," Rule reveals. "With Qatar, the world was truly awash in helium. But that party came to a screeching halt."

Helium forms naturally through the breakdown of uranium and collects in sedimentary horizons adjacent to large historical sources of uranium. This makes Saskatchewan in Canada and Kazakhstan theoretically attractive locations to explore. "Finding a pure play is difficult," Rule admits. "Traditional sources in the US—the Hugoton and Hugoton Embayment—have been producing so long and so much gas that they're underpressured and becoming increasingly difficult to work in."

Helium fits between mining and oil and gas in terms of capital intensity. "You have to drill out enough of it that you can afford to put in extraction infrastructure—strip the helium from other gases present," Rule explains. "I don't suspect we will have a challenge finding helium if we start looking. But there's an awful lot of work that needs to be done, and I don't know if they can attract the capital they need given the Qataris' sustainable competitive advantage if Qatari capacity is restored."

For less sophisticated investors, LNG may represent a better opportunity—broader market, more participants, less required expertise. "Look for installed capacity that doesn't need to be built," Rule advises. "Look for high returns on capital employed, high deliverability, high operating margins."

## Fertilizers: The Quiet Beneficiary

The war's impact on fertilizers has been asymmetric. Urea, a nitrogenous fertilizer, has skyrocketed—nearly doubling in price—while potash and phosphates have lagged. Rule sees opportunity in that lag.

"The Gulf gas producers have a durable competitive advantage in nitrogenous fertilizers, given the abundance of feedstock and the incredible amount of fixed capital they have in place," Rule explains. "To the extent the Gulf is offline, the world's going to have disruption in the liquid nitrogen business—the urea business—for a long time. It will be a problem that can be addressed with other sources of natural gas, but it will be difficult to attract capital because all the people writing the checks know that the second the Gulf comes back into production, they are sitting on only marginally economic reserves."

Potash tells a different story. The largest and potentially lowest cost producer of potash is Russia, by far. "The Russians have not come anywhere close to making the sustaining capital and new project investments necessary to exploit their potash because they've had other uses for money—in a word, Ukraine," Rule notes. "There are constraints on the export of Russian nutrients. Once again, if sanity returns to the world and peace breaks out between Russia and Ukraine, there would be challenges for every potash producer in the world—with the exception of the Saskatchewan producers, who are not competitive with the Russians but beat the hell out of everybody else."

Saskatchewan potash producers occupy second place globally. "There's lots of scope to expand production. While they will not be as cheap as the Russians, they are much, much cheaper than anybody else—and it's a jurisdiction that, for the most part, has the advantage of the rule of law."

Phosphates are becoming interesting as well, with Morocco emerging as the dominant global producer. "If there was ever political disruption in Morocco—and I don't see that—I see Morocco as a country that's improving—you would have challenges around phosphate."

The long-term thesis for fertilizers, however, is straightforward. "There are eight billion people on Earth," Rule says. "As poor people become less poor, one of the first things that happens is they increase their per capita consumption of calories. One of the great things we've done as a species in the last 40 years is we've moved two billion people away from being desperately poor to being very poor. We have a billion people to go. We'll accomplish that in the next 20 years. More people will eat more—and without the green revolution, without modern DAP and those forms of fertilizer, we can't feed eight billion people. It's as simple as that."

Rule has been invested in the nutrient business for 25 years. "It's exposed me to an awful lot of cyclical rises and falls and an awful lot of volatility too," he says. "But if you look back over 20 years, 25 years, my return on capital employed on a compounded basis has been superb. A lot of the return I've enjoyed has been current return in the form of dividends—and my real return has been higher because I've reinvested those dividends without ascribing that benefit to the share price."

For investors seeking a starting point: "I'd probably start with Nutrien," Rule suggests. "If you have the ability—and I don't know if you will—my European listeners who live in Switzerland should probably consider the Russian producers if they're speculators. They are the low-cost producers."

## Gold: Simply a Matter of Sanity

Moving to precious metals, Rule addresses gold's role in portfolios—a topic on which he has strong opinions forged over decades.

"I don't think you measure safe haven in weeks," he begins. "I began systematically saving in gold in the 1970s—except I wasn't. I was more of an investor and speculator than saver. From the time I developed a lot of surplus capital—which was the beginning of this millennium—I began to systematically save in gold and maintain liquidity in US dollars."

Over 25 years, gold has generated almost a 9% internal rate of return annually in Rule's portfolio. "It has kept pace with pricing levels. When I informally look at my income expressed in gold, my assets expressed in gold, and my costs expressed in gold, I'm surprised at how cheap things are. Real estate measured in gold is cheap. Food measured in gold is pathetically cheap. Health insurance and healthcare measured in gold are cheap. When, by contrast, I measure them in US dollars, all of those things are frightfully expensive."

This tells you gold's true role. The recent decline in the gold price, Rule argues, paradoxically demonstrates something positive about the metal. "It was a source of liquidity in times of need. The Turks, in particular, needed to sell their gold to defend their currency—to keep politically expedient domestic pricing low—and they were able to do that. Had the Turks gone to the global bond market and tried to borrow any currency in the world, they would have been singularly unsuccessful. Given the fact that they had 60 tons of gold to sell, they sold it. They weathered the storm."

The lesson: gold has spectacular liquidity. "247 in any market. I can show you my logo—I'm the founding shareholder of a bank, and one of our premises is that we love to lend against gold as a piece of collateral. We love to lend against that collateral because we can perfect and sell that collateral if somebody didn't pay us back, anytime, in any quantity."

Gold maintains its purchasing power in a real sense, is uniform, and is insanely liquid. Yes, it's volatile in price in the near term—but so is every other asset. "If you want to see real volatility, look at your home," Rule suggests. "Have your home appraised by three different appraisers, and you will find at the same minute of the same day that the price varies by as much as 10% per second depending on the valuer."

His conclusion: "Including gold in one's savings portfolio is simply a matter of sanity. I don't think there's an excuse for not holding it. Whether or not gold is an investment class or a speculative class depends more on the investor. But as a savings—or insurance—class, I think not holding gold is irresponsible."

As for whether Middle Eastern nations under financial pressure might sell gold holdings, creating a headwind for prices: "I own a lot of gold," Rule admits. "A financial planner would look at me as the largest shareholder in SPDR, effectively, a financial services brand name for gold. And that planner would say, 'Rick, you are crazy owning gold. You don't need any gold.' But I'm an old man. Gold allows me to sleep nights and stay calm. My gold isn't for sale at this price. So I would like to see it cheaper."

"Had I generated more liquidity, I likely would continue to overweight gold relative to short-term US cash, which is my other savings vehicle."

## Silver: Speculation, Hatred, and Patience

On silver, Rule's recent move tells the story. "I was a buyer in January when I sold 80% of my physical silver," he says. "I had that silver in a speculative account—the role it played for me was speculation. And when silver went from hated to loved, when there was that parabolic up move, I sold 80% of my silver and I put 25% of the sales proceeds into savings, which is to say I put it in gold."

Silver miners, Rule notes, were priced on Wall Street and Bay Street using an approximate $45 per ounce price of silver, which at the time was $75. "That seemed too cheap. So half the money I raised selling my silver went into silver miners. The arithmetic to me was: if the silver price kept going up, the miners would go up with it. If the silver price went sideways, by definition I'd make no money on the silver, but the miners were priced at $45—so I could still make substantial profits. And paradoxically, if the silver price fell, there was some shelter because the equity prices were discounting $45 silver in a $75 world."

That circumstance still exists today. But personally, Rule isn't a silver buyer at current prices. "I believe the next 10 years will unfortunately be good for the gold price. I say unfortunately because circumstances that are good for the gold price are usually bad for credit markets—and credit markets are where people live. I suspect over the course of 10 years that silver will outpace gold. But I've always regarded silver, for my own portfolio and this is always personal, as a speculative asset, and there are other asset classes within speculation that I prefer to silver."

Until silver returns to being hated—truly hated—the way it was below $20 an ounce after the aborted silver squeeze four and a half years ago—Rule won't be a buyer.

"I love circumstances like that. I love circumstances where a commodity for which there's ongoing demand experiences rank hatred. There's nothing in the world I like as a speculator more than hate."

He won't rule out a return to such conditions, particularly if higher interest rates derails both gold and silver temporarily. "Certainly in 1975, there was widespread hate of gold and silver, particularly among people who were new adherents to it in 1974 and got highsighted. If that occurred, I might return silver to my portfolio in a speculative place."

## Junior Miners: A Stockpicker's Paradise (Or Trap)

When asked about opportunities in precious metals miners, Rule is direct: "A lot. I own a lot."

On the question of whether juniors have moved less than expected given the underlying commodity prices: "If you merge every junior gold explorer in the world into one company, Junior Explore Co., that company would lose about $2 billion a year," Rule observes. "So what should you pay for that company? Ten times losses, twelve times losses in a bull market, maybe fifteen times losses."

The junior mining industry is always overcapitalized. "It's probably 1,500 companies worldwide, and there's probably 200 that are viable. The best 10% of juniors generate so much value that they add legitimacy and even luster to a sector that loses between $2 and $5 billion a year."

This doesn't mean speculation in juniors can't work. "You may know that I've graded now, over 35 years, almost 100,000 portfolios, and I've learned an awful lot about the mistakes that individual speculators make: much too narrative-oriented, much too lazy, own way more stocks than they can follow, much too impatient, not tenacious enough."

Making money in junior explorers requires doing a lot of work, holding for five or six year time frames—during which individual equities can fall 50% in price or more without derailing the value story. "Most speculators aren't willing to do that."

On single-asset developers: "Certain developers are amalgamators—more financial jockeys like myself than engineers—and participating in their game is very, very risky. It could work, but it's very risky. What you look for in a developer is track record and track record around specific expertise."

Rule points to G Mining as an example. "The Gvernyak family has been involved as mine builders and contractors for 40 years. They've developed a specialty in constructing gold mines in orogenic settings in tropical remote locations with high rainfall. Guess how they built G Mining? They built high-quality gold mines in orogenic environments in tropical high rainfall environments. In other words, they stuck to what they did. The consequence of that is the stock is up tenfold in three and a half years and is still, from my point of view, undervalued."

Developers who know what they're doing and stick to their regional and deposit expertise can be expected to do very well. "The Bay Street cowboys ultimately always round-trip. The stock goes up, the stock goes down—and that process of falling is a lot less pleasant if you're long than the process of it going up."

Rule also anticipates a wave of M&A in the mining space. The recent G Mining takeover of G2 at a 72% premium—yet still accretive—illustrates the opportunity. "That'll tell you how much room there is in the next three years in the gold mining space in terms of value arbitrage. Mercifully for me, I expressed that four to five years ago, and if you looked at my own portfolio in an allocation sense, you would see a gross overallocation to gold. So I don't need to go deeper into the space."

## Finding Hate in a Market That Has Run Out of Dislikes

When pressed on what commodity currently fits his preferred criteria—rank hatred—Rule is surprisingly blunt: "Sadly, no. We have run out of hate."

The only hate currently expressed in markets is geographic. "There are places in the world that are still hated. Mercifully for me. The one that I would most like to take advantage of is Russia, and I can't because of political constraints both in Russia and the United States. But there are other parts of the world that are hated."

Were he still a real estate speculator, Rule says he'd be in Dubai, Abu Dhabi, and Qatar "in a flash." Iran, despite its evident risks, would be attractive if he were allowed. "Western investors are overly concerned about political risks in a lot of places that they can't pronounce or spell, mostly because of ignorance. I'm not suggesting those places aren't risky. I'm just suggesting to you that they are not as risky, relative to the US and Canada, as they're priced—which is to say, I think the US and Canada are more risky than people give them credit for being."

In Rule's view, the only classification of oil companies he's still actively trying to buy is conventional explorers in frontier and emerging markets—"places that other people can't spell. Unfortunately for me, the shares of those companies, which were really in the dumpster in November and December, are up 30 or 35% as a consequence of the higher oil price. And so right now, my bids for them are unrequited love."

## Conclusion: The Long View in a Short-Term World

The conversation with Rick Rule closes with a reminder that the resource investment game rewards patience and discipline over market timing and narratives. Whether assessing oil's two-to-three week tipping point, potash's structural advantages in Saskatchewan, or gold's role as a savings vehicle rather than a trading instrument, Rule consistently returns to the fundamentals: systematic underinvestment compounds over time, demographic tailwinds underpin long-term demand, and the hatred of quality assets creates the opportunities that patient investors need.

The world may be focused on the immediate crisis at Hormuz. But for those willing to look three to five years out—the timeframe where Rule's convictions lie—the real opportunity may be the calm before a storm that's already priced in, prepared for, and ultimately unavoidable.
