## Introduction

On October 19th, financial markets marked the 39th anniversary of the 1987 Black Monday crash, when the Dow Jones Industrial Average plummeted 22% in a single day. While today’s market did not experience a similar crash—with stocks rebounding and the NASDAQ up 1.4% intraday—the underlying conditions bear a striking resemblance to those that precipitated the historic collapse.

Jim Welsh, founder of Macro Tides, joined the discussion to dissect these parallels. He argues that despite the superficial bounce in semiconductor stocks, the broader market is exhibiting significant weakness. With AI stocks appearing overvalued and Treasury yields surging, Welsh suggests that a major shift in capital allocation from equities to bonds may be imminent.

## The Illusion of Strength: Market Breadte and Semiconductors

Although the S&P 500 appeared positive during the session, Welsh points to deteriorating market breadth as a critical warning sign. While the index rose, there were approximately 600 more stocks declining than rising. Furthermore, the S&P Equal Weight index remained negative, indicating that the gains were driven almost exclusively by mega-cap tech stocks and semiconductors rather than broad-based participation.

### The Semiconductor Pullback
The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (SOX) ETF experienced its longest losing streak since October 2008. Welsh had previously forecasted a decline in the SMH (VanEck Semiconductor ETF) from highs around $668 to near $515. The index bottomed at $503 before rallying back toward $600.

Welsh views the recent rally as an "ABC" correction pattern that has likely completed. He anticipates further downside for semiconductors, potentially breaking below the $503 level. While the Relative Strength Index (RSI) on SMH dropped to 30, signaling a technical bounce, Welsh believes this is a dead cat bounce rather than a sustainable recovery. The lack of money flowing into the broad market creates a "tag team" dynamic where capital rotates between the Magnificent 7 and the broader market, but rarely sustains both simultaneously.

## Interest Rates: The Key Driver for Equities and Bonds

The central theme of Welsh’s analysis is the relationship between Treasury yields and asset prices. He argues that the broad market’s health is directly tied to the direction of interest rates.

### Yield Trends and Contrary Opinion
Welsh notes that The Economist recently featured a cover story warning of a bond crash, signaling extreme bearish sentiment. Historically, such media attention often marks the end of a trend rather than its beginning. Welsh observes that Treasury bonds have declined by 47% from their March 2020 highs over six years. He posits that we are nearing a trading low in bond prices and a top in yields.

A key technical indicator to watch is an intermarket divergence between the 10-year and 30-year Treasury yields. If the 10-year yield rises to take out its recent high of 5.36%, but the 30-year yield fails to break above 5.73%, it would signal a bullish turning point for bonds. Welsh suggests that once this divergence is confirmed, it will be an opportune time to buy bonds.

### The Fed’s Next Move
Regarding the Federal Reserve’s upcoming decision, prediction markets indicate an 86% chance of holding rates steady in October. Welsh agrees with this assessment, citing several factors:
1.  **Supply Shock vs. Demand Inflation:** Fed Chicago President Austan Goolsbee highlighted that while supply shock inflation (which persisted for five years) is fading, the Fed must respond to it now. However, this response will likely be paced rather than aggressive.
2.  **FOMC Hesitation:** Nine members of the FOMC did not support a rate hike in June. They are likely reluctant to hike again immediately after September, preferring to see more data.
3.  **Political Timing:** With elections just days away, the Fed is unlikely to make a controversial move unless absolutely necessary.

Welsh predicts no hike in October but expects one in December, with future policy dependent on incoming economic data.

## The AI Trade: Mania or Productivity Revolution?

The disparity between AI-related earnings and the rest of the market is stark. Since 2023, AI-related earnings have surged 300%, while the rest of the market has seen only a 15% increase. This has created a self-fulfilling prophecy where capital flows into AI stocks because they are delivering results, further driving up their valuations.

However, Welsh warns of a mismatch between spending and adoption. Companies are investing billions in data centers and infrastructure, but the average business is not yet seeing meaningful productivity gains. NFIB surveys indicate that small businesses are resistant to replacing staff with AI due to uncertainty about its effectiveness. This slow adoption rate means the promised productivity boom may take longer to materialize than the market currently prices in.

Welsh draws parallels to the dot-com era, noting that while the internet was ultimately transformative, many infrastructure companies failed before seeing returns. Semiconductors are a highly cyclical sector; SMH dropped over 65% during the 2001-2002 and 2008 financial crises. With Nvidia now representing 8% of the S&P 500, an unwinding of the AI trade could have profound effects on the broader economy and consumer wealth.

## Economic Vulnerabilities and Consumer Spending

The U.S. economy is currently growing at roughly 5% nominally, similar to 1987. However, Welsh highlights a structural vulnerability: the top 10% of earners account for nearly 50% of consumer spending. This segment is highly leveraged to asset prices. If a bear market causes significant wealth destruction for this group, they will cut back on spending, potentially triggering or deepening a recession.

Welsh argues that we are approaching a secular bear market environment. The weakness in the advance-decline line—a technical indicator tracking stocks making new highs versus lows—has been consistently negative. Historically, every major decline since 1928 has been preceded by such divergence. The trigger for the next major sell-off could be rising rates, an oil shock, or simply a slowdown in AI capital expenditure growth.

## Conclusion: Caution Ahead

Jim Welsh remains skeptical of the current market rally. While he acknowledges the potential for a short-term bounce in semiconductors and the Magnificent 7, he believes there is more downside risk than upside potential. The key to a sustainable equity rally lies in a sustained decline in Treasury yields, which has not yet occurred.

For investors, Welsh advises patience. Waiting for a definitive top in yields and a clearer signal of broad market participation may provide better opportunities than chasing the current AI-driven momentum. As the bond market approaches a potential inflection point, the rotation from equities to fixed income could be one of the most significant shifts of the coming year.
