## A Warning That Echoes 2007

In the turbulent landscape of global finance, a veteran market watcher is raising alarms that should command everyone's attention. John Rubino, a respected financial analyst and editor of the Rubino Survey, recently appeared on a major financial channel to explain why he believes the next major market crisis won't emerge from housing or student loans—but from an area that has largely escaped mainstream scrutiny: private credit.

"The amount of money that's in there now is way more than was in subprime mortgages in 2007," Rubino warns. "So the danger of that being the first domino that starts knocking down a lot of other dominoes is very real."

This is a stark comparison. The subprime mortgage crisis of 2007-2009 nearly collapsed the global financial system, triggering the Great Recession. If Rubino's assessment is correct, we're standing at the edge of something potentially far more devastating—and the warning signs are already accumulating.

## The Private Credit Timebomb

### Understanding the Shadow Banking Threat

Private credit represents a massive, largely unregulated corner of the financial world that has exploded in size over the past decade. Here's the basic mechanism:

- **Pooled Capital**: Wealthy investors and institutions combine their money into private credit funds
- **Direct Lending**: These funds lend directly to medium-sized companies that traditional banks have largely abandoned
- **Premium Returns**: Borrowers accept higher interest rates in exchange for faster approvals and less bureaucracy

For years, this arrangement worked beautifully. Companies got needed capital, investors earned attractive returns, and everyone avoided the scrutiny that comes with public markets. But according to Rubino, that arrangement is now unraveling.

### Why Private Credit Is the Leading Candidate for the First Domino

Rubino identifies several interconnected problems:

- **Rate Sensitivity**: Private credit portfolios were assembled during an era of near-zero interest rates. With current rates at 4.45% on the 10-year Treasury, the math that made these investments attractive has fundamentally changed
- **Asset Quality Deterioration**: Much of the capital flowed into software companies—the exact sector now being disrupted by artificial intelligence. Many of these investments are worth far less than when they were made
- **Redemption Pressure**: Funds are experiencing significant outflows as investors reassess risk
- **Valuation Gaps**: Private credit companies face the impossible choice between selling assets at steep discounts or locking investors out entirely

"We've been seeing lately a lot of private credit companies have either had to—in one case they had to put money in themselves, in other words, the owners of the private credit company had to come in and bail out one of their big funds," Rubino explains. "You don't see that very often. Mostly you see them just close the gate and say, 'No, I'm sorry. According to the contract, we don't have to pay you back.'"

### The Cascade Effect

The concern isn't just about private credit funds themselves. These institutions have deep connections throughout the financial system:

- **Pension Funds**: Retirement systems bet heavily on private credit's promised returns
- **Insurance Companies**: These "low-risk" portfolios now contain significant exposure to assets that may be worth far less than reported
- **Regional Banks**: Many hold private credit obligations or have financed private equity activities
- **Shadow Banking Sector**: The entire ecosystem of non-bank financial intermediaries faces contagion risk

"If interest rates stay where they are, or God forbid go up from here, then we're going to see a torrent of bankruptcies in that space," Rubino says. "We're in the early stage of it now."

## Crosscurrents in Today's Market

What makes the current environment particularly difficult to navigate is the presence of multiple, contradictory market forces operating simultaneously. As Rubino observes:

**Inflationary Pressures:**

- Crisis-level deficits in the United States and many developed nations
- Oil price volatility (trading between $80 and $120 per barrel)
- Ongoing fiscal spending that stimulates economic activity

**Deflationary Pressures:**

- Rising interest rates that constrain borrowing
- Corporate layoffs accelerating (driven by AI but also broader economic concerns)
- Credit conditions tightening across sectors

**Other Conflicting Signals:**

- Low official unemployment statistics
- Waves of announced corporate layoffs across industries
- Stock markets at all-time highs despite unprecedented uncertainty
- The AI bubble distorting traditional valuation metrics

"You put all that together, and it's not clear there's any way to explain and or predict these markets," Rubino admits. "They're just doing what they're doing right now. I don't think anybody within the financial markets has a good idea of what the world one week from now is going to be like."

This uncertainty creates a challenging environment even for professional money managers. "If you're somebody who has to manage money and you have to be 100% invested in equities, even though you're making money right this minute, it's hard to imagine you sleeping well at night because you don't know what you're going to wake up to in the morning."

## The Oil Volatility Factor

### A Wide Trading Range with Geopolitical Premium

Oil markets have been exceptionally volatile, trading in what Rubino calls "a rangebound between $80 and $120"—a remarkably wide band that reflects genuine uncertainty about supply and demand fundamentals.

Factors creating oil market chaos:

- **Strait of Hormuz tensions**: One day blocked, the next day ships being escorted through
- **Strategic stockpiles**: Countries appeared to have comfortable reserves, then suddenly supplies seemed critically tight
- **Economic restrictions**: draconian energy consumption measures emerging in Japan, Korea, and elsewhere
- **Middle East conflict**: The Iranian missile campaign against the UAE added significant geopolitical risk premium

The uncertainty is compounded because oil's economic effects are contradictory. Higher prices are inflationary (squeezing consumers and businesses), but they also signal economic strength and energy demand. Lower prices suggest demand destruction but provide consumer relief.

## The Federal Reserve's Impossible Position

### Conflicting Pressures on Interest Rates

The Federal Reserve finds itself trapped between competing imperatives:

**Reasons to Keep Rates High:**

- Inflation remains a persistent concern
- Deficits at 6% of GDP require market discipline
- Currency stability in a world of competitive devaluation

**Reasons to Cut Rates:**

- Housing market frozen with mortgage rates "too high to unfreeze the market"
- Private credit crisis threatening broader financial stability
- Recession risks mounting as credit conditions tighten
- Political pressure from an administration that made low rates a priority

Rubino notes that during recent Federal Reserve meetings, governors were "divided in a really unusual way"—with one wanting immediate cuts and three wanting to remove language promising future reductions.

### The Fiscal Dominance Question

One school of thought suggests that massive government spending ($2 trillion deficits) effectively does the Fed's job for it, creating enough economic stimulus that rate cuts aren't necessary. Rubino is skeptical: "I kind of think that's probably not true, but we'll have to see because it felt like a recession was coming a year ago. There were just so many things that were going wrong in the economy, and we didn't get that."

He also notes the uncomfortable truth about how economic data is compiled: "We may have actually seen a recession a few years ago. Well, we'll change the definition of everything if we have to."

### The Inevitable Response

Regardless of the immediate Fed posture, Rubino believes the endgame is clear: when crisis hits, central banks will respond aggressively.

"In the background of all of this chaos is the fact that the Federal Reserve is ready to massively increase the money supply, massively start buying back bonds and equities, and cut interest rates back to zero and maybe negative," he explains. "That's all out there waiting to happen because those are the only tools the big central banks have now to combat any kind of a crisis."

This anticipated response shapes his investment strategy: "Whatever the crisis is that's coming—and there's the possibility of several big crises hitting the financial markets in the not too distant future—they're going to be responded to by extremely easy money."

## The Case for Real Assets

### Why Rubino Is Accumulating Commodities

Despite—or perhaps because of—the uncertainty, Rubino has a clear investment approach: "I'm ignoring this market, on the assumption that gold is eventually going to go to $10,000 an ounce and silver is going to go to $200 or $300 an ounce. And we need so much more copper than we have that its price has to go up. And same thing with uranium."

His strategy involves gradually accumulating "really high quality assets in the commodity space," regardless of short-term price movements. The reasoning is straightforward:

- **Gold**: Central bank response to crisis will drive prices dramatically higher
- **Silver**: Industrial demand (solar panels, electric vehicles, electronics) combined with constrained supply
- **Copper**: Essential for electrification, infrastructure, and technology—supply deficits are structural
- **Uranium**: Nuclear energy renaissance accelerating globally

"The long-term story for gold, silver, copper, uranium, and a lot of other commodities—it's just so great," Rubino says. "There are so many things that are going to go right for commodities in the coming decade that there's no reason not to just placidly add to positions going forward."

### Gold's Current Consolidation Phase

After an extraordinary run from below $2,000 to above $5,000 per ounce, gold is undergoing a normal consolidation. Rubino sees this as healthy rather than concerning:

- The move was so rapid that some profit-taking was inevitable
- Corrections in gold and silver are often triggered by equity market stress (2008, 2022), but these are typically "V-bottoms"
- Historical precedent suggests gold bounces back quickly when the Fed pivots to easing

"Gold normally likes chaos," Rubino notes. "That's where capital flees when the world becomes unpredictable, especially with an inflationary bias. So you would expect gold and silver to be doing really well in this kind of a hyper unpredictable market."

The current pattern—a few months of consolidation following a 40-67% move—is technically normal. "You can't have a bull market run in one direction and go up continuously forever because too many people have to take profits along the way."

### The Silver Outlook

Silver presents an interesting case. The metal has been "consolidating and grinding lower since February," trading around $73 per ounce at time of recording.

Rubino won't predict short-term movements: "I never get short-term predictions right. I've just given up trying."

But his long-term thesis remains intact: "Silver will go to a couple hundred dollars an ounce sometime in the next five or so years." The industrial demand story is compelling—solar panels, electric vehicles, high-tech weaponry, and electronics require significant silver inputs while above-ground supplies remain limited.

"There's going to be aggressive, maybe even panic buying that causes a default in one of the paper metals exchanges, and that shifts pricing to the physical exchange at a much higher price," he predicts. "I don't know if silver's going down to $40 an ounce from here, don't know if it's going to 120 in the next three or four months, but I think it's going to 120 in the not too distant future, even if it goes to 40 in the next couple of months."

## Bitcoin's Surprising Strength

### Why the Cryptocurrency Is Outperforming

Bitcoin has reached $80,000, defying expectations of many traditional analysts. Rubino offers several explanations:

- **Institutional Acceptance**: Bitcoin has gained legitimacy among institutional investors
- **Capital Flight**: With Middle East tensions elevated, wealthy individuals in the region may be using Bitcoin to move capital quickly across borders
- **Alternative to Fiat**: As traditional currencies face credibility questions, Bitcoin attracts capital seeking alternatives
- **Historical Patterns**: Bitcoin has previously shown a pattern of falling hard, recovering to new highs, then falling again—currently in recovery mode

"Anything that's not a fiat currency should be attracting attention right now because the fiat currencies are wildly badly run, and their management is getting worse," Rubino observes.

However, he maintains his primary focus remains on physical precious metals and industrial commodities rather than digital assets.

## The Shrinking Trust Horizon

### A Meta-Trend Driving Capital Flows

Beyond specific market predictions, Rubino sees a fundamental shift in how people view institutions and systems:

"The big background story in the world right now is the shrinking trust horizon. In other words, we just do not believe that the guys in charge of the big systems either have our best interest at heart or know what they're doing at all. We think they're incompetent or corrupt or both."

This trust deficit is manifesting in concrete ways:

- **Return to Resilience**: People are becoming "preppers again"—growing food, reducing debt, building savings
- **Lifestyle Changes**: A shift toward self-sufficiency reminiscent of previous generations who lived through economic crises
- **Capital Reallocation**: Money flowing from intangible assets toward real assets
- **Institutional Distrust**: Declining confidence in government, central banks, and established financial systems

"The trust thing is the meta idea that overlays the financial markets right now," Rubino explains. "As we lose trust in big systems, then capital is going to flow towards real assets."

This shift aligns perfectly with his commodity-focused strategy. Real assets—gold, silver, copper, land, productive businesses—hold value regardless of what central banks do or don't do with interest rates.

## The Unsustainable Fiscal Path

### Numbers That Cannot End Well

Rubino is direct about the long-term fiscal picture facing the United States:

**Current Situation:**

- Government debt: 120% of GDP (unprecedented in peacetime)
- Annual deficits: 6% of GDP (twice what European Maastricht Treaty allowed for eurozone nations)
- Interest costs: $1.2 trillion per year and climbing
- Debt service costs exceeding military spending

"These are numbers that are unsustainable, and they certainly shouldn't be increased from here," Rubino warns. "But we're going to increase all those numbers from here."

**The Death Spiral Dynamic:**

When borrowing to cover interest costs becomes standard practice, and those costs exceed spending on national priorities like defense, historical parallels emerge. "When you get to the point where you're borrowing to cover your interest cost and that interest cost is higher than your military budget, and you're trying to run a global military empire—you're the Roman Empire. The barbarians are at the gate, and you're headed for hyperinflation."

These aren't predictions of imminent collapse, but rather recognition that the current trajectory is unsustainable. Eventually, decisions will have to be made: default, devalue, or inflate away the debt through currency depreciation. Each path benefits different groups and harms others—and the transition periods tend to be chaotic.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Private credit represents the most significant systemic risk**: The shadow banking sector now contains more capital than subprime mortgages did in 2007, and it's showing serious stress fractures. Rising interest rates make refinancing impossible for many borrowers, and redemption pressures are mounting.

2. **Multiple contradictory forces are creating unprecedented market uncertainty**: Deficit spending pushes inflationary pressure while rising rates create deflationary pressure. Low unemployment coexists with waves of layoffs. These crosscurrents make traditional market analysis unreliable.

3. **Central banks will respond to any crisis with massive money printing**: The Fed's toolkit is essentially limited to easing, which means the endgame for any market stress is likely to be more currency debasement—a fundamentally bullish environment for precious metals.

4. **Real assets should outperform in the trust-deficient environment**: Gold, silver, copper, and uranium all have strong fundamental cases—industrial demand combined with supply constraints—and they benefit from institutional distrust of fiat currencies and traditional financial instruments.

5. **Dollar-cost averaging into quality commodities is the recommended approach**: Rather than trying to time markets, Rubino advocates placing low-ball bids on assets you want more of and letting the long-term thesis play out while ignoring short-term volatility.
