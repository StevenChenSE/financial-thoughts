## Exploring the Authentic Flavors of the Capital

Beijing is a city with a rich history, and its food culture is equally deep. In this culinary exploration, we dive into some of the most iconic dishes of the city, starting from bustling street stalls to high-end restaurants. From the savory aroma of beef pies to the crispy perfection of top-tier roast duck, every bite tells a story of tradition and craftsmanship.

## The Street Food Experience: Beef Pies and Water-Boiled Tripe

Our journey begins at a renowned street food spot known for its beef pies. We ordered two types: beef with scallions and beef with fennel, along with a side of water-boiled tripe (shui bao du). 

The water-boiled tripe is a dish that demands precision. Cooked for just seconds, it retains a perfect texture—crisp, chewy, and slightly elastic. It’s dipped in a "28 sauce," a blend of sesame paste and soybean paste, enhancing its rich flavor. While there are many variations of tripe dishes in Beijing, this quick taste was a delightful introduction.

The beef pies were the real star here. Freshly made, the crust was thick, chewy, and crispy, with a golden, oily sheen. The beef with scallions was savory, a flavor profile that might be considered slightly salty to some but is typical of Beijing tastes. The beef with fennel pie offered a more aromatic experience, with the fennel adding a subtle fragrance that balanced the saltiness. Interestingly, while often associated with Beijing, these pies actually originate from Hui Muslim cuisine, evolving from simple dough-wrapped meat snacks to the pan-fried delights we know today.

## Traditional Zhi Zi Roast Meat by the Lake

Next, we headed to a historic restaurant near Shichahai Lake, famous for its traditional Zhi Zi roast meat. This area, once a gathering place for nobility, is now a vibrant tourist spot. The restaurant itself showcases the traditional "Zhi Zi," a large iron grill used for cooking.

We started with some unique appetizers. A honey-glazed lamb dish offered a sweet and tangy flavor, unlike anything typical. We also tried a "Chinese pizza," a cornmeal pancake filled with leeks and eggs, which had a pleasant corn aroma.

The main event was the Zhi Zi roast meat. Thinly sliced lamb leg meat was cooked on the sizzling iron grill right before our eyes. The meat was tender with a slight lamb flavor and a delightful charred aroma. We paired it with "smile buns" (Kai Kou Xiao), a type of sesame bread. The tradition here is fun: the more meat you sandwich into the bun for your friend, the deeper your friendship. A generous serving of juicy lamb, with the oil seeping into the crispy bun, was an absolute delight.

## Sweet Treats and Century-Old Pastries

After the savory feast, we strolled along Shichahai Lake, where we picked up a classic Beijing snack: a large candied hawthorn skewer (tanghulu). For five yuan, we enjoyed the perfect balance of sweet, crispy sugar coating and tangy hawthorn fruit—a must-try experience in Beijing.

We also visited a century-old bakery, a staple for souvenirs. We sampled their pea paste cake (Wandou Huang), said to be a favorite of Empress Dowager Cixi. However, our expert companion noted that while good, it lacked the ultra-smooth, silky texture of the premium versions, likely due to modern "healthier" recipes.

## The Crown Jewel: Premium Roast Duck

The final stop was a highly acclaimed roast duck restaurant, known for its Michelin and Black Pearl recognition. The dining experience started with some classic Beijing appetizers.

First was the "Qianlong Cabbage," a raw cabbage salad dressed in sesame paste. Despite the name, it has no real connection to Emperor Qianlong; it's simply a modern creation using the tenderest part of the cabbage, seasoned with Beijing's beloved sesame paste. We also tried mustard duck feet, which were fresh, chewy, and had a stimulating mustard flavor reminiscent of the classic "Mustard Turnip" dish.

Then, the star arrived: the roast duck. The restaurant uses a traditional "Guazhi" (hanging oven) method, the same lineage as the famous Quanjude. Our guide revealed a secret: the owner is a disciple of Xu Fulin, a master chef from Quanjude and president of the China Roast Duck Association.

The duck was roasted over an open flame, which gives the skin its signature crispiness. Unlike "menlu" (closed oven) duck, which focuses on tender meat, Guazhi duck is all about the skin. The chef, with years of training, expertly carved the duck tableside.

We learned the proper way to eat it. First, a slice of crispy skin and meat, dipped in sugar, offering a burst of oil and sweet flavor. Next, a classic wrap: a thin, chewy pancake, a smear of sweet bean sauce, some cucumber, scallions, and slices of duck. The key is not to overpower the duck's natural flavor with too much sauce. For the most tender part, the duck tenderloin, it's best eaten simply with garlic paste.

Finally, the duck bones were fried with pepper salt, creating a crunchy and flavorful finish.

## A Feast for the Senses

From the humble street beef pies to the elegant roast duck, Beijing's food scene offers a diverse and unforgettable experience. Each dish, with its unique history and preparation method, reflects the city's rich cultural heritage. While our time was short, the flavors lingered, leaving us eager to return and explore more of Beijing's culinary treasures.
