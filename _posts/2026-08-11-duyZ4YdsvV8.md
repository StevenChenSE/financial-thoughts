## Chart Breakouts, Macro Tailwinds, and How to Play Them

In this episode of *Finding Value*, Andy reviews social media commentary and charts to identify multi-decade breakouts in commodities and related sectors. He emphasizes long-term perspective, risk-reward optimization, and position sizing, while contrasting trader-centric approaches with durable investing strategies.

## Multi-Year Bases Finally Breaking

Andy highlights that major breakouts don’t occur “out of the blue.” Looking at charts from 1999 to 2026, he points to large multi-year consolidations and a notable bottom in 2020 that aligned with extreme valuation dislocations—especially in the oil-to-gold ratio and equities vs. underlying commodities (e.g., XLE vs. crude oil).

He notes:
- A “three-hump” consolidation pattern preceding the breakout.
- Out-of-cycle trading and a period of aggressive liquidity expansion (“they printed money”) that created an exceptionally favorable setup.
- Breakouts now confirmed across multiple commodity-linked sectors.

In his view, these are not isolated moves but coordinated breakouts consistent with an inflationary impulse rather than an imminent implosion.

## Key Sectors and ETFs Breaking Higher

Andy reviews several ETFs and sectors that are breaking out from long-term bases:

- XLE (Energy Select Sector SPDR Fund): Breaking out of a multi-year base, signaling strength in the energy group.
- COPX (Global X Copper Miners ETF): Surging on a breakout, reflecting bullish momentum in copper miners.
- Emerging Markets: Also breaking higher from a multi-decade base, challenging recession narratives.
- SLX (VanEck Steel ETF): Showing a strong bullish breakout; Andy describes steel as about as bullish as it gets.
- GDX (VanEck Gold Miners ETF): Breaking out of a falling wedge, which he interprets as a continuation pattern rather than bearish behavior.

He argues that seeing breakouts across energy, copper, emerging markets, steel, and gold miners simultaneously is inconsistent with a looming crash or recession.

## “What If We Get a Recession?”

Andy directly addresses recurring recession fears:

- If a major implosion were truly imminent, you would not see synchronized breakouts from multi-year bases in cyclical and commodity-sensitive assets.
- He contrasts this with prior cycles where “smart money” and banks positioned ahead of downturns by moving into bonds, pushing interest rates lower and inverting the yield curve.
- In today’s environment, the 2-year rate has been rising, supporting his view of an inflationary impulse higher rather than a deflationary crash.

He concludes that while recessions may occur in the future, the current structure suggests you should “buy what is given to you in front of you” rather than speculate on hypothetical downturns that contradict the price action.

## Risk-Reward, Asymmetry, and Position Sizing

Andy uses a viewer’s comment about an IRA tripling versus AMD quadrupling to illustrate a core principle: optimize risk-reward, not just headline returns.

Key points:
- A trade that triples with limited downside (e.g., 10–20%) can be superior to one that quadruples but carries 80% downside risk.
- The goal is to “play the game” long enough to compound by avoiding catastrophic losses.
- When asymmetry is extreme (e.g., potential 100x with controlled probability), he allows exposure via small, carefully sized positions rather than all-in bets.

He contrasts highly volatile junior silver and uranium names (which can pull back 70–80%) with more stable companies in the same sectors or those executing share buybacks, which offer better risk-adjusted upside. His preference is to build core positions in names with durable downside protection and steady upside pressure, while keeping satellite allocations to higher-risk juniors.

## Investing vs. Trading: Patience and Frictional Losses

Andy critiques many trading philosophies and quotes circulating online, including ideas around:

- “Being wrong is inevitable; staying wrong is a choice.”
- “Mastering position sizing is like a volume control for fear.”
- “Don’t be a hero; don’t have an ego; always question yourself.”

His perspective:
- Constantly using price and chart patterns to justify being right or wrong can cause traders to be shaken out of large, multi-year winners.
- He doubts that traders who rely on tight stop-losses (e.g., 6–10%) can realistically capture 30–40x moves over 5–10 years without being whipsawed.
- He favors buying fundamentally cheap assets, understanding the business and sector, and using technicals to confirm—not replace—the fundamental thesis.

He praises Munger-like thinking: wait for rare, obvious opportunities where the price is “stupid cheap,” the risk-reward is extreme, and you can concentrate meaningfully. These moments (like the 2020 bottom) are infrequent, so investors must have dry powder and the patience to deploy it decisively.

## Macro Context: Spending, Debt, and the Liquidity Story

Andy touches on macro themes that underpin his bullish stance on hard assets:

- Skepticism that “DOGE” (and political narratives around massive spending cuts) can meaningfully reduce federal spending without destabilizing the system.
- Belief that the currency and debt structure requires continuous money creation; otherwise, the system faces solvency and liquidity stress.
- Emphasis on physical, real-world assets as protection against ongoing monetary expansion and debt accumulation.

He also highlights a specific liquidity indicator:
- The widening spread between the effective federal funds rate and the 30-year Treasury yield.
- Banks borrow short and lend long; a wider spread improves their profitability and can fuel risk-taking, including in housing.
- He suggests this dynamic could support further moves in housing and risk assets if banks resume aggressive lending.

## Practical Takeaways

Andy’s framework can be summarized as:

1. Trade with the structure: When multiple sectors break out from multi-year or multi-decade bases, treat that as the signal rather than fighting it with recession scenarios.
2. Focus on risk-reward asymmetry: Prefer outcomes where limited downside accompanies significant upside, and use position sizing to manage tail-risk ideas.
3. Combine fundamentals and technicals: Understand the business, sector supply/demand, and macro backdrop; let charts confirm those theses.
4. Avoid overtrading: Resist the urge to optimize every small move. Capture the major trend, reduce frictional costs and taxes, and let compounding work.
5. Be prepared for the rare big opportunities: Maintain cash or flexible allocations so you can invest “promptly in scale” when conditions become obviously favorable.

## Summary

Andy argues that we are witnessing coordinated, multi-decade breakouts in key commodity-related sectors—energy (XLE), copper (COPX), steel (SLX), gold miners (GDX), and emerging markets—driven by an inflationary impulse and supportive liquidity dynamics. Rather than chasing hypothetical recessions, he advocates buying into these structures while prioritizing risk-reward, thoughtful allocation, and long-term holding. Technical patterns should confirm, not replace, a deep understanding of fundamentals, and investors should position themselves to act decisively when rare, high-conviction opportunities arise.
