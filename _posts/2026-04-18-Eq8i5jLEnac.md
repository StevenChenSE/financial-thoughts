## Why Would You Buy a 30-Year Bond at 4.75%?

The markets have staged a dramatic V-shaped recovery from the lows in late March, with the S&P 500 approaching new all-time highs once again. But according to Ted Oakley, founder of Oxbow Advisors, investors should think twice before chasing this rally.

"I'm not surprised by it because we really think that inflation will go on up all through the rest of the year—eventually go over 4% at the end of the year," Oakley said during a recent appearance on the show. "What I am surprised is some of these big governors talking about lowering rates. It's transitional and all that sort of thing. It doesn't look transitional to us."

The V-shaped recovery appears driven more by hope than fundamentals. "People are trading off of the news really as much as anything," Oakley explained. "They think, well, this will be over really soon and so things will be back in the money again. I think it's a chasing of the market."

## Historical Parallels: Lessons from 2003

Oakley draws striking parallels between the current environment and the beginning of the Iraq War in 2003. At that time, his firm had sold out of their last tech stock in March 1999—practically a year early—and faced significant criticism. But that patience paid off.

"By the time we got to March of '03, which is when the market bottomed and started up, we had bought a lot of cheap things over the last six months," Oakley recalled. "We owned a lot of value stocks, REITs, things that pay real high cash flows, and all of those did really well."

Most importantly, they maintained liquidity. "We had probably 40-45% liquid, and so we were able to really own some things after a really tough three-year bear market, and it really paid off."

Today, Oakley sees similar opportunities for those with dry powder. The current environment mirrors that period in another way: the "Magnificent Seven" tech stocks are playing the role that tech stocks did before the 2003 lows. "They've underperformed really since October. They bounced back some in here, don't get me wrong, but they're chasing them again when they probably would be better off to look at some single names or more value names."

## Oil: Higher for Longer Than Expected

Perhaps no topic generated more conviction in Oakley's discussion than energy markets. Despite recent volatility, he believes oil prices will remain elevated longer than most expect.

"Twenty percent of the oil and 20% of the natural gas of the world comes through those straits over there, plus all the things they blow up," Oakley noted, referring to the Strait of Hormuz. "I think people are underestimating that. They want to trade it because they think it's going to come back to 30 or 40 or some crazy number, but we don't. We think that oil will continue to be a good something you should look at."

The structural dynamics haven't changed quickly for U.S. oil companies. "The cheapest well you can drill now is going to be $8 to 10 million even on land," Oakley explained. "They don't move that quickly after just a short period of move like that. It takes time for them to see that that's going to stay that way before you're going to see a lot of capital come out."

The portfolio has been positioned heavily in energy for two years, which proved beneficial during the first quarter volatility.

## The Bond Vigilantes Are Watching

Treasury yields have risen significantly since the beginning of March, with the 2-year yield climbing 50 basis points. Oakley sees this as the market doing what central banks might not accomplish through official channels.

"We really think that inflation will go on up all through the rest of the year," he emphasized. "The only thing I would say is if they press the rates hard, and we said this in the call we had: if the 2-year were to get to 4%, or the 3-year got to like 4.25%, 4.5%, buy some of that paper, okay? Some of it. Because that probably tells you you're going to go into a weaker economy eventually, and when you do, the rates are going to come down, and you'll make more than the 4%."

The longer-term picture, however, remains troubling. "We ran the debt—it went from $34 trillion to $39 trillion in 18 months. It went up 11% in just 18 months. And they keep spending money. It doesn't make any difference with a Republican or Democrat. Neither one of them care."

This fiscal trajectory has profound implications for bond investors. "Why would you buy a 30-year bond at 4.75%? Think about it—when you could buy a 2-year bond at 3.85%. There's no rhyme or reason for somebody to do that. I don't think you can ever buy long bonds again unless you just want to trade them for three or four or five months. I'm pretty sure the rest of your life, you don't want to own long bonds."

## Private Credit: A Warning Signs

Oakley has increasingly sounded alarms about the private credit market, where he estimates that 40% of companies backed by this capital have negative free cash flow and about 25% have interest coverage below one.

"Wall Street is always doing something to make money," he observed. "If they get in a position where they're not making them money on one thing, then they'll do another. The latest two ventures of that really were private equity and private credit. Both, in my opinion, will go down as not so great—or not what people thought they were."

The liquidity mismatch is particularly concerning. "It's always interesting to me to see an industry like private credit that has these long-term loans with companies, and yet they tell you as an investor, 'Yeah, but if you want out by the quarter, we'll give you your money.' Those two don't match up. It never did match up."

When investors requested $14 billion to come out of credit funds in the first quarter, "they didn't even get half of that because they threw the gates up and they couldn't do it."

The valuation methodology compounds the problem. "You tell me this is a really good company, but they're paying 11 and a quarter percent on this debt when they could borrow at the bank for 6 and a quarter, 6 and 3/4, 6 and 1/2, maybe 6 and a quarter. Who's fooling who here?"

## The Gambler's Generation

Oxbow Advisors' most recent quarterly letter, titled "The Gambler," examines a concerning trend among younger generations. The title reflects observations about how Gen Z and millennials are increasingly drawn to speculative activities—crypto, sports betting, and one-day options trading—rather than traditional long-term investing.

"They don't have a lot of money, and so they're really risking a lot to try to make something," Oakley said. "They've never learned the fine art of really how to invest actually in something you can own for 5, 10, 15, 20 years. I think they'll look up after 10 years and say, 'I really made a lot. I lost a lot. I'm not any better off than I was.'"

One-third of these age groups participate in gambling-type activities, a statistic that reveals broader financial literacy challenges. "If a third of all the age groups are doing those two things, that just tells me that they're not really laying a foundation for good investing."

Regarding the anticipated wealth transfer from Baby Boomers to younger generations, Oakley offers a cautious historical perspective. "Once they get a lot of money, they won't necessarily go that direction. If they do, then they're really making a mistake because then they've got a lot of net worth in hand. But I've seen a lot of this movement where we go from one generation to the next. I'd say a third of them, at least a third, I've seen this my whole career—they lose the money because they didn't make it."

## The Mag 7 Problem

The recent market rally has been driven primarily by tech stocks, with Oracle bouncing 13% in a single day. But Oakley sees troubling echoes of the late 1990s dot-com bubble.

"I think it's muscle memory," he explained. "They have recency bias where whatever's worked for you well the last two years, you're like, 'I can't wait to do it again,' and they do it this time. But they're forgetting that at the end of these situations, especially when these companies are—I mean, look at Microsoft. It's 14 or 15 times sales. These companies are expensive."

More concerning is how these companies have altered their balance sheets. "These big top 10, they were cash machines. They had tons of cash. They had free cash flow. But they spent it all, and they put it all back into data centers and AI. Whether it pays off or not, I don't know. But I do know this: they've changed the structure of their balance sheets from what was healthy to what I think is unhealthy."

The valuation question remains paramount. "You don't want to pay a premium when you're buying a company that's messed up the balance sheet. If you're going to pay a premium, it should be prior to what they're doing now, where they didn't have a lot of debt and they produced a lot of cash."

To get excited about these stocks again, Oakley says valuations would need to come down significantly. "They would have to come down. You'd have to get them in a range where you could say, 'Hey, for the money, this could be a good buy here.' I'm not against the businesses. It's just the price."

## Gold Miners: Different This Time?

Gold has surged toward $5,500, prompting Oakley to take some profits. "At the very end of '25 and the very first week of '26, we sold the gold back. We didn't get out, don't get me wrong, but we took part of the gold out, we took part of the gold miners out, and all of the silver miners—because they were too wild. Silver was up 200% last year. We thought for sure that was overdone."

But unlike the 2011 peak, Oakley sees fundamental differences in the gold mining sector today. "Back then, they had a lot more debt, and they were selling at crazy multiples of cash flow in 2011. If the gold miners sold at the same multiple of cash flow today that they did in '11, we'd have another 200% on them from here. They're cheap, and their margins are huge right now."

The recent dip in gold miners, followed by a rebound after he repurchased them at lower prices, validates this approach.

## Investment Philosophy: Buying When Nobody Wants It

Oakley's approach emphasizes buying quality assets when they're out of favor. "Our business is the only one in the world where when things are low-priced and on sale, virtually nobody wants them."

Recent examples from the portfolio include Hershey—purchased after cocoa prices declined, offering a 3% dividend and trading well off its high. DHT Maritime, a tanker company yielding about 9.5%, and National Energy Services Reunited, which operates in the Middle East and stands to benefit from reconstruction needs.

"You got to think about their business going forward is going to be good," Oakley noted. "Just because if you're in the business of fixing things and everything gets blown up, then you're probably going to have a lot more business."

On position sizing, Oakley advocates discipline over concentration. "Instead of taking 5% positions in one company, we'll take 1%. If it's a little expensive or whatever but it's on our buy list, we're going to keep that 1%. If it gets a little cheaper, we might buy a little bit more of it. Most people get hurt in a portfolio because they've got too much of something and they thought it was going to be great, and it's not."

## The Cost of Being Right Too Late

Perhaps the most poignant observation in the discussion touched on missed opportunities—a familiar experience for any investor.

"I've had it happen a lot of times," Oakley admitted, citing a recent example with Lionell Chemical. "I didn't buy it. It went up 40% in about three or four months. But I've done this a long time, and I've made a lot of errors. You learn a lot from them, but there's always some of that."

His advice? "People that look back with regret on what they're doing will never be good investors because you're always going to make some mistakes."

## What Keeps Him Up at Night

When asked about tail risks, Oakley expressed concern about potential escalation in the Middle East conflict. "If you can't make your normal weapons work—and I'm talking about the U.S.—they really haven't been as effective as they should have been probably with our big weapons because they're using little weapons. And if something changes to where one of these countries uses small nuclear capability, that would really bother us. That wouldn't be good for them or us or mankind."

This uncertainty is precisely why maintaining liquidity remains paramount in his strategy.

## Final Thoughts

Despite geopolitical concerns, Oakley hasn't fundamentally altered his thesis. The energy market may have changed for an extended period, he believes, and the portfolio remains positioned accordingly.

"People think, well, it'll go right back to normal," he concluded. "When you have that sort of interaction, though, it's going to be hard to go back to normal in a month or two."
