Behind closed doors, a sobering warning is being delivered to the highest levels of U.S. leadership: America’s ammunition stocks are dangerously low. According to military analysts, if current support commitments continue and the United States suddenly faces a confrontation with a near-peer adversary like China or Russia, the nation could find itself in serious trouble.

The concern centers on industrial capacity—the ability to manufacture munitions at the scale and speed required for a major conflict. This isn’t a hypothetical worry; recent history offers a stark lesson. During the first two years of the war in Ukraine, a recurring narrative played out in Western media. “Every other week or so,” recalls one observer, “someone would come on and say, ‘The Russians are running out of ammunition. The Russians are running out of missiles.’”

That assessment, it turns out, was “all crap.” Instead of collapsing, Russia rapidly invested in its manufacturing base, bringing it to full strength. The result was a wartime production engine capable of outproducing its opponents—a decisive advantage in a protracted conflict.

In today’s world, that industrial might is not just an asset; it is critical. As the analyst bluntly states, the ability to sustain and surge production is essential “in order to fight and win a” modern war. The implicit question hangs in the air: Does the United States still possess that foundational capability, or has decades of lean, just-in-time supply chains left it vulnerable?

The warning is clear: Without a robust, resilient, and scalable defense industrial base, military strategy and technological superiority may not be enough. The next major conflict may not be won by who has the best weapons, but by who can produce the most of them, for the longest time.
