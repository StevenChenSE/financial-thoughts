## Introduction

In a keynote address at the Mises Institute’s annual summit, economist and current Counselor to the U.S. Treasury Secretary, Dr. Judy Shelton, reflected on the centennial of Murray N. Rothbard’s birth. Shelton explored how Rothbard’s ideas are increasingly influencing global economic policy, from Argentina to Europe, while navigating her own transition from academic advocacy to government service. Her talk offered a candid look at the tension between ideological purity and political reality, highlighting the enduring relevance of sound money principles in an era of fiat currency debasement.

## From Academia to the Treasury

Shelton began by acknowledging the unique circumstances of her appearance. Having recently joined the U.S. Department of the Treasury as Counselor to the Secretary, she operates under strict ethical guidelines that prevent her from discussing official duties. Despite this constraint, she emphasized her excitement about moving from theoretical debate to the "crucible of policy decision-making."

Her career has spanned significant institutions:
*   **The Hoover Institution:** Where she worked alongside figures like Milton Friedman and Condoleezza Rice, authoring *The Coming Soviet Crash* and *Money Meltdown*.
*   **Atlas Network (formerly Atlas Economic Research Foundation):** Where she co-directed the Sound Money Project.
*   **Government Service:** Serving as the U.S. Executive Director of the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development in London.

Shelton also recalled her controversial 2019 nomination to the Federal Reserve Board of Governors by President Trump, a nomination that was ultimately rescinded by the Biden administration in 2021. She noted with amusement the hostile online reactions to her potential appointment, which often characterized her advocacy for the gold standard as "dangerous" or rooted in misunderstanding of fiat currency benefits.

## The Moral and Intellectual Compass

Shelton described the Mises Institute as her "conscience," a source of intellectual grounding that keeps her honest while serving in government. She revealed that she keeps three specific books from the Mises Institute prominently displayed in her new office:
1.  *Money, Sound and Unsound* by Joseph T. Salerno.
2.  *Murray Rothbard, The Making of an Austrian Economist* by Dr. Salerno and Dr. Patrick Newton.
3.  A compilation of Rothbard’s works, including *What Has Government Done to Our Money?* and *The Case for a 100% Gold Dollar*.

She used the third book as a metaphor for the subtle pressures of public service, joking that while she displays the others, the "incendiary" titles by Rothbard are kept locked away. This anecdote illustrated her broader point: being in the arena requires balancing conviction with consequence.

## Jefferson, Rothbard, and the Definition of Money

Shelton drew a direct line from Thomas Jefferson’s foundational monetary philosophy to Murray Rothbard’s modern critiques. She highlighted Jefferson’s 1784 notes on establishing a money unit, where he argued that the dollar should be defined by precise weights of gold or silver to serve common Americans rather than financial elites. This concept is enshrined in Article I, Section 8 of the U.S. Constitution, which grants Congress the power to coin money and fix standards of weights and measures.

Shelton contrasted this original intent with the current Federal Reserve policy, which explicitly targets a 2% annual inflation rate. She criticized the Fed’s redefinition of "stable prices" as "stable inflation," calling it an oxymoron that deliberately debases the currency.

However, Shelton acknowledged the practical limitations of Rothbard’s proposal for a 100% gold-backed dollar. With $2.4 trillion in Federal Reserve notes circulating against roughly $1 trillion in official U.S. gold reserves (valued at current spot prices), full convertibility is currently impossible. Instead, she advocates for gold as a "monetary anchor" through mechanisms like Treasury Trust Bonds, a concept she has promoted for years.

## The Global Resurgence of Austrian Economics

Shelton argued that Rothbard’s ideas are no longer confined to libertarian think tanks but are gaining traction in mainstream political circles worldwide.

*   **Argentina:** She cited President Javier Milei as a prime example of Rothbard’s influence. Milei, who named one of his dogs after Murray Rothbard, credits the economist’s work on monopoly and competition for shifting him away from neoclassical theory. Shelton noted Milei’s declaration that he is "fulfilling Rothbard's dream" through deficit reduction and market reforms.
*   **Europe:** Shelton expressed concern over the political instability in France and Germany, noting the rise of parties like the National Rally and the Alternative for Germany (AfD). More importantly, she pointed to policy discussions within these movements that echo Austrian School principles. Advisers to the AfD have discussed replacing the euro with a new currency partially backed by gold, repatriating German gold reserves, and allowing currency competition to protect savings from inflation.

Shelton observed that these global shifts reflect a growing desire for "less monetary discretion" and stronger protection of individual wealth against state-driven devaluation.

## The Greenspan Paradox

To illustrate the difficulty of maintaining free-market convictions in positions of power, Shelton shared a personal anecdote about Alan Greenspan. Greenspan, an acolyte of Ayn Rand, had written famously that "gold and economic freedom are inseparable" and that gold protects property rights from inflationary confiscation.

When Shelton was writing *Money Meltdown*, she sought permission to quote Greenspan’s earlier essay, *Gold and Economic Freedom*. Greenspan refused, citing the potential market implications of his words as Fed Chairman. He asked her to paraphrase him in the main text but cite the original in the footnotes. When Shelton offered to deliver a copy of the book to him, Greenspan requested it be wrapped in brown paper—a symbolic gesture of hiding his past free-market views from his current institutional role.

Shelton contrasted this with a 2019 email exchange during her Fed nomination controversy. When critics attacked her for supporting gold, she emailed Greenspan about the "almost hysterical antagonism toward the gold standard." His reply was succinct and powerful: *"If gold is such a worthless metal, then why does the US government and all other major governments hold so much of it in storage?"*

## Conclusion: Rothbard’s Bequest

Shelton concluded by framing Murray Rothbard not just as a historical figure, but as a source of ongoing intellectual energy. She described his work as a "bequest" to the world—a gift of analytical logic and moral clarity that challenges the legitimacy of the state and champions individual rights.

Whether viewed through a leftist lens (taxation as theft) or a rightist one (free-market infrastructure), Rothbard’s ideas defy simple categorization but resonate across the political spectrum. As global financial systems face increasing strain, Shelton suggested that we are on the cusp of a pivotal moment where these ideas may reshape the proper role of the state and the future of money.
