## Strategic Chokepoints and Global Supply Shocks

John Butler, author of the M4 Report, argues that the global economy is facing a fundamental shift driven by geopolitical instability and the loss of guaranteed freedom of navigation. He highlights the critical role of strategic chokepoints, specifically the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandab Strait. While Iran may be a weaker military power, its ability to deny access to these waterways creates an asymmetric threat. Approximately 20% of the world’s energy, petrochemicals, and fertilizers flow through Hormuz. Butler explains that forcing these waterways open is far more difficult than closing them, leading to a situation where insurance companies refuse coverage, effectively choking off trade.

This disruption is not limited to crude oil; it impacts essential downstream products. Butler warns that the cumulative effect is stagflationary: reduced supplies lead to higher prices while hitting economic growth. Although stockpiles in major economies like the US, China, and India are currently buffering the shock, these reserves will eventually deplete. The era of Pax Britannica and Pax Americana, where trade was largely frictionless, is over. Investors are failing to price in this uncertainty, keeping valuations artificially high.

## The AI Bubble and Market Valuations

Butler compares the current AI hype to the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s, suggesting it is even less justifiable. He notes that the Nasdaq and S&P 500 are ignoring significant risks, driven instead by earnings season and AI build-outs. He likens this to Wile E. Coyote running off a cliff but not yet feeling gravity. Until a "smoking gun" event occurs—such as major corporate profit warnings or debt defaults—markets will likely continue to ignore fundamentals.

He argues that in a world of uncertainty, investors should place lower valuations on companies with unpredictable operating environments. The current price-to-earnings ratios, hovering around 20 for US indices, are unsustainable in a stagflationary environment reminiscent of the 1970s. Butler predicts a re-rating of the market, potentially seeing multiples contract to single digits, similar to the early 1980s. He advises avoiding AI and luxury goods, favoring basic industries with pricing power that provide essential needs like food, shelter, and energy.

## Sovereign Debt and Central Bank Limitations

The interview also covers the precarious state of sovereign debt in the UK, Europe, and the US. Butler describes these nations as "sailing very close to the fiscal wind," with deficits funded by confidence games that are nearing their limits. He cites Milton Friedman’s view that inflation is always a monetary phenomenon. When supply shocks occur, central banks typically respond by expanding the money supply or providing guarantees, which fuels inflation rather than containing it.

Butler is skeptical that central banks can maintain a hawkish stance for long. He believes that if leaders like Kevin Warsh attempt to raise rates into a supply shock, they will crash the economy and be forced to cut rates. This dynamic supports a long-term bull market in precious metals. He asserts that the only thing that would reverse this trend is a complete departure from neo-Keynesian, inflationary policies, which he sees no evidence of.

## Precious Metals and Real Assets as Safe Havens

Despite short-term volatility, Butler remains bullish on gold and silver. He attributes recent sell-offs to "buy the rumor, sell the fact" dynamics following geopolitical tensions. He emphasizes that real interest rates are not the sole driver of precious metals prices; shifts in demand due to sanctions, loss of trust in financial systems, and currency debasement play crucial roles. He recommends a portfolio allocation of 15-20% in precious metals.

Beyond metals, Butler advocates for "limbo investing"—focusing on low-end value chain assets like energy, agriculture, and basic chemicals. He argues that energy is essential for maintaining current living standards, making it a core holding alongside gold. He specifically mentions the Argentinian state oil company due to its access to undeveloped oil fields. As the world moves toward a multipolar order, Butler suggests that the US must adapt to being a "normal country," balancing power rather than seeking hegemony, while investors should prioritize tangible assets over speculative technologies.
