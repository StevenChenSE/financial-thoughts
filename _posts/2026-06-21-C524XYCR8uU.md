## Steve Hanke on Kevin Warsh's First FOMC Meeting and the Road to Higher Rates

---

Professor Steve Hanke of Johns Hopkins University joined the program to analyze the Federal Reserve's latest policy decision and what it means for markets and the broader economy. The discussion comes just one day after Kevin Warsh took the podium as Fed chair at the FOMC meeting for the first time.

---

## Why the Fed Kept Rates Steady

Despite mounting inflation concerns, the Federal Open Market Committee voted to maintain the Fed funds rate unchanged. According to Hanke, this decision was both expected and strategically wise.

"The market had totally priced in exactly what they did—steady as you go," Hanke explained. The CME FedWatch Tool showed a 99.6% probability that rates would remain unchanged the day before the announcement.

However, Hanke identified three key reasons why Warsh chose to hold rates:

1. **Market expectations**: The financial markets had already priced in no change, so a rate hike would have caused significant volatility.

2. **Geopolitical uncertainty**: The economic landscape remains highly uncertain, both domestically and internationally.

3. **FOMC unity**: Raising rates at Warsh's first public appearance would likely have created a divided committee, potentially undermining his credibility.

"You wouldn't want a big split decision in the FOMC the first time Warsh comes out with a public statement," Hanke noted. "He's the maestro leading the orchestra, but if you would have raised rates, the orchestra would have been very much out of tune."

---

## Market Reaction and Rate Hike Expectations

Immediately following the FOMC press conference, markets reacted sharply:

- **Two-year yield** shot up significantly
- **Stocks fell**
- **Bonds fell**
- **Gold fell**
- **Bitcoin fell**

According to the CME FedWatch Tool, the bond market began pricing in up to three rate hikes by the end of December. The probability breakdown shows:

| Meeting | Probability of Rate Hike |
|---------|---------------------------|
| July | 30% (25 basis points) |
| September | 65-66% |
| October | 75% |
| December | 85% |

As Hanke observed, "There's only a 15% chance that it'll stay at 350 basis points by December."

The key insight from the market reaction is that while rates didn't change, the forward guidance shifted toward tighter monetary policy. This explains the simultaneous decline across multiple asset classes.

---

## The Inflation Genie Is Out of the Bottle

Hanke was direct about the inflation problem facing the Federal Reserve: "The inflation genie is out of the bottle. It's at 4.2% now, and the target is 2%. That's over double a target. It's not even close."

He argued that with inflation running more than double the Fed's target, tightening is inevitable: "It's very hard to argue against not squeezing and tightening things down when you have inflation running over double its target rate."

---

## The Real Driver of Inflation: Money Supply

Perhaps the most important analytical point Hanke made concerns what he believes the Fed is ignoring: the growth rate of the money supply.

"The inflation is there because of something the Fed is not paying any attention to, and that's the rate of growth in the money supply," Hanke stated.

He emphasized that approximately 80% of money supply growth is created by commercial banks, not the Federal Reserve itself. "The Fed is kind of peanuts in the whole picture. It's all about commercial banks."

According to Hanke, commercial bank loans have been growing at a "very hot rate" because banks are highly profitable, their capital is increasing, and they have significant lending capacity. When banks are healthy and see good opportunities for loans, they make loans, which directly contributes to money supply expansion.

---

## The Fed's Dilemma: Rate Hikes vs. Regulatory Loosening

Hanke highlighted a fundamental contradiction in current Fed policy discussions: while officials debate whether to raise interest rates to combat inflation, they're simultaneously considering loosening bank regulations.

In their book *Making Money Work*, Hanke and co-author Sekurki explain that regulations imposed on commercial banks are one of the biggest influences on monetary policy—often more significant than the Fed funds rate itself.

"Warsh is talking a lot about liberalizing or altering the regulations that are imposed on banks," Hanke observed. "What they're talking about is imposing new rules that would allow for a looser monetary policy in which the commercial banks would have more capacity to make more loans."

This creates a dilemma: "If there was a substantial growth in the money supply, of course, the nominal GDP—the real growth plus inflation—is going to go up. And the inflation genie is not going to go back in the bottle."

---

## Warsh as a "Monetarist Light"

The discussion then turned to Warsh's philosophical approach to monetary policy. While Warsh is widely known as a monetarist, he notably failed to address the balance sheet at the FOMC meeting.

Hanke explained that during his tenure as Fed governor between 2006 and 2011, Warsh's relationship with Ben Bernanke strained after he publicly opposed quantitative easing and the expansion of the balance sheet in 2009.

To demonstrate he is indeed a monetarist, Hanke argued Warsh would need to explicitly embrace the quantity theory of money: **MV = PY**.

"Volcker was proactive, and that was it—the quantity theory," Hanke noted. "Paul Volcker was very explicit."

Hanke contrasted this with Jerome Powell: "The Fed has explicitly rejected the quantity theory of money, and they've rejected monetarism on the record."

Regarding Warsh, Hanke offered a nuanced assessment: "If he's a monetarist, he's kind of a monetarist light. He's not a monetarist with a capital M like Paul Volcker. There's no question about that."

The key difference: "He's not rejecting it like Jerome Powell. He has some eye on the ball of the money supply, but it's hard to say because he's never clearly articulated it."

---

## New Task Forces and What's Next

The FOMC has established five new task forces addressing:

- Communications
- Balance sheet policy
- Data collection
- Productivity and jobs
- Inflation framework

Hanke expressed particular interest in the balance sheet policy task force, noting that Warsh has not addressed balance sheet expansion in public statements—a notable omission given his historical skepticism of QE.

---

## Summary

The Federal Reserve finds itself in a challenging position:

1. **Inflation is running hot** at 4.2%, more than double the target
2. **Markets expect rate hikes** — up to three by year-end
3. **The real driver** of inflation is money supply growth from commercial bank lending
4. **The Fed faces a dilemma**: Higher rates while potentially loosening bank regulations could send conflicting signals
5. **Warsh remains an enigma** — possibly a "monetarist light" but hasn't clearly articulated his approach

As Hanke concluded: "Inflation is roaring away. They've kept the Fed funds rate constant. They indicated they're tilting towards increasing those Fed funds rates before the end of the year. That's on one side. But the first order condition is: what are the bank regulations?"

The coming months will reveal whether the Fed can successfully thread this needle or whether the inflation genie will continue to run wild.
