In 1991, Scott Stornetta and his colleague Stuart Haber published a groundbreaking paper that would eventually become the architectural blueprint for Bitcoin—though neither of them knew it at the time. Three decades later, Satoshi Nakamoto cited their work in the Bitcoin white paper, acknowledging the foundational contributions of their data integrity structure: the concept of linking records into blocks, using Merkle trees, and distributing verification across a network of witnesses. Now, as the cryptocurrency landscape has evolved from speculative trading to stablecoins and smart contracts, Stornetta remains deeply engaged with the technology he helped create—and increasingly concerned about a new threat: artificial intelligence's ability to make "seeing no longer believing."

In a wide-ranging conversation that touched on monetary theory, proof of stake versus proof of work, Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs), and digital identity verification, Stornetta offered a nuanced perspective that defies easy categorization. He's neither a Bitcoin maximalist nor a crypto skeptic; instead, he sees the technology as an ongoing evolution, still in its early innings, with the most transformative applications yet to emerge.

## The Man Behind Bitcoin's Foundation

Scott Stornetta's path to blockchain wasn't driven by ambition to create digital money. Instead, he was addressing what he calls "the immutable record problem"—a challenge that became increasingly urgent as the world transitioned to digital record-keeping.

"We weren't focused on money so much as a broader issue of how do you create an immutable record and specifically how do you create an immutable record out of bits that are infinitely fungible?" Stornetta explained. "They almost seem like oil and vinegar. And yet since all records were going digital, we realized that there had to be some way to create reliable records out of easily changeable bits."

The core insight that Stornetta and Haber developed was deceptively simple yet profound: by grouping events into blocks, linking each block to the previous one, and distributing the chain across many participants, you could create a record that was fundamentally resistant to tampering. Since everyone was watching, no one could cheat.

### What They Invented vs. What Satoshi Added

When asked to clarify the distinction between his foundational work and Bitcoin's innovation, Stornetta drew a clear line:

"What Bitcoin adds—and again I'm a fan of what Satoshi did—is the incentive structure, the idea to focus strictly on money and a completely self-contained system for creating the currency as mining rewards. So that everyone is highly incentivized to pay attention to what's going on."

The key contributions from Stornetta and Haber that Bitcoin built upon included:

- **Block grouping** using Merkle trees for efficient verification
- **Chain linking** where each block references the previous block
- **Distributed consensus** making tampering practically impossible

What Satoshi added was the economic mechanism: a self-stabilizing system where computational work served as both the validation method and the incentive structure.

"I think what Satoshi did was a tremendous tour de force and I wouldn't want to take anything away from that," Stornetta said. "But in essence, Stuart and I think of him having built Bitcoin on the kind of early blockchain layer that we established."

## Bitcoin as the First Inning: The Evolution of Blockchain

When Stornetta first appeared on this show three and a half years ago, he used a memorable metaphor: Bitcoin represented the first inning of a much longer game. The conversation revisited this framework, asking where we stand now.

"I'd say at least the third inning, maybe fourth inning," Stornetta responded. "Maybe the rise of stablecoins have taken us from the third inning to the fourth inning."

### The Innings Framework

Stornetta outlined the progression of blockchain innovation:

**First Inning: Bitcoin** — The original breakthrough, establishing the foundational mechanism for a decentralized ledger without a central authority.

**Second Inning: Ethereum and Smart Contracts** — The Swiss Army knife of blockchain, introducing programmable logic that expanded possibilities beyond simple value transfer.

**Third Inning: Layer 2 Protocols and Bitcoin Evolution** — Innovations like ordinals that enable indexing and richer data structures within the Bitcoin network, along with scaling solutions built on top of the base layer.

**Fourth Inning: Stablecoins** — The emergence of cryptocurrencies pegged to stable assets (like the US dollar) that address Bitcoin's volatility while maintaining the benefits of blockchain technology.

"I think it's a terrific intermediate step for those that are looking for more stability," Stornetta said of stablecoins. "It's not because I'm suggesting that the endgame is devolving blockchain-based currencies, having them fall back to fiat currency foundation."

### The Long-Term Vision

Despite speculation and volatility, Stornetta expressed optimism about the technology's trajectory:

"We're not there yet because there's this almost ironic conjunction of asserting the truth of the situation—a shared, agreed space—and yet it's coupled with what is often times highly speculative activity. Now I can say for myself that I never envisioned the highly speculative activity, which shows you how unprophetic I am. But I think there's a good thing going on, and that is yes, there's still quite a bit of speculation that drives a lot of the trading and the holding, but gradually actual value-creating use cases are creeping into the system."

He pointed to stablecoins as evidence of genuine progress: "The evolution of stablecoins, for example, I think is a terrific example of actual value creation and progress that's being made."

## Proof of Work vs. Proof of Stake: A Fundamental Debate

Perhaps nowhere is Stornetta's contrarian perspective more evident than in his preference for proof of stake over Bitcoin's proof of work mechanism—a preference that might surprise those who know he's not a Bitcoin maximalist.

### The Self-Stabilizing Mechanism

When asked about whether advances in computational efficiency (including AI) could destabilize Bitcoin's block reward system, Stornetta offered a nuanced response:

"The power needed to produce a block is the race to be the first to compute the nonce that allows you to claim longest chain status of the block and therefore be issued some fraction of Bitcoin. That's a self-stabilizing mechanism, namely because there are multiple parties engaged, and if some energy reduction energy efficiency is achieved somewhere, it becomes available to all the parties that are competing to create that block."

His conclusion: "If more efficient compute mechanisms are discovered, will that destabilize things? I think the answer is no. Because those mechanisms are going to rapidly become broadly available and therefore whatever temporary advantage one miner gains over another, that advantage is going to rapidly get distributed and we get back to the equilibrium."

Yet despite acknowledging the elegance of proof of work, Stornetta prefers proof of stake—and he has a historical reason.

"Proof of stake is what we essentially proposed in our original papers," he revealed. "Now it doesn't go under that name, but the idea of randomly selecting from the population based on involvement in the community so that everyone didn't have to struggle to do the same thing, but that there was a randomizing process for deciding who processed the certification of the next block. That was our assumed starting point."

### Addressing the "Rich Get Richer" Critique

Critics of proof of stake often argue that the mechanism allows those with more tokens to earn more rewards, effectively making the rich richer. Stornetta pushed back on this framing:

"They can argue that proof of stake makes the rich richer. Okay. Let's take that. It says that the community that has the most invested in maintaining the system has the most influence in maintaining the system. I don't know that that directly translates into a rich get richer."

He drew an analogy: "It's no different in my opinion than the fact that companies that issue stock—the voting on company matters is not one person one vote. It's one share one vote. If you own more shares, you have more vote. I think that's fully proportionate."

The concern about wealth concentration, Stornetta suggested, is legitimate but not inherent to proof of stake: "If you want to have more shares, then get more shares. I'm still a little from Missouri on that. I don't think proof of stake leads directly to a rich gets richer."

### A Hybrid Approach

Stornetta proposed an intermediate solution: rather than choosing between pure proof of work and pure proof of stake, the system could incorporate elements of both:

"Proof of stake from my perspective is fundamentally saying, 'Let's not all fight at once.' We can choose a subset of the population to fight it out for this round, and the next round we'll choose a different subset. In that way you can reduce the energy needs. You can still play proof of work but within that subset and then move to a different subset."

He acknowledged the trade-offs: "You definitely want people to be incentivized to obtain additional tokens. And to the extent that you can align that with maintaining a system that allows for low-friction interaction, all to the good. So there's a lot of ways to skin a cat."

## The Bitcoin 21 Million Cap: Design Philosophy

When asked about Bitcoin's 21 million supply cap—perhaps its most defining characteristic—Stornetta traced the logic back to Satoshi's stated intent:

"I think he makes his intention clear on that point. He wanted an inflation-resistant currency and by limiting the money supply, the complaint against any fiat currency is that a government will eventually be tempted to start printing money to dig itself out of its financial woes, but with the long-term consequence of devaluing the currency, digging the hole deeper, if you will."

The irony Stornetta observed was significant: "What I think is again ironic is the fact that he was trying to create a stable currency—one not subject to inflationary pressures—and yet coming in through the back end is the whole speculative nature that Bitcoin has taken on, which has led to a kind of volatility that if I had to guess he hadn't anticipated."

## Bitcoin's Pseudonymity vs. True Cash

Stornetta made a crucial distinction that challenges common narratives about Bitcoin's purpose: "You did say Bitcoin is actually too transparent to be the true digital cash."

He elaborated: "The title of the white paper was Bitcoin, a peer-to-peer electronic cash system. It wasn't Bitcoin, a peer-to-peer electronic money system. And obviously cash is a subset of money, but more restrictive. Cash allows for completely anonymous transactions. Bitcoin is really just a ledger that records every transaction. And so ultimately there is no anonymity in Bitcoin."

The distinction matters: "There's a pseudonymous component because your physical world identity is not in a transparent way bound to your key pair. But the instant you cash in from the Bitcoin world out to the physical world, you are disclosing identity. So it's not a cash system. It's a money system supported by a ledger, which immediately means that the true anonymity of cash was given up on day one by making a ledger that everyone can view."

### Money's Three Functions

Stornetta then applied the economist's framework for what constitutes money:

- **Medium of exchange**: Bitcoin serves this function in some settings—particularly countries with hyperinflationary currencies like Zimbabwe
- **Unit of account**: Very few contracts are written using Bitcoin as the unit of account
- **Store of value**: This is where the confusion lies, according to Stornetta

"Store of value is inferring store of *stable* value," he clarified. "If I store this currency, a year from now I'll still be able to purchase effectively something close to that same basket of goods. That is not the thesis of people that are purchasing Bitcoin. Their thesis is I'll be able to buy *more* of that basket of goods. Bitcoin is still a largely speculative asset—a bet that the value will go up. That's not what store of value means. That's hope of increasing value."

## Central Bank Digital Currencies: The Asymmetry Problem

The conversation turned to CBDCs—a topic where Stornetta offered a sophisticated analysis that cut through common objections.

"I don't think it follows necessarily" that CBDCs benefit everyone, he said. Then he offered a thought experiment designed to challenge the reflexive opposition to centralized digital currencies:

"Let's imagine that David and Bonnie and Scott are themselves individually nation states. You have your currency issued by your CBDC and you have yours and I have mine. What's the downside of the fact that anyone that uses my CBDC, I know everything about their transactions?"

The answer, he suggested, is nothing—as long as reciprocal information sharing exists: "To trade with you, I'm going to also have to use yours, and then you're going to know everything about me. As long as there's a kind of parity, I think information loses its fang."

### The Real Concern: Information Asymmetry

Stornetta distinguished between two distinct issues:

1. **Disclosure of information**: The fact that a central authority can see transactions
2. **Asymmetric power**: The central authority knowing everything about individuals, while individuals know nothing about the authority

"The biggest concern that people express is that suddenly everything can be tracked by some centralized entity," Stornetta acknowledged. "But I think that question is often broken down in the wrong way and doesn't recognize how much of the anxiety we have about it is not so much the disclosing of information per se, but the asymmetric accumulation of power of one entity knowing lots about lots of people and the individual people not being in a favored position."

His prescription: "If you were to ask me, is a CBDC that means the government can know everything about me but I don't know reciprocally everything about the government—is that a good thing? No, probably not. But it's due to the asymmetry of information. It's not that the information disclosure is inherently bad. It's that it's asymmetrically held."

## The AI Threat: Seeing Is No Longer Believing

Moving to artificial intelligence, Stornetta revealed a personal connection to the field: his doctoral dissertation at Stanford was, in his words, "the first neural networks dissertation that Stanford approved"—though he quickly added that his contribution was "very modest, sort of a historical accident."

This long engagement with AI informs his current concerns: "The one I'd probably draw immediate attention to is deep fakes—AI-generated deep fakes. The growing fidelity of AI imitations that make seeing no longer believing. Inability to distinguish the synthetic from the natural or the real."

### The Instrument Flying Analogy

Stornetta drew an analogy to aviation: "When airplanes started out, there's no cockpit, there's no instruments. You go off the ground, you look around, you find a safe place to land. But as the capabilities of airplanes grew, we wanted to fly higher and faster, wanted to fly in stormy weather. What did that dictate a need for? Instrument-based flying. Because you get disoriented when you're up in the clouds or in the storm."

The parallel to AI is direct: "Seeing is no longer believing. Hearing is no longer believing. There's a lot of stormy weather ahead. But the solution is not to say well it's hopeless. The solution is say we need to learn to fly by instruments."

### The Concrete Threat: Real-World Example

Stornetta described a vivid case study: "A company called Arup Limited, a UK-based company that had extensive dealings in Hong Kong, the senior officers of a group got together on a Zoom call and made decisions about where funds should be transferred. Except all of the people on the call were fakes except the victim. The victim fought—he was interacting with senior management and following their instructions for wire transfers. But it was all synthetic."

This is the threat his current company, Shermark, aims to address.

## Shermark: Credential-Based Identity Verification

Shermark, the company Stornetta now leads alongside his original collaborator Dr. Stuart Haber, focuses on what he calls "positive assertion of identity rather than detection of deep fakes."

### How It Works

The credential system operates through a simple process:

1. A user has a credential stored on their smartphone
2. Another party looks up the user in a universal registry
3. They press a button that challenges the user's smartphone to respond in a way that proves only the legitimate credential holder can respond
4. The verification is complete, establishing confirmed identity

"It's simply a credential that lives on your smartphone," Stornetta explained. "What this allows us to do is meet virtually with the same confidence that we have face to face."

He returned to the aviation analogy: "You can still fly when it's a clear day at low speeds, there's no clouds, and you can just fly visually. That's what face-to-face meetings are. But would you also like to fly above the clouds, through storms, gain the additional power of flying fast and far? Well, then you're going to need instruments. You're going to need a credential."

### The Impact He Envisions

Stornetta expressed confidence in the long-term significance of this approach: "The credential-based identity will someday have a level of impact equivalent to blockchain-based record-keeping."

## The Future of Currency: Kilowatt Hours

Perhaps the most provocative idea in the conversation was Stornetta's vision for what could replace or supplement existing cryptocurrencies: using the kilowatt hour as a universal unit of currency.

"I think there's a very tempting opportunity for money to get grounded in something that is universally measurable and tradable and agreeable on a kind of new functional goal, which I would like to suggest would be the kilowatt hour," he proposed. "A kind of options and futures market on energy to be delivered at a given location at a given time."

His reasoning: "I think the kilowatt hour provides a very nice gradually deflationary currency that is universally applicable and can run on pretty decent rails. And that I think could provide direct inherent utility rather than substitutionary utility."
