In a recent roundtable discussion, Anthropic’s experts—spanning research, consumer applications, and enterprise solutions—gathered to unpack the evolving discipline of prompt engineering. The conversation revealed that while prompting might seem like simple conversation, it blends clear communication, systematic experimentation, and deep psychological intuition about how AI models “think.”

## What Is Prompt Engineering?

At its core, prompt engineering is about “trying to get the model to do things, trying to bring the most out of the model,” explained **Zack Witten**, a Prompt Engineer at Anthropic. “A lot of it is just clear communicating. I think at heart, talking to a model is a lot like talking to a person.”

But why call it *engineering*?  
“The engineering part comes from the trial and error,” Zack noted. Unlike human conversation, interacting with a model offers a “giant go back to square zero” button. This allows for controlled, repeatable experimentation—designing, testing, and refining instructions without interference.

**David Hershey**, who works closely with enterprise customers, added that prompt engineering often involves integrating prompts into larger systems. “It’s like way more complicated than just writing one prompt and you’re done.” This systems-thinking—factoring in data sources, latency, and scalability—elevates prompting into its own specialized domain.

## What Makes a Good Prompt Engineer?

**Amanda Askell**, who leads a Fine-tuning team at Anthropic, highlighted several key traits:

1. **Clear Communication** – The ability to articulate tasks and concepts precisely.
2. **Relentless Iteration** – “In a 15-minute span I’ll be sending hundreds of prompts to the model. It’s back and forth, back and forth.”
3. **Anticipating Edge Cases** – Good prompt engineers don’t just test typical scenarios. They ask: What happens with unusual inputs, empty data, or typos? “You have to think about your prompt and be like, ‘What are the cases where it’d be really unclear to me what I should do?’”

David emphasized the importance of understanding real-world usage: “Customers think about these really perfectly phrased things… In reality, it’s like they never used the shift key and every other word is a typo.”

Zack pointed out another critical habit: **reading model outputs closely**. “If you aren’t reading the model outputs, you might not even notice it’s making a mistake.” For example, simply adding “think step-by-step” doesn’t guarantee the model actually reasons stepwise—you have to verify.

## The “Theory of Mind” Challenge

A recurring theme was the difficulty of divorcing your own knowledge from what the model actually knows. “It’s so hard to untangle in your own brain all of the stuff that you know that Claude does not know and write it down,” Zack observed. A common pitfall is writing prompts that only make sense if you already understand the context.

Amanda shared a useful technique: “One of the first things I do with my initial prompt is I’ll give it the prompt and then I’ll be like, ‘I don’t want you to follow these instructions. I just want you to tell me the ways in which they’re unclear.’”

Surprisingly, you can even ask the model to debug itself. “If people see that the model makes a mistake, the thing that they don’t often do is just ask the model,” Amanda said. “You say to the model, ‘You got this wrong. Can you think about why?’ A lot of the time, the model just gets it right.”

## Knowing When to Trust the Model—and When to Stop

Amanda, known internally for extensively testing Claude in Slack, doesn’t take trust for granted. “I don’t trust the model ever, and then I just hammer on it.” She advocates for small, well-crafted test sets over large, noisy ones: “If you have a really well-constructed set of a few hundred prompts, that can be much more signal than thousands that aren’t as well-crafted.”

But how do you know if a task is *possible* with prompting?  
“I’m usually checking for whether the model kind of gets it,” Amanda said. “If the model just clearly can’t do something, I won’t grind on it for too long.”

Zack recalled a telling experiment where he tried to get Claude to play Pokémon Red via a Game Boy emulator. Despite a weekend of increasingly complex prompts—including overlaying grids on screens and generating ASCII maps—Claude could only progress from “no signal to some signal, but nowhere close to good enough.” His conclusion: “I could grind on this for four months, and the thing that would come out is another model. That’s a better use of my time.”

## The High Stakes of Prompting

The panel agreed that prompting can make or break an AI application. “The very best of prompting can make the difference between a failed and a successful experiment,” Amanda stressed. In research, a better prompt could mean the difference between top 1% and top 5% performance—potentially determining whether a project ships or not.

David echoed this from a product perspective: “It’s so easy to say, ‘Oh, we can’t ship this.’ And then you change the prompt around and suddenly it’s working.”

Yet they warned against chasing a “mythical, better prompt” indefinitely. The key is balancing rigorous iteration with knowing when diminishing returns set in—or when the task requires a more capable model.

## Final Insights

Prompt engineering sits at the intersection of writing, psychology, and systems design. It demands clarity, iteration, and a willingness to engage deeply with the model’s outputs. As AI models evolve, so too will the art of guiding them—but the fundamentals of clear communication and thoughtful experimentation will remain essential.

As Zack aptly summarized: “We’re in this paradigm where written text, like a nice essay that you wrote, is something that’s looked like the same thing as code. But it is true that now we write essays and treat them like code, and I think that’s actually correct.”

In the end, prompt engineering isn’t just about commanding an AI—it’s about collaborating with it.
