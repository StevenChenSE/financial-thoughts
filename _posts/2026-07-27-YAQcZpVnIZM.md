## A Disconnect Between Physical Reality and Financial Perception

The global oil market is currently experiencing a baffling anomaly: inventories are being drawn down at unprecedented rates, yet oil prices are declining. This counterintuitive trend has sparked widespread speculation among investors about potential market manipulation by entities such as the US Treasury. However, Mike Rothman, a veteran oil analyst and founder of Cornerstone Analytics, attributes this disconnect not to physical oversupply, but to a collapse in what he terms "financial demand" for oil. In a recent discussion, Rothman detailed how the market’s perception of a looming "super glut" clashes with the harsh reality of supply disruptions, particularly in the Persian Gulf.

## The Pre-Epic Fury Structural Bull Case

To understand the current crisis, one must look at the structural conditions that existed prior to the "Epic Fury" conflict. Rothman notes that since 2020, a divergent trend was unfolding: global oil demand was returning to normal growth rates, roughly half the pace of GDP, while non-OPEC supply failed to keep pace due to twelve years of underinvestment. This dynamic was steadily eroding OPEC spare capacity and drawing down inventories.

Contrary to the consensus view promoted by the International Energy Agency (IEA), which predicted the "death of oil demand," Rothman argues that oil exhibits perfect inelasticity. Historical data shows that even as oil prices rose 180% this century, demand grew by 40%. The IEA’s forecast relied on the assumption that limited non-OPEC supply growth would not matter because demand would stagnate—a premise that has proven false. Furthermore, the IEA’s estimates of spare capacity were significantly inflated; while they suggested over 4 million barrels per day (bpd) of spare capacity, Rothman’s analysis indicated a reality closer to 700,000–800,000 bpd, creating an extremely thin margin for error in a market consuming 107 million bpd.

## Epic Fury and the Unprecedented Supply Shock

The conflict known as "Epic Fury" triggered four major supply risks that materialized simultaneously: disrupted shipping, reduced Iranian production, attacks on neighboring countries' infrastructure, and interference with Persian Gulf exports. The result has been a historic loss of supply. Rothman calculates that approximately 1.5 billion barrels of "forfeited supply" have been lost to the system through the end of June. This figure has no precedent in oil history, surpassing the impacts of the Arab Oil Embargo, the Iran-Iraq War, or the invasion of Kuwait.

Despite these massive physical losses, the market initially behaved as if conditions had normalized. However, recent developments suggest a regression. With renewed attacks between the US and Iran, and threats of blockade by Yemen in the Red Sea, the movement of oil out of the Persian Gulf has diminished. Rothman estimates that exports are now back to May levels, with roughly 14–15 million bpd of exports effectively shut in across seven Gulf countries.

## The Geology of Disruption: Why Recovery Is Slow

A critical, often overlooked aspect of the supply crisis is the geological difficulty of restoring production after shutdowns. Rothman highlights the difference between shallow, high-pressure reservoirs in the western Persian Gulf (Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Iraq) and the thick, low-pressure columns found in Iran. When Iran shuts in production, it disrupts the wellbore pressure, making recovery difficult. Historically, after Iran’s production dropped in 1979, it took 16 years to reach 4 million bpd, and it has never exceeded that level since.

Similar concerns apply to Iraq and Kuwait, where production was shut in in a "disorderly manner" during the conflict. Restoring these fields may be more tedious and time-consuming than widely believed. This geological reality undermines the narrative of a quick return to pre-conflict supply levels.

## The Financial Market Disconnect

The core of the current price anomaly lies in the financialization of the oil market. Rothman points out that the ratio of futures trading volume to actual physical oil use has ballooned to approximately 60:1, compared to a normal range of 3:1 to 5:1. With roughly 6 billion barrels of oil traded daily via futures, options, and OTC contracts against a physical demand of 0.1 billion barrels, price discovery has shifted from the cash market to the futures market.

This excessive leverage creates volatility based on anxiety rather than physical reality. The current disconnect is the largest on record, surpassing the 2011 episode where fears of a Spanish debt default caused oil prices to fall $45 despite strong demand and flat inventories. Today, the market is pricing in a "super glut" that does not exist, ignoring the massive inventory draws. Rothman’s models suggest that, based on inventory levels alone, WTI should be trading around $98–$99 per barrel. The current lower prices reflect a collapse in financial demand, not physical oversupply.

## Global Refining and the Diesel Crisis

The supply crunch is also impacting the refining sector. Russia’s oil production is declining, and Ukraine’s attacks on Russian refineries—which account for 60% of the country’s capacity—are straining diesel supplies. The diesel crack spread, a measure of refining margins, is nearing all-time highs, signaling a desperate need for diesel fuel. Global crude runs have fallen, forcing refiners to drain product inventories to meet demand. This cannot continue indefinitely.

China has borne the brunt of this availability issue, throttling back its own oil usage and imports. While some speculate this indicates a collapsing Chinese economy, Rothman argues it is primarily a response to a lack of available barrels. With a 12-day shipping buffer from the Persian Gulf, China has exhausted its immediate supply and is drawing down strategic reserves. However, suppressing oil demand risks derailing the broader economy, suggesting strong pressure to resume imports once supply stabilizes.

## Outlook: A Bullish Reckoning

Rothman remains bullish on oil and energy equities, viewing the current market conditions as "middle innings" of a multi-year bull cycle. He notes that energy stocks have outperformed crude prices, a pattern that historically precedes a major bottom and a subsequent rally lasting at least three years. The divergence between physical supply deficits and financial market pricing cannot persist indefinitely.

The geopolitical situation remains volatile. Iran has demonstrated its ability to control shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, creating a mutually exclusive goal structure with the US. Rothman suggests that the conflict may escalate, potentially requiring significant military action to disable the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC). Regardless of the political outcome, the physical reality of lost supply and depleted inventories will eventually force a price correction. As Rothman concludes, the risk is disproportionately skewed to the upside, and the market is ignoring the fundamental truth: the world runs on oil, and there is currently not enough of it.
