**March 19, 2026** – Global markets are reeling from a perfect storm in the Middle East. Crude oil surged overnight, stock markets sold off sharply, and a potential challenge to the decades-old petrodollar system emerged, sending shockwaves through the financial world.

The trigger was a significant escalation. An Iranian missile strike hit Qatar’s Ras Laffan Industrial City, the world’s largest LNG export complex, damaging facilities responsible for roughly 20% of global LNG supply. European natural gas prices jumped 35% at the open.

In response, former President Donald Trump issued a stark threat on Truth Social, blaming Israel for provoking Iran's retaliation and warning that the U.S. would "massively blow up the entirety of the South Pars gas field" if Iran attacked Qatar again.

Meanwhile, a more profound, structural threat simmered beneath the immediate violence. The Strait of Hormuz, a critical chokepoint for global oil shipments, remains effectively closed, with traffic plummeting from over 100 ships daily to about 90 total since the war began on February 28th. CNN reported that Iran is considering a condition for reopening: oil must be traded in Chinese yuan, not U.S. dollars.

"This is the most direct challenge to the petrodollar system since the 1970s," said David, the show's host. "Oil priced in yuan at the world's most important chokepoint would weaken the dollar, erode American sanctions leverage, and rewire global finance."

Amid this turmoil, veteran energy analyst **Joseph Shakar**, President of Shaker Energy Research Services, joined the program to decipher the chaos and its alarming implications.

### The Unprecedented Oil Divergence

The crisis has violently distorted oil markets. Brent crude surged nearly 7% to above $113 a barrel, while West Texas Intermediate (WTI) hovered around $96. The roughly $17 spread between the two benchmarks is its widest in 11 years.

"What does that tell you about how the global oil supply chains are broken right now?" David asked.

Shakar explained the divergence: "WTI is, of course, the U.S. price... Brent, because a lot of the Middle Eastern crude and European crude is priced off Brent. There's lack of... supply of Brent-priced crude." He noted the spread is normally around $5, but the closure of strategic straits has created a severe shortage for markets reliant on Brent-priced oil.

While some Iranian-linked tankers are moving, Shakar highlighted a more alarming trend: "The IRGC is firing at both the cargo ships and at tankers... they're also firing at the Saudi refineries, Saudi facilities. That's really the one that's alarming the market." He emphasized that destroying infrastructure—pipelines, processing plants—creates a "massive expenditure and a lot of time to repair," which is why prices are spiking.

The damage is already exceeding previous peaks. "Prices of product because of the shortages in Europe and in Asia have gone over $200 a barrel," Shakar said. "The 147 [dollars per barrel], which was the peak in '08, has already been exceeded."

### A Protracted Inflation Shock

The host posed a critical question: if the war ended immediately, would prices revert just as quickly?

Shakar was pessimistic, citing broken logistics and the time needed to assess physical damage. "Once we get to the point where we know that major facilities have not been destroyed and can be easily repaired, then we're going to come down from these lofty levels, but maybe down into the 80s... I don't believe we're going to see those [pre-war lows] again."

He agreed with analyst Jeff Currie's assessment that the world has changed, disrupting not just oil but "gas, fertilizers, metals, petrochemicals. The list goes on and on."

This supply chain rupture has dire inflationary consequences. Shakar warned of a **5 to 8% global inflation problem** if the conflict persists into May and June. "The inflation data that we're seeing... by months from now, if the war has not ended, those numbers will go up," he said, noting the impact on fertilizers and, consequently, food prices during planting season.

The duration of this inflation spike hinges on the war's length and the extent of physical destruction. "If this drags on, every week that it drags on, add a couple bucks to WTI," Shakar stated. "If it drags on for a month or two, we could be $120, $130."

### Recession Risks and a Shifting Petrodollar

Could these prices trigger a recession? Shakar referenced a Wall Street Journal economist survey pointing to $138 per barrel as a potential tipping point. "Today I would think $125 to $130... you could be $138," he concurred, noting that such a level could precipitate a global recession.

The conversation then turned to the seismic report about Iran potentially demanding yuan for oil transit. "Do you think countries will fold and start trading in yuan to gain access?" David asked.

Shakar confirmed the trend is already growing in Asia. "Russia is now selling oil to Japan... in yuan. So, they do not want U.S. dollars... In Asia, the yuan is becoming more of a trading currency." However, he noted it remains absent from major financial centers like London and New York.

### Investment Opportunities and Hidden Crises

Amid the turmoil, Shakar sees opportunity, particularly in North American energy security. "The Canadian energy index is at a record high for the year," he said, highlighting that Canadian energy stocks are cheaper relative to their U.S. peers. He expressed optimism that a more centrist Canadian government could unlock the country's potential as the world's fourth-largest producer.

Yet, beneath the oil shock lurks a potentially greater danger: a burgeoning credit crisis. Shakar pointed to stress in private debt, private equity, and widening junk bond spreads. "We have a bigger credit crisis than we had in '08," he suggested. In such a scenario, even safe-haven assets like gold—which was down nearly 18% from its January highs—can sell off violently as investors face margin calls and are forced to sell their most liquid profitable assets.

"Gold's had such a great run, it doesn't, who says you can't have a bit of a correction?" Shakar remarked, though he acknowledged the long-term bullish case for precious metals given global debt levels.

### The Path Forward

The interview concluded on a note of profound uncertainty. The immediate crisis in the Strait of Hormuz, compounded by the threat to the Red Sea, has put 80% of Asia's oil supply at risk. The physical damage to energy infrastructure may take months to repair, and the financial architecture of the global oil trade faces its stiffest challenge in half a century.

As Joseph Shakar made clear, the world is not just grappling with a spike in oil prices, but with a fundamental rewiring of energy logistics, finance, and geopolitical power. The weeks ahead will determine whether this is a transient shock or the onset of a new and volatile era for the global economy.

*Joseph Shakar is the president of Shaker Energy Research Services and author of The Shakar Report on Substack. He is a 40-plus year veteran of the Canadian investment management industry and a former chairman of the Canadian Council of Financial Analysts.*
