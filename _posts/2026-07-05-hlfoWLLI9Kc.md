## Expert insights on capitalizing on the gold pullback and positioning for long-term gains

---

Gold has dropped below $4,000 per ounce, marking a significant correction from recent highs. For investors watching this pullback, Jordan from Mining Stock Monkey sees a compelling buying opportunity—and he's putting his money where his mouth is.

"I love buying opportunities," Jordan explained during a recent InvestingNews interview. "When the price of gold or any other metal or stock is at an all-time high, I'm often tentative or hesitant to buy. But I like to buy on pullbacks."

The current decline represents roughly a 25-27% pullback from gold's highs near $5,500. According to Jordan, this is entirely normal behavior for a gold bull market. "In previous bull markets since they took the price fix of gold away in early 1970s, we've seen multiple pullbacks of 25%, a pullback of 35%, even a pullback of 49% in the middle of a gold bull market."

That 49% pullback would bring gold to approximately $2,750—still well below current levels.

## The Macro Case for Gold

Jordan remains bullish on gold over the longer term, citing the dollar's continued devaluation as a primary driver. "When the dollar gets devalued, when its purchasing power goes down and every dollar you have buys less stuff, the price of gold should go up along with that devaluation."

He acknowledges there will be ups and downs along the way. "It's not going to be a straight line with the devaluation of the dollar, but as the purchasing power goes down, the value of gold goes up."

Gold has nearly tripled from approximately $2,000 per ounce at the beginning of 2024 and remains roughly double those levels—still significantly higher than two and a half years ago.

## Timing the Market: A Contrarian Approach

One of the most common questions investors ask is whether the bottom has been reached. Jordan's answer is characteristically pragmatic: he doesn't try to time it perfectly.

"I typically when I like something, I buy all the way down. It goes down, I buy more. It goes down, I keep buying," he said. "I never time it perfectly, but I usually get some purchases near the bottom."

His approach uses sentiment as a guide. "When lots of people are bearish, when sentiment is really bad, when the multiples that publicly traded companies in the gold sector are getting in the stock market—well then when the multiples shrink, that's a pretty good sign that sentiment is worse."

He also watches the comments section. "When you see a lot of people commenting saying 'this market is over, this gold bull market is done, it's going to keep going lower,' that I'm looking at that as a buying opportunity. So, basically the opposite of what people are saying in the comments."

For those with different circumstances—such as retirees with a lump sum versus active earners with regular income—the strategy may differ. "If I had a big lump sum of money today, I would deploy most of it right now instead of trying to buy a little bit at a time because I see outstanding prices."

## Where Jordan Is Deploying Capital

Jordan is focusing heavily on gold and silver royalty companies, viewing them as a relatively safer way to gain exposure.

"Elemental Royalty has been a pretty good buying opportunity. When it's around $15 a share, I really like Elemental," he noted. He's also been "betting heavily" on Royal Gold, which he continues to accumulate despite having bought it six months ago when prices were higher.

"I see a lot of value in the stock. I think it's significantly undervalued, but I don't know when it's going to get closer to fair value. I just know that it's going to happen eventually—or at least I'm highly confident that it's going to happen."

Jordan acknowledges that while predicting direction is easier, timing remains elusive. "I don't really care if it happens today or in a couple of years because I mean actually I'd prefer that it take longer because then I have more of an opportunity to buy more shares."

## Quality Over Value: The Buffett Lesson

As valuations have compressed, Jordan has shifted toward prioritizing quality. He references Warren Buffett's evolution from buying "cigar butts"—cheap companies with more cash than market cap—to focusing on great companies at fair prices.

"The longer I've been doing this, the more I get into like when I have the opportunity to buy higher quality, I tend to do it. I think it's generally a better idea to buy the higher quality company when it's trading at a fair price."

Currently, major royalty and streaming companies are trading at reasonable valuations rather than the three to four times NAV premiums seen a few years ago. "Right now we're not there, and you can buy these really high quality companies for a fair price."

## What to Watch in Quarterly Reports

With Q2 results approaching, Jordan will be watching costs rather than revenue. "We basically know how much revenue they're going to bring in because you basically know how many ounces they're going to produce and then the approximate average gold price during the quarter. That's pretty easy to gauge. But what we don't know is costs and what kind of inflationary pressures are we seeing."

Royalty structures add another layer of complexity. "Just about every gold mine has some sort of royalty component on it. So, as the gold price goes up, the royalties the companies pay go up too. For example, if a mine has a 10% royalty on it and the gold price goes down $1,000, their cost just went down $100."

Energy costs are also in focus. "The oil price was pretty high for much of this quarter, and those that weren't hedged and use a lot of diesel fuel—they'll probably see some cost pressures. Now that has changed very rapidly over the last few weeks and the oil prices plummeted. So, the energy costs for these miners will likely be a lot lower in quarter 3."

## The Copper Question

Jordan remains long-term bullish on copper but is cautious due to recession risks. "Copper gets killed in a recession, and it's been almost 20 years since we had the last real recession."

He prefers copper royalty exposure over producers. "Elemental Royalty has a pretty big copper royalty portfolio, but that's a much safer way to own copper because they have 100% margins on most of their copper assets."

The math illustrates the difference: if copper drops from $6 to $4 per pound, royalty companies lose 33% of revenue, but copper producers might lose all their margins entirely.

For pure copper royalty plays, Jordan also mentions Altius Minerals, though it's more diversified with only about 20% copper exposure compared to roughly a third for Elemental.

## Silver: A Different Beast

After silver's dramatic spike to $125 per ounce and subsequent decline to around $58, Jordan is now looking for opportunities. However, finding quality silver investments is challenging.

"Pure play silver companies is tough, and what's also tough is finding silver companies that actually make money," he explained. "If you go look back at their cash flow per share over 20 years, they lose money most of those years. And today they're valued at 10 billion."

Most silver mining is a byproduct of copper, lead, zinc, and gold mines. "Silver's unique in that a huge spike in the silver price doesn't necessarily create much more supply in terms of mine supply."

Political risk is significant, as quality silver deposits are concentrated in Russia, Mexico, and Peru. "To get a quality silver deposit, you usually have to go to those countries. Peru changes their president every year. Mexico is not great—they had a socialist in power and they just elected another socialist, and the last one hated mining and this one hates mining."

Jordan prefers streaming companies like Wheaton Precious Metals, which can achieve 80% margins on silver.

## The Final Word: Buy Low, Sell High

For investors wondering whether to act on current lower prices, Jordan's advice is straightforward:

"Remember rule number one of investing is buy low, sell high. This should be the first thing you ever learn as a kid about investing. So when you see lower prices, you should be thinking buy, buy low—because to buy low and sell high, you have to buy when sentiment is bad, when everybody around you is saying that the prices are only going to keep going lower."

"So when you see much lower prices, it's usually a pretty good time to buy."
