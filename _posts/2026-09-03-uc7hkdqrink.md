## Introduction

The US 10-year Treasury yield recently touched 4.81%, its highest level since November 2023, against a backdrop of escalating geopolitical tensions and rising oil prices. As Brent crude climbed above $96 and WTI surpassed $91, economists are sounding the alarm on the direction of US interest rates. Professor Steve Hanke, an applied economist at Johns Hopkins University, argues that the bond market is entering a "red zone," with a high probability of another 50 basis point increase in long-term yields.

## The Monetary Driver of Inflation

Hanke attributes the primary driver of rising yields not to geopolitical shocks, but to monetary policy. He highlights that the M4 measure of money supply, released by the Center for Financial Stability, is currently growing at 7.9% per year, a significant acceleration from 5.4% the previous year.

According to Hanke, this rapid expansion of the money supply leads to inflation with a 12-to-24-month lag, which in turn forces bond yields higher. He notes that the current 7.9% growth rate significantly exceeds his "golden growth rate" of 6%, which is the level consistent with a 2% inflation target. Hanke dismisses the "cost-push" theory of inflation—often cited regarding oil prices—as a secondary factor, arguing that without a rapid growth in the money supply, relative price changes in commodities cannot sustain overall inflation.

## Geopolitical Escalation and Oil Prices

The current economic climate is further complicated by the escalating conflict between the US and Iran. Following US strikes on Iran's southern coast near the Strait of Hormuz, Iran retaliated against US military bases in Bahrain, Jordan, Kuwait, and Iraq. This exchange, described as the heaviest since July, has resulted in at least 19 Iranian casualties and intensified fears regarding the security of the Strait of Hormuz.

Hanke points out that while the market perceives the rising oil prices as a driver of inflation, the reality is more nuanced. He argues that the US is strategically losing ground in the region, noting that US troops have effectively retreated from the Gulf to bases in Jordan. Furthermore, Hanke suggests that the Strait of Hormuz is functionally closed, contradicting administration claims that it remains open, a reality that is reflected in the 30% spike in oil prices since July.

## The Impact on the US Economy

The implications of a 5% 10-year yield are severe for the American consumer. Hanke explains that the 10-year Treasury yield serves as the benchmark for virtually all consumer debt, including mortgage rates, credit card interest, and auto loans.

*   **Housing Market:** Mortgage rates are already at their highest levels since the 2008 financial crisis. Hanke warns that further increases will continue to stifle the housing market, which is already sluggish.
*   **Consumer Costs:** A rate hike would directly increase the cost of credit, slowing consumption.
*   **Political Impact:** Hanke notes that the rising cost of diesel (up 50% since the war began) and fertilizer is creating significant economic pain for truckers and farmers, a factor that could impact the midterm elections even in traditionally Republican states.

## Fed Policy and the Jackson Hole Signal

The Federal Reserve, under Chairman Kevin Warsh, faces a difficult mandate. At the Jackson Hole Symposium, Warsh signaled a hawkish stance, stating that the responsibility for 65 months of elevated inflation rests with the central bank. Notably, Warsh mentioned the "money supply" for the first time in years, a move Hanke interprets as a potential return to "monetarism."

Hanke predicts that the Fed will likely raise the Fed funds rate at the next FOMC meeting, estimating an 80% probability of a hike compared to the 64% implied by the CME FedWatch tool. He argues that the Fed must tighten monetary policy to slow the money supply growth from 7.9% to the 6% target. However, Hanke notes that the bond term premium has remained stable, suggesting that the market has not yet fully priced in the risks of the US fiscal deficit and the weaponization of the dollar.

## The Venezuela Oil Deal

The discussion also touched on the recently signed oil deal between the US and Venezuela. The Trump administration described it as the "biggest oil deal in the country's modern history," aiming to increase Venezuelan production to 1.5 million barrels per day.

Hanke, who is currently advising the Venezuelan government on its economic crisis, views the deal with skepticism. He argues that the agreement was signed under duress and is likely illegitimate. From a technical standpoint, Hanke points out that Venezuela's state-owned oil company, PDVSA, has a depletion rate of only 0.124% per year. This means it would take 558 years to deplete half of its proven reserves, rendering the present value of those reserves nearly zero. Hanke advocates for the privatization of these resources through open auctions rather than secret bilateral deals, and he proposes that Venezuela should dollarize its currency to combat its current 380% annual inflation rate.

## Conclusion

Steve Hanke’s analysis suggests that the US is facing a "red zone" in the bond market driven by a combination of rapid money supply growth, geopolitical instability, and a widening fiscal deficit. With the 10-year yield approaching 5%, the impact on consumer debt and the housing market will be profound. While the Fed moves toward a hawkish stance, the underlying monetary conditions suggest that inflation and yields will remain elevated for the foreseeable future.
