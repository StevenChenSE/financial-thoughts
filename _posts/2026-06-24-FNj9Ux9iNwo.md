## Energy Expert Dismantles the Narrative Around Strait of Hormuz and Global Oil Reserves

Former President Donald Trump's claim that global oil reserves would run out in just four weeks—made during the G7 Summit in France while discussing the memorandum of understanding with Iran—has been thoroughly debunked by energy expert Doomberg.

Speaking on a recent episode, Doomberg, head writer of the Doomberg Substack, addressed Trump's assertion that "there are some reserves all over the world and we would really run out and there will be a time when you wouldn't be able to get it." The expert's response was unequivocal: "Absolutely not."

## Reading Between the Lines: Trump's Strategic Communication

According to Doomberg, understanding Trump's statement requires examining the context surrounding it. The former president had just signed the MOU with Iran and secured a 60-day ceasefire, facing significant pressure from various lobbying interests in Washington.

"I would characterize that as management of the narrative around the MOU more than reflecting the physical reality of oil inventories around the world," Doomberg explained. Rather than representing genuine concern about imminent shortages, the four-week timeline appears designed to justify the Iran agreement to critics.

Trump likely directed this message toward certain allies who were "ringing and talking his ear off about the need to reopen Hormuz," the expert noted.

## The Strait of Hormuz: Separating Fact from Fiction

When asked about conflicting reports regarding the Strait of Hormuz—with Iran claiming it's closed while the United States maintains it's open—Doomberg offered a pragmatic approach: let the market be your guide.

"WTI today trading at 72 or 73 or wherever it is, that kind of tells us that the market believes the Strait is effectively open," Doomberg observed. The current oil price sits remarkably close to pre-war levels, "confounding to many people" but revealing about actual conditions on the ground.

The expert emphasized that sophisticated oil traders possess access to "impressive modern array of intelligence" that the general public lacks, and the market's behavior reflects this privileged information.

## Why the Crisis Never Materialized: Three Key Factors

Despite widespread fears of catastrophic oil shortages when conflict loomed, the market navigated the crisis "quietly compared to what everyone assumed would happen." Doomberg identified three critical reasons:

**China's Strategic Stockpiling**: "The Chinese have clearly been stockpiling an enormous amount of oil under the radar," Doomberg stated. Beijing offset approximately four million barrels per day of the five to six million barrels lost due to the strait disruption.

**Hydrocarbon Flexibility**: China invested heavily in what Doomberg calls "hydrocarbon fungibility"—the ability to switch between coal, ethane, naphtha, and other energy sources on a scale few thought possible.

**Shadow Operations**: The global oil market, more heavily sanctioned than any other commodity market, developed sophisticated methods for moving oil despite restrictions: "shadow fleets, sanctioned vessels, all kinds of middlemen and interesting characters."

## Debunking Jeff Currie's July Shortage Prediction

Veteran market strategist Jeff Currie from Carlyle Group had predicted that the US, Asia, and Europe could face shortages by July, with "Europe hitting tank bottoms." Doomberg offered a measured assessment: partially agree with the first part regarding Europe, find the second "plausible," but firmly reject the third.

"I disagree with for the reasons I just said," Doomberg stated, referencing North America's unique energy position.

The expert explained that many analysts make a critical error: "they don't include Canada." Canada produces 5.5 million barrels per day of oil, "most of which can only come to the US" due to pipeline constraints to British Columbia. "When you draw a circle around North America, which is how the oil market actually operates, the US and Canada combined are vast excess producers."

## The Geographic Asymmetry of Energy Vulnerability

Not all regions face equal risk from strait disruptions. Doomberg identified a "wild chasm" between different areas:

**Fortress North America**: The US has refining capacity exceeding domestic needs, a large strategic reserve, and Canada as a captive supplier. The Trump administration "used all of those assets to put a lid on the price of oil for political reasons, totally predictable, totally understandable, the correct thing to have done."

**Island Nations at Risk**: Countries entirely dependent on tanker deliveries face genuine vulnerability—Australia, New Zealand, Philippines, Taiwan, and Japan. These regions lack pipeline alternatives and must rely on maritime deliveries to replenish supplies.

**Europe's Mixed Position**: Despite having "plenty of pipeline capacity and lots of ports and long-standing relationships with the Middle East," Europe still faces challenges that Doomberg's analysis identified as concerning, particularly regarding jet fuel destocking.

## The Real Story Behind Cushing

Images of Cushing, Oklahoma—the crucial WTI pricing hub—circulated widely as supposed evidence of American vulnerability. Doomberg dismissed this as "misinformation for those that aren't as familiar with the mechanics of North American oil trade."

"Cushing is a relatively insignificant tank farm," the expert clarified. "Yes, it is used for pricing WTI," but physical supplies remained secure throughout the crisis.

## Key Takeaways

Trump's four-week oil depletion claim does not reflect physical reality. The global oil market, despite surface-level chaos and conflicting narratives, proved remarkably resilient. China absorbed the largest supply shock through strategic reserves and energy flexibility, while North America remained a secure fortress due to Canadian production and existing infrastructure.

Perhaps most significantly, Doomberg concluded that "the Strait of Hormuz is not even all that important anymore"—a statement that, if accurate, fundamentally reshapes how we should view future Middle East energy tensions.

The lesson, according to Doomberg: in times of uncertainty, follow the market rather than Twitter headlines. "Twitter's useful for inbound information, you have to filter it very carefully," he advised. "Twitter is not real" has emerged as one of the key meta-conclusions from navigating this crisis.
