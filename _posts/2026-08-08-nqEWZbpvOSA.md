## A Correction Within a Bull Market—Not the End of the Rally

Gold has held above $4,000 an ounce for months, even as central banks keep buying, Middle East tensions linger, and markets worry the Federal Reserve might tighten again. Yet instead of surging, gold has moved sideways while the S&P 500 has pushed to new highs and long-end Treasury yields have climbed.

In a recent interview at the Vancouver Resource Investment Conference, Daryl Thomas of VRIC Media spoke with Brian Lundine of Jefferson Financial and the New Orleans Investment Conference about what is behind this pause—and what it signals for gold, silver, miners, and broader markets.

### Why Gold Is Sideways Right Now

Lundine identifies two near-term headwinds that are temporarily holding gold back:

- Fear of Fed tightening: Markets are pricing in the possibility of rate hikes, even though Lundine believes they are unlikely.
- War-driven inflation via oil: Middle East conflict is lifting energy prices, which complicates Fed policy and keeps investors cautious.

He argues both factors are temporary. The war’s impact on oil and inflation will have to subside or be resolved before the U.S. midterm elections, and the Fed, in his view, cannot truly hike without spiraling debt-service costs.

Superimposed on those macro forces is normal summertime seasonality in gold. Lundine sees gold as being in a bottoming process, with seasonality typically resolving by mid-August. In his view, this creates a closing window of opportunity before what should be a strong fall rally.

### Lessons from Past Gold Bull Markets

This kind of correction is normal in a long bull market:

- Over the 2000–2011 bull market, gold rose roughly 7–8x, and mining stocks (e.g., the XAU index) outperformed even more.
- Large drawdowns, including 25–30% pullbacks, are typical.
- Bull markets test convictions and shake out weak hands.

For long-term investors, the key is to focus on macro drivers—debt, deficits, currency debasement, and monetary policy—and either:

- Buy and hold through volatility, or
- Trim froth at tops, build cash, and redeploy during corrections.

### Why the Fed Is Unlikely to Hike—and May Ease Again

Several points support Lundine’s view that rate hikes are improbable:

- Rising debt-service costs: Higher rates would make U.S. debt servicing unsustainable.
- Inflation source: Current inflation is largely from supply shocks in oil and energy, not an overheated economy. Higher oil prices already throttle demand; rate hikes wouldn’t fix that and could worsen it.
- Balance sheet expansion: The Fed is quietly increasing its balance sheet again, similar to what it did in 2019 before the pandemic—though not yet calling it quantitative easing.
- Political optics: Lundine suggests current hawkish rhetoric from Fed Chair Jerome Powell (referred to as “Walsh” in the transcript) is partly theater, with tacit approval from the White House, designed to manage market expectations rather than signal a true tightening path.

He also notes that the fundamental problem of the Fed—centralized setting of the price of money by a group of economists rather than by markets—will never be fixed as long as the Fed exists.

### Rising Yields: Not Hawkish Policy, But Debt Reality

Long-end Treasury yields have climbed sharply, which on the surface looks like a headwind for gold:

- Positive real yields (e.g., 4–5% on 10- or 30-year Treasuries versus ~3% CPI) traditionally pressure gold.
- However, Lundine disputes the official inflation figures and argues the real story behind rising yields is different.

He believes higher yields reflect:

- Market concerns about the ability to service massive debts in current-dollar terms.
- Expectations that the dollar will need to depreciate significantly over time to make those obligations manageable.

This dynamic is not unique to the U.S.; long-end yields are rising across developed economies after 45 years of ever-easier money and growing debt. Rising yields now signal fiscal stress and future currency depreciation, not a pure hawkish Fed stance.

### The AI Bubble and Risk of a Broad Market Correction

While gold consolidates, major equity indices—the S&P 500, Dow, and NASDAQ—are near or at all-time highs, driven largely by an AI boom. Lundine’s take:

- Valuations in AI-related stocks have exceeded levels seen before the 2000 tech wreck.
- The market is top-heavy and leveraged; a correction or liquidity event is possible if the AI rally “trips over itself.”
- Potential triggers include:
  - An escalation in the Iran conflict.
  - Stress in Japan’s markets and FX dynamics, with Treasury and Fed intervention to stabilize the yen and prevent cascading Treasury sales.
  - A blow-up in overleveraged funds.

If equities correct sharply, it would initially pressure gold, silver, and miners—but would also create a major buying opportunity. Lundine doesn’t think investors should wait for that, as current levels already offer attractive entry points.

### Central Banks: Buying the Dip, Supporting the Floor

Central banks continue to accumulate gold:

- China has increased purchases during this price dip, effectively “buying the dip” while Western investors create the volatility.
- Most central banks are dollar-cost averaging: allocating a fixed amount of capital over time. When prices fall, they buy more tonnage; when prices rise, they buy less.
- Central bank buying no longer drives the big rallies—Western investment does—but it provides a firm floor, likely around the $4,000 level.

This structural demand supports gold even as sentiment and flows from Western traders create peaks and valleys.

### Where Gold Could Go From Here

Lundine avoids precise price-and-time predictions but offers ranges:

- Near term: He leans toward a year-end target around $4,600–$4,700, closer to Deutsche Bank’s view.
- Cycle top: Based on historical bull markets where gold rises 6–8x from lows, he sees $6,000–$8,000 as a reasonable long-term target.
- Upside scenario: If there is a monetary reset or gold is used to address the global debt situation, prices could go considerably higher.

He also stresses that a multi-year, steady rise to $6,000–$8,000+ would be healthier than a rapid spike. A longer bull run gives investors more time to benefit from leveraged plays like mining equities and reduces the risk of the rally burning itself out.

The old floors of $1,500, $2,000, or $2,500 are behind us; Lundine sees $4,000–$4,500 as the new support zone.

### Why Silver May Outperform Gold

Lundine is bullish on silver for several reasons:

- Historical pattern: Silver has consistently outperformed gold in bull markets and will likely do so again.
- Industrial demand shock: After years of supply deficits, above-ground stocks are depleted. New mined silver will increasingly be consumed by industry—solar, electronics, and other technologies.
- Industrial vs monetary bidding: Early this year, industrial buyers were forced to compete with monetary buyers, paying over $100/oz because they couldn’t substitute or innovate around silver.
- Inelastic industrial demand: Unlike past cycles, industry demand doesn’t easily drop when prices rise, while monetary demand increases as prices climb.

This creates a more powerful setup for silver than in previous bull markets. However, silver is more volatile:

- It outperforms gold on the way up but also falls harder on the way down.
- It is more of a speculative instrument than a pure store of value, though it still preserves value over the long term.

### Silver’s Physical Market Bottlenecks

During silver’s rapid move to $120 earlier this year, many holders struggled to sell:

- Large bid-ask spreads appeared.
- Refineries and dealers faced logistical constraints.
- Large physical sales traded well below spot.

Lundine attributes this to the speed of the price move and unprepared infrastructure. If silver rises quickly again, similar friction could occur. However, the memory of $120 may make holders “stickier,” reducing sell pressure as prices climb.

A slower, steadier advance would give the market time to adjust and support more orderly flows.

### What’s Happening With Gold and Silver Miners

Despite strong cash flows, miners have sold off significantly. Lundine sees this as attractive:

- Producers: Many are “printing money hand over fist.” As they pay down debt and run buybacks and dividends, they will eventually need to reinvest in growth.
- Mid-tier and majors: Aggregation is underway; fewer pure majors exist, and new ones are likely to emerge.
- Explorers and developers: Well-funded, drilling aggressively, and seeing better success as they revisit targets they’ve understood for years.
- Future M&A: Once balance sheets are stronger, majors will start buying juniors again, likely sparking bidding wars for top assets.

On major producer earnings (e.g., Newmont), Lundine notes:

- Earnings dipped slightly as prices softened.
- But rising all-in sustaining costs (AISC) can be positive at these gold prices: miners are processing more ore, including lower-grade material, to maximize production.
- Shareholders should welcome this: higher output at rich margins beats hoarding high-grade ore.

### Key Takeaways

- Gold’s pause near $4,000 is a correction within a bull market, driven by temporary fears of Fed tightening, war-related inflation, and summertime seasonality.
- Rising long-end yields reflect global debt stress and expected dollar depreciation, not a pure hawkish Fed path.
- The AI-driven equity rally is stretched and vulnerable; a correction would pressure metals temporarily but create a major buying opportunity.
