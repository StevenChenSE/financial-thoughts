The race to secure critical minerals has become the defining geopolitical contest of the 21st century—and by most accounts, the West is losing. China controls between 80% and 99% of global production for a suite of elements essential to everything from smartphones to precision-guided missiles. A single embargo could cripple Western military capabilities and halt the technological advancement that underpins modern economies.

In this comprehensive discussion, Thomas Nrosski, portfolio manager at the Amethyst Terodan Critical Materials Fund and author of *Mineral War: China's Quest for Weapons of Mineral Destruction*, offers a sobering assessment of the West's vulnerability and the steps necessary to restore strategic independence.

---

## China's Dominance: More Than Just Rare Earths

The conventional narrative frames China's critical mineral advantage as a legacy of rare earth processing. The reality is far more extensive and more troubling.

> "China accounts for the majority of the world's smelting of critical materials, including copper," Nrosski explains. "In the refining and smelting space, China is so dominant that some of the Chinese operators can operate without TCRC—or even at negative ones. That's a big concern for that market."

China has not merely dominated rare earths but systematically expanded its control across the value chain. In Shandong Province, new copper smelters continue to come online—a development that seems counterintuitive to global demand but makes strategic sense when viewed through Beijing's lens.

### The Critical Minerals at Stake

Nrosski identifies the materials where China's grip is most pronounced:

| **Mineral** | **China's Market Share** | **Strategic Importance** |
|-------------|--------------------------|-------------------------|
| Gallium | ~99% | Semiconductors, optoelectronics |
| Germanium | ~80% | Infrared optics, solar panels, defense applications |
| Graphite | ~80% | Battery anodes, industrial uses |
| Tungsten | ~82% | High-performance metals, munitions |
| Bismuth | ~50% | Pharmaceuticals, alloys |
| Antimony | ~50% | Flame retardants, munitions |
| Indium | ~50% | Display screens, semiconductors |

> "These are fairly small markets," Nrosski notes, "and so small technological changes can completely transform the supply-demand relation."

### The Obstacle to Substitution

A critical insight emerges around the paradox of constrained supply: when a mineral is too scarce, downstream technologies never develop to utilize it at scale. Nrosski cites niobium as a case study.

Niobium production has historically been limited to three mines—two in Brazil, one in Canada. This constraint has prevented broader adoption beyond steel alloys and superalloys. Had supply been more abundant, niobium might have emerged as a viable anode material for batteries, potentially substituting for graphite.

> "If there are more mines of niobium around the world, it's possible that this material would be used as a substitution for others which are extremely constrained. These technologies exist. Toshiba worked on this many years ago. What just happens is there's just not enough production."

## The Export Ban Situation: Suspension, Not Lifting

A crucial clarification emerges regarding China's restrictions on critical mineral exports. In late 2024, China banned exports of gallium, germanium, and antimony—restrictions that were suspended for 12 months as part of trade de-escalation efforts.

> "The ban has not been lifted," Nrosski emphasizes. "It has been suspended for 12 months until November. So the situation—this is a crisis as it was before October."

This distinction matters enormously. A suspension suggests these controls could be reinstated at any point, leaving Western industries perpetually vulnerable to supply disruption.

---

## What the West Gets Wrong: Processing Over Mining

The conventional Western response to critical mineral dependency has focused heavily on exploration and mining projects. Nrosski argues this approach misses the point entirely.

> "It's not sufficient to back explorers or developers in Brazil or Africa or somewhere because at the end of the day they have to sell it somewhere."

The critical bottleneck is not mineral extraction but processing and refining. Without domestic smelting capabilities, mined materials still flow to China for refinement, perpetuating dependency.

### The Korea Zinc Deal: A Model for the Future

The recognition of this problem is beginning to manifest in policy. Nrosski points to the Korea Zinc deal in Tennessee as an example of the right approach.

> "Korea Zinc will produce not just zinc and other base metals but also a whole range of other byproducts. You can have indium or germanium out of zinc, gallium out of bauxite, bismuth out of copper—these are often those byproducts that are absolutely critical, but you need to have a full-service smelter for that."

This represents a recognition that the West cannot simply extract minerals; it must rebuild the entire middle section of the value chain.

## The Path Forward: A Multi-Step Strategy

Nrosski outlines a comprehensive framework for restoring Western critical mineral independence. The solution requires three interconnected policy pillars.

### 1. Price Floor Protection Through Tariffs

> "In order to incentivize investment within the broader West and especially in the United States, you really have to establish a different price structure because we're overdependent on the so-called Chinese price, for which the demand curve is much more elastic in China—given the overcapacity, overproduction—than it is in the West."

China's dominance allows it to subsidize production, creating prices that Western producers cannot match. Protective tariffs can establish a price floor that makes domestic production economically viable.

### 2. Downstream Subsidies

Revenue generated from tariffs should fund subsidies that incentivize manufacturers to diversify away from Chinese supply chains. This addresses the reality that Western manufacturers have spent decades optimizing for Chinese inputs.

> "You use the revenue generated by those tariffs to provide subsidies—and that is possible if you build three things: specific incentives for the prices, not for the operating margin, but for the capital intensity that is necessary to put back a facility at a specific node of the value chain of a given element."

### 3. Capital Market Reform

> "The second thing that needs to be done is to reduce the cost of capital for these investors, both on the equity side and on the debt side, and that could be done by tweaking the IRC codes accordingly."

Currently, mining projects in the United States struggle to access capital. The solution requires both tax incentives and permitting reform—a topic Nrosski addresses directly.

## The Permitting Problem: Why America Can't Build Mines

Since 1960, permitting a new mine in the United States has become increasingly difficult. In the first 23 years of this century, only three new mines received full permits.

> "It's not just a question of capital allocation. I think the capital patience for such long timelines—going into a decade and a half or longer—is just simply not there. And so there was no political pressure because there was no capital interest for something like this."

Nrosski traces part of this problem to the early 1980s, when the Nuclear Regulatory Commission classified rare earth ore containing even trace radioactivity as Class 7 material.

> "This boosted the costs for operating these mines and therefore basically pushed this industry out of the country—and predictably into China at that time."

The solution requires both permitting reform and a recognition that mining can be conducted responsibly. The environmental movement's opposition has created regulatory barriers that now threaten national security.

## The OPEC Comparison: Is This Our 1973 Moment?

When the United States faced the OPEC oil embargo in 1973, it responded with strategic petroleum reserves and eventually the fracking revolution that made America the world's largest oil producer. Could critical minerals represent a similar inflection point?

Nrosski is measured in his optimism.

> "Fracking was such an amazing breakthrough that we had a capital market system that fit the requirements of this relatively small footprint, very flexible operations. We don't have that flexibility in mining because of just the nature of mineral exploration, development, and production."

However, he draws a parallel to the geopolitical significance.

> "When I look at the levers that rare earth magnets have on our electrical grid and generally anything electric—anything that produces some electricity flow—probably affects about 47-48% of any of that. Then the leverage is similar."

The 1974 breakthrough was not just domestic production but the petrodollar system—Saudi Arabia agreeing to price oil in dollars, creating perpetual global demand for the currency. Nrosski notes the emergence of similar arrangements with cobalt-rich nations in the DRC, though the dynamics are more complex with many more players.

## Military Vulnerability: The Numbers That Should Worry Everyone

The conversation turns to a CNN report from April 2025 detailing the rate at which the U.S. military has depleted its munitions stockpiles:

> "Over the last seven weeks of the war, the U.S. military has expended at least 45% of its stockpile of precision strike missiles, at least half of its inventory of THAAD missiles, and nearly 50% of its stockpile of Patriot air defense interceptive missiles."

Nrosski provides the more granular picture from his analysis:

| **Munition Type** | **Inventory Depleted** |
|-------------------|------------------------|
| Tomahawk missiles | 27% |
| JSMs | 23% |
| PrSMs | 78% |
| SM-3 | 61% |
| SM-6 | 32% |
| THAAD | 81% |
| Patriot | 61% |

> "CIS I think calculated that we need between four, five, sometimes six years for some of these to rebuild those inventories."

### Is This a Minerals Problem or a Production Problem?

Nrosski identifies both dimensions.

> "It's going to be sourcing and production. Production has to be boosted by a long stretch. Luckily, there are some other products our allies built—whether in Germany, Japan, Taiwan, or elsewhere. But that was a particularly wasteful campaign from this perspective."

The underlying question is whether sufficient gadolinium exists for electromagnetic warfare protection systems in new Tomahawk production—and whether Japan will receive the 400 Tomahawks it has ordered for next year.

## The Drone Revolution: A Missed Wake-Up Call

Beyond traditional munitions, Nrosski identifies a more fundamental strategic failure: the West's slow response to drone warfare.

> "Did you see what happened with Awacs—which was splintered in two? This is two years after Ukraine sent its spiderweb drone attack on the Siberian base and deep into Russia, when it basically obliterated a big chunk of the strategic bomber fleet of its number one adversary. Everybody seemed to have learned from that—the Russians, certainly the Chinese. I don't know that the Taiwanese have. Somehow we haven't."

Ukraine demonstrated that cheap drones can neutralize billion-dollar military assets. Yet two years later, the U.S. has not adequately hardened its own infrastructure against similar threats.

> "It's more important than expanding all these patriots. It's just hardening your defense, putting strong hangers around this. Russians have been building it since that debacle against Ukraine two years ago. Why haven't we?"

## Can the U.S. Fight a Sustained Conventional War?

The question is posed directly: Is the United States prepared for sustained conventional conflict with Russia or China?

> "If a war were to happen tomorrow, some people may question whether we are a peer adversary with China, given that they can build 232 vessels per year and we can build one."

Nrosski frames this in Cold War terms.

> "During the first Cold War in the European theater, the USSR had massive conventional superiority, and that meant that the threshold for nuclear response was very low. We're going back to that world now."

The conventional inferiority created by Chinese shipbuilding capacity creates the same dynamic: if China can achieve conventional dominance in the Western Pacific, the threshold for nuclear escalation by the U.S. becomes dangerously low.

## Investment Landscape: Where Are the Opportunities?

For investors seeking exposure to critical minerals, Nrosski offers a sobering assessment of the available options.

> "The only way to really be exposed to most of them is equity. You're not going to start stockpiling some of the things—not only because it's tough to lay your hands on that, but also the cost of building and maintaining this inventory could be just exorbitant."

Some materials oxidize quickly; others require precise storage conditions. Rare earth oxides cannot simply be held indefinitely—they must be metalized, which currently requires processing in countries like Vietnam.

> "So you're down to equity. And equity means if you want to avoid China, then for most of the opportunity, you're going to be in pre-production—with few exceptions. So you have to be comfortable with the duration of your portfolio given how high the interest rates are. That's not for everybody's liking."

### Gallium: An Emerging Opportunity

Despite the suspension of export bans, Western companies are beginning to develop alternative supply chains.

> "A deal with Alcoa, who's a refinery in Australia, will start producing gallium rather than sending the material to China for separation. There's another smaller deal in the U.S. with a talco. Wouldn't take long for us to settle our differences with Canada so that Rio Tinto and Quebec can also produce some."

Currently, non-Chinese production represents only about 1% of global output—but this is beginning to change.

### Antimony: A Brighter Outlook

> "I'm more optimistic in terms of rebuilding antimony production lines, also because it's often associated with gold, and therefore bankers don't have the problem backing these projects as much as for the others. They can basically translate everything into gold equivalent."

Nrosski projects that within five years, the antimony supply problem will be largely solved—though this still requires substantial capital deployment and policy support.

## The Demand Drivers: AI, Defense, and Batteries

Three major forces are driving critical mineral demand:

### 1. Semiconductors and Electronics

Everything from consumer devices to military systems requires an expanding array of elements. Nrosski notes that the complexity of semiconductor manufacturing has grown dramatically.

> "When I started using the first CPU computer, I think Intel used about 16 different elements. By now, your tiny GPUs and your smartphone—it's almost 10 times more. So it's just amazing how complexified our technologies have become in terms of the different inputs that go into them."

### 2. Defense and Munitions

The war in Ukraine and ongoing tensions globally have accelerated military spending across nations—from India to Germany to Japan to Australia.

> "Historically, civilian market was 95% of all demand for most of these things—if not more. But it's changing in the current circumstances."

Western militaries are simultaneously replenishing stockpiles and expanding production capacity, creating sustained demand growth.

### 3. Battery Technology

> "On the battery material side right now, definitive leadership is with the Chinese producers on the LFP side and increasingly maybe sodium ion as a potential replacement for lithium ion."

The West faces an uphill battle in competing with Chinese battery dominance, though the strategic calculus may shift as nations grow wary of Chinese EV imports that transmit data back to Beijing.

## Key Takeaways

1. **China's dominance spans the entire value chain** — Not just mining and rare earths, but refining, smelting, and processing of copper and other base metals. This control has accumulated over three decades and cannot be unwound in a single election cycle.

2. **Export bans are suspended, not lifted** — The restrictions on gallium, germanium, and antimony remain a live threat. Any investor or policymaker treating the current respite as permanent faces significant strategic risk.

3. **The bottleneck is processing, not extraction** — Western governments must invest in building smelting and refining capacity domestically and with allied nations. Supporting mining projects without processing infrastructure simply transfers dependency downstream.

4. **Military readiness is critically compromised** — Current munitions stockpiles would take four to six years to replenish under peacetime conditions. A sustained conflict with a near-peer adversary would expose severe vulnerabilities.

5. **Investment requires long-term commitment** — The only viable public market exposure to most critical minerals is through pre-production equity. Investors must be prepared for extended timelines and must accept that liquidity will be limited in this space.

The critical minerals crisis is not a future problem—it is a present reality. The question is not whether the West will act, but whether it will act quickly enough to prevent the kind of strategic catastrophe that would make the 1973 oil embargo look like a minor inconvenience.
