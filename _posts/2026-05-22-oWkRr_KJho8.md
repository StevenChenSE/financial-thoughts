## The Question That Defined a Summit

When Chinese President Xi Jinping opened his summit with President Trump, he posed a question that has haunted international relations scholars for decades: Can the United States and China avoid the Thucydides Trap? The concept, drawn from ancient Greek history, suggests that when a rising power threatens to displace an established one, war becomes almost inevitable—as it did when Sparta challenged Athens. Yet according to geopolitical analyst George Friedman, founder of Geopolitical Futures and former CEO of Stratford, the answer to Xi's question is emphatically yes: not because of goodwill or diplomacy, but because geography and economics have fundamentally altered the calculus of power.

"The fundamental difference being that the economies of each of these countries are in some way blocking the possibilities of war," Friedman explains. "It is more toward economic power than from military power."

## Why War Doesn't Pay: The Geography and Economics of US-China Relations

Friedman's argument centers on two interlocking realities that distinguish the Sino-American relationship from every historical case of Thucydides Trap. The first is geography. Sparta and Athens were close neighbors with the technology to wage war against each other and the possibility of one defeating the other through conquest. The United States and China are separated by an ocean—primarily controlled by the United States—and the question of occupation becomes preposterous.

"How do you occupy China? How does China occupy the United States?" Friedman asks. "Exploiting the other is simply not there."

The second reality is economic interdependence so profound that military conflict would be mutually suicidal. China possesses the world's second-largest economy, but per capita income ranks it 71st globally—a statistic Friedman considers "critical" to understanding Beijing's behavior. China's vast productive capacity cannot be absorbed by its domestic market. It must export, and its largest export market is the United States. Meanwhile, American consumers depend on affordable Chinese goods to maintain their standard of living.

"Trump, I think, knew this and imposed the tariffs on China to force them into this position," Friedman notes. "He doesn't have an option if he wants to maintain his economy, but to have a relationship with the United States that's favorable to him."

## The Myth of Ideological Warfare

One of Friedman's most provocative arguments challenges how we frame the US-China competition. He dismisses the notion that competing ideologies will drive conflict. "China is built on business. It is built on corporations, production, employment," he argues. "The principle of Marxism-Leninism aside from the authoritarianism to some extent declining inside of China is irrelevant. Maoism is gone. China is now a major economy and as such it has to operate in those terms."

In Friedman's analysis, China has emerged as what amounts to a "massive capitalist country with great inequality"—not fundamentally different from the United States in its economic DNA. The ideological packaging that Beijing officially maintains is largely theater. "We should get away from the ideology part. The Chinese will officially claim to be whatever they want to be. But in practice, they have emerged as a massive capitalist country."

The implications are significant: if ideological competition was the engine of Cold War rivalry, the absence of genuine ideological conflict fundamentally alters the nature of Sino-American competition. It becomes a matter of economic negotiation, market access, and technological rivalry—serious but not existential.

## Taiwan: The Negotiating Chip Nobody Wants to Admit

The Taiwan question illustrates how economic reality is quietly reshaping political positions. Friedman observes that even on an issue supposedly sacrosanct to Chinese national identity, the dynamics are shifting. "In Taiwan, the KMT party, which is the opposition party, favors becoming part of China again as an autonomous area with the ability to have relation with the United States as well."

The majority in Taiwan may still favor formal independence, but Friedman sees the mood subtly evolving. More importantly, the economic logic that binds Washington and Beijing makes military confrontation over Taiwan increasingly irrational for both sides. "The most important thing is where the world where the cold war was basically a military confrontation... This is primarily an economic confrontation. Neither side having any military desires against the other."

## Russia and China: The Alliance That Isn't

At the time of this interview, Putin was visiting China, leading many analysts to speak of a new Sino-Russian axis. Friedman challenges this framing with striking directness: "China and Russia are not friends. Even under communism they fought border wars against each other."

Even during peak Cold War tensions, Mao called Khrushchev a revisionist and denounced him. Today, China officially claims the eastern part of Russia—the area around Vladivostok that Russia occupied in the late 19th century. Chinese state maps display this territory as belonging to China. "So when we take a look at this, there are fundamental issues between Russia and China," Friedman notes.

Their interests diverge on the most consequential issue of the moment: the Iran war. Russia benefits from oil price spikes that continued Middle East instability would generate. China loses—its economy depends on oil flowing through the Strait of Hormuz, and rising oil prices threaten its economic model. "The Chinese are not going to give him that because the Chinese want the war. They want the price of oil to go down."

For Friedman, the Russia-China relationship is one of convenience, not alliance. Russia is "fragmented"—its economy "a wreck," its military failures in Ukraine exposing weakness that renders it incapable of projecting power. It desperately needs foreign investment and Chinese goodwill. China, meanwhile, finds itself in the position of mediator precisely because its interests align more closely with ending the war than sustaining it.

## The New World Order: From Military Alliances to Economic Architecture

Friedman's thesis extends beyond bilateral relations to a fundamental restructuring of international affairs. The post-1945 world order, he argues, was built on military alliances—NATO, the Warsaw Pact—their entire purpose being to manage the possibility of superpower war. "The basis of the systems in the previous period was alliances, military alliances," Friedman explains. "Now the Soviet Union has demonstrated that it is no threat to Europe. If it could not take Ukraine, it can't take Poland."

The military alliances that "were the foundation of the last 80 years" have become less central. What emerges in their place is an architecture built on economic interdependence—and competition. "The way in which economic interdependence evolves is now more important."

This doesn't mean military power disappears. Friedman strongly defends continued American defense spending, but for a reason often overlooked: defense investment has been "the primary pivot" of American technological development since World War II. "The microchip was first developed in the 1950s by Texas Instruments for the US Air Force." The contemporary AI revolution, nuclear energy, advanced computing—all trace their origins to defense-funded research. "We may now have nuclear reactors rather than oil. We're talking that emerged from it. So it's not a marginal thing. It's been a central pivot of the American economy."

Free trade agreements, meanwhile, served a specific Cold War function—making Europe prosperous enough to stand as an American ally against the Soviet Union. "Free trade was a mantra, something we believed in fundamentally as a moral principle, but it really only took place in the context of the cold war." In the new era, trade policy becomes a tool of economic competition, subject to negotiation rather than moral principle.

## The Iran Crisis: Why Mutually Assured Destruction May Not Apply

The Iran nuclear question presents the most direct challenge to Friedman's optimistic narrative about economic interdependence preventing war. His analysis is nuanced. The American concern about Iranian nuclear weapons is "legitimate," Friedman argues, because of a factor that complicates the logic of mutually assured destruction (MAD).

"The principle of mutual assured destruction depends in part on ideology. The Soviets had the ideology: we do not want to be devastated. The Americans had the ideology." But MAD breaks down when one party operates under a fundamentally different moral calculus. "In the Islamic world, there is a tendency towards suicidal actions."

He points to the Hamas invasion of Israel: "The Hamas invasion was suicidal in the sense that Hamas in the end would be destroyed and they're willing to take that risk." Iran shelters al-Qaeda, the group responsible for the original 9/11 attacks. The combination of nuclear capability and ideological commitment to proxy warfare creates a threat profile that standard deterrence theory cannot address.

"The Americans have a legitimate concern about the thing," Friedman concludes. "That is the only thing they demanded of Iran. Iran has refused to give that and that increases the American concern."

Yet Friedman also sees a path to resolution, mediated by economic reality. China, deeply dependent on oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz, has "volunteered openly to help end the war in Iran." China can help engineer an exit for both sides that avoids humiliation—extracting economic concessions from the United States in the process. "They can extract concessions from the United States possibly on economics."

## What Comes Next: The End of America's Internal Crisis

Friedman's book *The Storm Before the Calm* predicted the current American political crisis before COVID-19 arrived. He sees the pattern as structurally determined: "Every 50 years there's this sort of crisis... every 50 years but I know why it is in America. This is an invented country. We live with the principle of obsolescence and reinvention in every dimension of our lives, personal and economic."

The pattern is creative destruction followed by reconstruction. The 1950s brought crisis over Vietnam and race; the 1970s brought Watergate and economic malaise; each crisis was followed by renewal. "The internal conflict was not the one I predicted," Friedman admits. "I just knew one would come." What follows is predictable: "I do know that these crises always end. The calm that follows is a golden age."

This doesn't minimize the current disorder. "We're certainly in a storm internally," Friedman acknowledges. "The most important thing is the United States remains the pivotal country in militarily, economically and so on. Yet that country, our country is in crisis at this point. Deep political crisis." But the historical pattern suggests recovery, not decline. "We always think we're finished. No hope. It turns out there's something new coming."

## Key Takeaways

- **Geography and economics have defanged the Thucydides Trap**: The Pacific Ocean makes occupation impossible, while economic interdependence makes military conflict suicidal for both the US and China.

- **Ideology no longer drives great power rivalry**: China functions as a capitalist economy despite official rhetoric; the US-China competition is economic rather than ideological.

- **A new world order is emerging based on economic rather than military power**: The post-1945 system built on military alliances is giving way to an architecture of economic interdependence and competition.

- **Defense spending remains strategically essential—not as a relic of the Cold War, but as the foundation of technological innovation**: From microchips to AI, civilian technology gains trace back to defense-funded research.

- **The American internal crisis is temporary and historically precedented**: Every 50 years, America undergoes transformative crisis followed by reconstruction and renewal—the current disorder will pass.
