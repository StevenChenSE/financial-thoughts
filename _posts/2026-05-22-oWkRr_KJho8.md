## The Economics of Peace: How Trade Became the New Deterrent

In the aftermath of World War II, geopolitical stability rested on a simple but terrifying principle: mutually assured destruction. The United States and Soviet Union could obliterate each other countless times over, and so they never fought directly. Now, geopolitical intelligence expert George Friedman argues that a new, equally powerful force has emerged to replace the nuclear standoff—one rooted not in weapons, but in wallets.

"The fundamental difference being that the economies of each of these countries are in some way blocking the possibilities of war," Friedman explains. "This is a different dimension of geopolitics when the military dimension declines in importance and the economic dimension rises dramatically."

The argument is straightforward yet profound: **war between major powers has become economically suicidal**, not because of nuclear annihilation, but because their fates have become inextricably linked through trade.

## Understanding the Facidities Trap

Friedman challenges the conventional wisdom that great powers are inherently expansionist. He introduces the concept of the "facidities trap"—the idea that geographic proximity historically created irresistible temptations for empires to conquer neighbors and seize their wealth.

Consider classical antiquity: Sparta and Athens stood as close neighbors with the military technology to wage devastating wars. Conquest was possible. Wealth transfer through domination seemed rational.

But geography has fundamentally changed the calculus for the United States and China:

- **An ocean separates them**—primarily controlled by American naval power
- **Mutual invasion is strategically impossible**—"How do you occupy China? How does China occupy the United States?"
- **Trade has replaced conquest as the path to wealth**

"The ability to wage war, the possibility of one defeating the other and therefore the other abstracting the wealth of the other—in the case of the United States and China, this is simply not there," Friedman notes.

## China's Economic Reality: The Export Imperative

Here's where the analysis cuts to the core of international relations. China presents a striking paradox: it is simultaneously the world's second-largest economy yet ranks 71st in per capita income. This massive internal contradiction shapes Beijing's every geopolitical move.

The implications are stark:

- **China cannot consume what it produces domestically**—its domestic market cannot support its vast productive capacity
- **China must export**—and the largest market for Chinese goods is the United States
- **American investment in China has been essential** to developing its wealth

"Therefore, [Xi Jinping] doesn't have an option if he wants to maintain his economy, but to have a relationship with the United States that's favorable to him," Friedman argues.

This economic reality explains China's behavior more than any ideological framework. Friedman is direct: "We should get away from the ideology part. The Chinese will officially claim to be whatever they want to be. But in practice, they have emerged as a massive capitalist country with great inequality."

Maoism is dead. Marxism-Leninism is largely ceremonial. What remains is a pragmatic authoritarian state built on corporations, production, and employment—the same foundations that drive any market economy.

## The Trump Tariffs: Economic Leverage or Diplomatic Tool?

Friedman offers a surprising defense of the Trump administration's tariff strategy. Rather than viewing tariffs as simple economic warfare, he sees them as calculated diplomatic pressure designed to force China into a more cooperative position.

"I think he came to power thinking that he had to have a collaboration with China that China would not be collaborating unless he put pressure on it," Friedman suggests. "The tariffs were particularly focused on China so that they moderate to some extent exports to the United States."

Crucially, Friedman notes that tariffs were also imposed on other countries precisely to avoid humiliating China—preventing Beijing from framing the measures as American aggression rather than legitimate trade renegotiation.

## What Each Power Actually Wants

The US-China relationship isn't about ideology or military dominance. It's about economic architecture and mutual assurance.

**What China wants from America:**

- Continued access to American consumer markets
- Massive productive capability absorbed by American demand
- "No one absorbs it as efficiently as the Americans do"

**What America wants from China:**

- Diversification of supply chains—less dependence on Chinese imports
- Military assurances that war will never occur
- Transparency measures such as advance notice of naval exercises
- No-surprise protocols in military operations

**On technology collaboration:**
Trump brought technology executives to China negotiations, signaling that competition doesn't preclude cooperation. "There's great interest in China and the United States about the possibility of collaboration in technology," Friedman notes. "They compliment each other."

## Taiwan: The Symbolic Flashpoint That May Be Resolving

Taiwan represents the most sensitive US-China dispute, yet Friedman sees significant movement toward resolution.

The political landscape on the island is shifting:

- The KMT (Kuomintang), Taiwan's opposition party, favors reunification with Beijing as an autonomous region
- A majority of Taiwanese now reportedly want "to become part of an autonomous part of China officially part of China but autonomous"
- The principle (unification) can be preserved while reality (autonomy) remains unchanged

"You have two powers," Friedman observes, comparing the emerging dynamic to corporate competition. "We're used to ever since the Cold War—if there are two great powers, they're likely to go to war. But possibly this is a different dimension of geopolitics."

## Russia and China: Not the Alliance It Appears

Conventional wisdom treats Russia and China as allied powers united against Western influence. Friedman dismantles this assumption with historical context:

**Key tensions between Beijing and Moscow:**

- They fought **border wars** against each other even during the Cold War
- Mao publicly denounced Khrushchev as a "revisionist"
- China officially claims **Vladivostok** as Chinese territory—publishing maps showing that eastern Russian territory belongs to Beijing
- Russia has been weakened to the point of irrelevance

"China and Russia are not friends," Friedman states flatly.

**On the Ukraine war:**

Russia's position is catastrophic:

- Demonstrated it is "too weak to conquer Ukraine"
- Economy is "a wreck"
- Central bank cannot print money without triggering massive inflation
- Foreign investment has dried up completely
- Eastern Europe, Central Asia, and the Caucasus have "broken away from Russia"

**China's priorities are clear:**

- China **wants the Ukraine war to end** (and will participate in peace negotiations)
- China **wants the Iran war to end** because it depends on oil flowing through the Strait of Hormuz
- Russia benefits from continued Middle East instability (higher oil prices)
- These interests directly conflict

"Putin cannot continue the war," Friedman concludes. "It has cost a vast amount of lives, money, and he's failed, and nothing indicates that he'll succeed."

When Putin visits China seeking support, he's coming "with hand in hand" as a supplicant, not an ally. China extracts concessions from both sides while serving its own interests.

## The Death of the Post-1945 World Order

Friedman has written extensively about the transformation occurring in global governance. The institutions built after World War II—designed around military alliances and superpower confrontation—are becoming obsolete.

**The old system:**

- Built on military alliances (NATO, Warsaw Pact)
- Structured around the threat of nuclear war
- Designed to prevent Soviet expansion

**The emerging system:**

- Structured around economic interdependence
- Driven by trade relationships and market access
- Built on mutual benefit rather than mutual destruction

"The United Nations is non-existent," Friedman offers with characteristic bluntness. "It's a place you go to have meetings. It doesn't do anything. It doesn't have any influence in the world."

NATO's purpose has fundamentally changed. The Warsaw Pact's successor (Russia) has been exposed as incapable of conventional military operations. Poland alone now fields a more effective military than Russia demonstrated in Ukraine.

## Defense Spending in a New Era: The Technology Imperative

With military threats declining, does massive American defense spending still make sense? Friedman's answer is unequivocal: **yes, but for different reasons**.

The real value of defense expenditure lies in its technological spillovers:

- **Microchips** were first developed in the 1950s by Texas Instruments for the US Air Force
- **Nuclear power** emerged from military programs
- **The internet** began as ARPANET, a military communications network
- **GPS** was a military navigation system
- **AI development** is heavily concentrated in defense-related research

"So much of the technology that emerged, for example, the microchip was first developed in the 1950s by Texas Instruments to the US Air Force," Friedman notes. "So there's a vast spin-off from military technology development and the contemporary economy."

His assessment is blunt: defense spending shouldn't be evaluated purely as a cost to be reduced. "If you go back in the history of technology since World War II, the pivot was defense. All of these things emerged out of defense. It's not a marginal thing. It's been a central pivot of the American economy."

## Free Trade: The Mantra That Served Its Purpose

The post-war economic order elevated free trade to near-sacred status. Friedman argues this was never about principle—it was about strategy.

"Free trade was a mantra, something we believed in fundamentally as a moral principle, but it really only took place in the context of the Cold War, just as aid to foreign nations evolved as a tool in the Cold War, not out of charity," he explains. "We gave money to other nations so they did not become Soviet allies."

Now that the Cold War is over and new economic powers have emerged, the calculus has changed. Tariffs and trade restrictions are returning as legitimate policy tools, not heresies against market orthodoxy.

## The Iran Dilemma: Ideology Over Rationality

Friedman turns to the Middle East, where the logic of mutually assured destruction may not apply as neatly as with China.

The American concern about Iranian nuclear weapons stems from a fundamental question: **Does MAD work against irrational actors?**

The traditional deterrence model assumes both sides share certain values:

- A preference for survival
- Rational cost-benefit analysis
- Recognition that nuclear war means mutual destruction

"Iran has a very different one," Friedman says of ideological frameworks. "Not all Islamic countries have this by far, but Iran in particular has this capability that MAD may not work."

The concern isn't just Iranian behavior—it's the groups Tehran harbors:

- Al-Qaeda (which launched the actual 9/11 attacks) maintains a presence in Iran
- Hezbollah operates under Iranian protection
- These groups share ideologies willing to embrace "suicidal actions"

"The Hamas invasion of Israel was suicidal in the sense that Hamas in the end would be destroyed and they're willing to take that risk," Friedman notes.

This creates an asymmetry. The United States, China, and Europe all calculate that nuclear war is unthinkable. Iran's ideological framework may be different.

**The fundamental impasse:**

- America demands: Iran abandons nuclear weapons development
- Iran calculates: Nuclear weapons are the only guarantee against regime change
- America's response: The US has never invaded a nuclear-armed nation (Britain, Iraq, Afghanistan, North Korea all lacked nukes and faced American military action)

"Iran has refused to give that and that increases the American concern," Friedman observes. "Can the United States accept a country with nuclear weapons that gives sanctuary to al-Qaeda?"

## America's Internal Storm: Cyclical Crisis and Renewal

Friedman's 2020 book, *The Storm Before the Calm*, predicted internal American upheaval. The COVID-19 pandemic and its aftermath have vindicated some of these projections, though Friedman admits he didn't predict the specifics.

"We're certainly in a storm internally," he acknowledges, comparing the current moment to the 1950s, which saw crises over Vietnam, civil rights, and social upheaval.

His theoretical framework is historical: "Every 50 years there's this sort of crisis... This goes back to the founding. I don't know why this is 50 years, but I know why it is in America. This is an invented country. We live with the principle of obsolescence and reinvention in every dimension of our lives, personal and economic."

**The pattern:**

1. Crisis emerges
2. Creative destruction occurs
3. Reconstruction follows
4. A golden age begins

"We're in this position. We always think we're finished, no hope. It turns out there's something new coming."

## AI and Automation: The Next Creative Destruction

Could automation create the next internal crisis? Friedman is cautious.

"One can succeed, the other can fail," he says of AI development. "It can fail with the singularity—the idea that it will evolve dramatically. They may be vastly overestimating it, just as the auto industry when it got into trouble tried to build cars that were also boats, which never got off the ground."

Historical precedent suggests moderation:

- Railroads were predicted to make schools obsolete (students would learn via television instead)
- Television was supposed to revolutionize education
- Each technology created disruption, but also created opportunities

"If there is massive unemployment in a country, a massive store comes out," Friedman admits. "But there's a time I don't think that'll happen. I think the ability of the United States to absorb new inventions with some social discord but fundamentally take them is significant."

## Europe: The Fragmented Question

The conversation ends with the most consequential unknown: what becomes of Europe?

"Europe remains a fragmented," Friedman observes. "There's no such thing as a European position. The French are going to do different things than the Italians."

Yet the EU's GDP exceeds China's. A unified European approach would be a third great power. As American-Chinese understanding develops and Russia weakens, Europe must decide whether to integrate or continue fragmenting.

## The New Multipolar Reality

What emerges from Friedman's analysis is a fundamentally transformed world:

- **Two major powers** (US and China) will compete for markets but have no interest in military conflict
- **Military alliances** matter less than economic relationships
- **Ideology** has given way to pragmatism in most nations
- **Regional wars** will continue, but great power conflicts have become economically irrational

"It's a different principle of relations," Friedman concludes. "The question of hostile relationships militarily between China and the United States has to be answered: Why? What would either gain from that, and what would the cost be?"

The answer, increasingly, is: nothing worth the cost.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Economic interdependence has replaced nuclear deterrence as the primary check on great power war.** The US and China are too economically linked—China needs American markets; America needs affordable goods—for military conflict to make rational sense.

2. **Geography creates an impassable barrier to conventional war.** The Pacific Ocean makes mutual occupation impossible. Conquest no longer offers economic advantages when trade is more profitable than plunder.

3. **China is pragmatically capitalist despite official ideology.** Beijing's foreign policy is driven by market access and resource security, not revolutionary ideology. The Communist Party labels are largely ceremonial now.

4. **Russia and China are rivals, not allies.** Historical border wars, Chinese territorial claims on Vladivostok, and conflicting interests (Russia wants high oil prices from Middle East instability; China wants low oil prices) reveal fundamental tensions.

5. **Defense spending drives technological innovation that fuels the economy.** From microchips to GPS to nuclear power, military research has been the foundation of America's technological advantage. Cutting defense budgets would shift that burden to the private sector with uncertain results.

6. **The post-1945 world order built on military alliances is giving way to a system based on economic relationships.** Traditional institutions like the UN and NATO face obsolescence as trade agreements replace military treaties as the primary diplomatic tools.

7. **Internal American crises follow a 50-year cyclical pattern of creative destruction followed by renewal.** Despite current political polarization, historical precedent suggests this crisis will resolve into a new period of stability and growth.
