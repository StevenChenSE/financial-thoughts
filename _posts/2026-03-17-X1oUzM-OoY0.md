A stark warning is being issued about the future of U.S. economic and diplomatic relations, centered on a fundamental failure of respect. The argument posits that the United States is on a collision course with a formidable, prideful nation—a nation of 93 million people, comparable in size to Western Europe.

The core of the problem is identified as cultural contempt. "These people are very proud," the analysis states. "They're proud of their origins, their culture, their identity, their history." The damning conclusion? "We spit on it. That's the problem."

This perceived disrespect is seen as having direct and severe economic consequences. The assertion is blunt: because of this dynamic, "they're not going to do business with us. Certainly not on our terms."

The path forward, as outlined, is one of diminished leverage. It suggests that the U.S. will be forced to seek an intermediary, to "find someone somewhere who can mediate for us." The outcome of such a mediated negotiation is framed not as a compromise, but as a capitulation.

The final, clipped thought delivers the sobering punchline: the terms the United States will have to accept are going to be "very humiliating." This points to a potential future where America, accustomed to setting global economic conditions, must instead submit to conditions that wound its national pride and redefine its position in the world order.

The message is clear: without a fundamental shift in approach—one that replaces dismissal with genuine respect—the U.S. may be steering toward a diplomatic and economic reckoning where it no longer holds the pen that writes the rules.
