## A generational bond, practical wisdom, and questions about China’s path forward

### Introduction
In this talk, Lin Yifu outlines three reasons for attending the event: to listen to stories from his contemporary and close friend Shang Weijian; to express admiration for Shang’s rare ability to turn knowledge into real-world success; and to raise forward-looking questions about how China can navigate today’s complex challenges and continue advancing toward national rejuvenation.

### Friendship across different life paths
Lin Yifu notes that he and Shang Weijian belong to the same generation: Lin was born in 1952 and Shang in 1953. Lin earned his PhD from the University of Chicago in 1986, while Shang received his from UC Berkeley in 1987. They are part of the first post-1949 cohort of Chinese students to pursue doctoral degrees in the United States—a small circle at the time, which naturally led to frequent contact.

Their backgrounds, however, are quite different. Lin was born in Taiwan and did not come to Peking University until 1976–1979. Shang grew up as a “educated youth” (shanzhang zhiqing) in Inner Mongolia. Despite sharing a generation, their personal histories reflect very different experiences of modern China. Lin values Shang not only as a friend but as a storyteller whose accounts fill gaps in his own understanding. Statistics and documents are useful, but without lived experience, they are hard to fully grasp. Shang’s stories have helped Lin better comprehend the social realities of China.

Their friendship began in the 1980s in the United States and has continued for decades. Although Lin works in Beijing and Shang has worked overseas, they make the most of every opportunity to meet. Lin says he has come to this event, in part, to listen to Shang’s stories again—stories that combine data, personal experience, and sharp observation, helping the audience better understand themselves and their society.

### The ideal of “knowledge and action as one”
Lin’s second purpose is to express admiration for Shang Weijian’s embodiment of what Lin calls “知行一体” (knowledge and action as one). In Lin’s view, theory should do more than help us understand the world; it should also help us improve it. At the national and social level, theory should guide better development. At the personal level, it should help individuals make better decisions and achieve real results. Yet putting knowledge and action together is very difficult.

Lin illustrates this with an anecdote about Nobel laureate Robert Shiller, author of *Irrational Exuberance*. Shiller’s book, published in 2000, argued that the internet boom was irrational and bound to burst. In 2001, the internet bubble did indeed collapse. When Shiller later promoted the Chinese edition of his book in Beijing, Lin asked him a direct question: if you predicted the bubble’s burst, did you invest accordingly? Did you make money? Shiller admitted he had made some investments but had not profited significantly. Lin pressed further: if you truly believed your own analysis, you should have become very wealthy. The fact that you didn’t suggests you did not fully trust your own theory.

Lin extends this observation to finance professors as a group: despite teaching how markets work and how to invest, they do not, as a cohort, outperform ordinary investors. This gap between knowing and doing, Lin argues, is a failure of “知行一体.”

In contrast, Lin admires Shang Weijian for living up to this ideal. In 1993, Shang left the Wharton School—one of the world’s top business schools—where he taught finance. Rather than remaining only in academia, he moved into practice: first in investment banking, then in private equity (PE). According to Lin, Shang’s track record shows consistent success: his investment banking deals largely succeeded; his PE investments, including in Korea’s First Bank, delivered very high returns; his involvement in acquiring Shenzhen Development Bank was also successful and profitable; and later, as the head of his own PE firm, Taikang Asset Management (implied as “泰盟” in the transcript), he has maintained strong fund performance. Lin sees this as proof that Shang can translate theory into effective action.

For Lin, this is what education and theory should aim for. He hopes Shang’s students can similarly transform knowledge into outstanding personal success. At the national level, he hopes theory does not simply produce critics who say “this is wrong, that is wrong,” but instead offers practical guidance on how to succeed—so that applying theory actually improves outcomes rather than making things worse. In Shang’s case, theory helps identify opportunities and pitfalls, and then action turns insight into results.

### China’s development, global comparisons, and future questions
Lin’s third purpose is to raise big questions about China’s future. He recalls that when he and Shang were young, China was one of the world’s poorest countries. Up to around 1990, China still performed better than India, and some believed China’s path to success should follow India’s model: strong constitutional institutions and democratic systems. Yet today, India’s per capita GDP, once higher than China’s, is roughly one-fifth of China’s level.

Other transitions offer cautionary tales. Lin points to the former Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, where countries followed mainstream reform theories. Before their transitions, their gap with advanced economies was not as large as China’s was at its starting point. Yet today, by market exchange rates, Russia’s economy—once comparable in relative size to China’s position versus the United States—is now smaller than China’s Guangdong Province alone.

Against this background, China’s achievements over the past four decades can be called a miracle in economic history. As often stated, China is now closer than ever before to the great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation. But Lin warns: “行百里者半九十” (the last ten percent is half the journey). The closer China gets to this goal, the more challenges it faces. These include:

- Accelerating “changes unseen in a century” (百年未有之大变局).
- Frequent external shocks in the international environment.
- The Fourth Industrial Revolution reshaping global technology and industry.
- Overlapping technological, economic, and geopolitical pressures.

Given this context, Lin asks several critical questions:

- Could China fall into a trap similar to Japan’s? In the mid-1980s, Japan’s economy was about two-thirds the size of the United States by market exchange rates. Today, it is less than one-fifth. Could China experience a similar long-term slowdown or stagnation?
- Can China not only avoid such a trap, but continue to grow?
- Is it possible for China’s economy, measured by market exchange rates, to surpass that of the United States in total scale?
- Beyond aggregate size, can a large portion of the Chinese population—not just a small elite—reach income levels comparable to those in the United States? Lin envisions the possibility of 300 million, 400 million, or even 500 million Chinese people attaining such living standards.
- Do the opportunities for this exist? If so, where are they?
- Drawing from Shang’s practical experience, insights, and successful philosophy, how can China identify and seize these opportunities to realize the multi-generational dream of true national rejuvenation?

### Summary
Lin Yifu’s remarks weave personal history, intellectual principles, and national strategy. He highlights his decades-long friendship with Shang Weijian as a window into different Chinese experiences, praises Shang’s embodiment of “知行一体” as a model for turning theory into success, and challenges the audience to think seriously about China’s path forward. Facing unprecedented global changes and technological shifts, Lin asks whether China can avoid the pitfalls experienced by others, sustain its development, and deliver prosperity not just in aggregate terms, but to hundreds of millions of its people.
