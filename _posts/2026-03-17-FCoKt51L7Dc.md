In a global landscape reshaped by the return of Donald Trump to the White House, a counterintuitive sentiment is emerging from China: a sense of strategic opportunity. According to Shawn Rain, founder of the China Market Research Group, there is "a feeling in China that Trump is the best thing ever to happen to China" because his policies have alienated traditional U.S. allies, driving them to seek stronger ties with Beijing.

This perspective frames a broader discussion on how China is positioning itself amid Middle East turmoil, a fragmenting Western alliance, and its own long-term economic vision.

## Energy Security: A Calculated Buffer Against Shock

The recent tensions in the Middle East, a critical region for global oil, present a direct challenge to China, which imports 20% of its oil needs from Iran via the Strait of Hormuz. This follows earlier losses, such as the 2-3% of imports that disappeared with the U.S. action against Venezuela.

However, Rain argues the impact is deliberately muted. China’s strategy over the past decade has focused on reducing dependence and improving energy security through massive investments in renewables.

"For example, China increased about 380 gigawatts of solar technology last year which is more than the rest of the world combined," Rain notes. "China also added for the 15th straight year... more wind power than the rest of the world."

Furthermore, the state manages price shocks differently. While U.S. gas prices can spike 10-20% quickly, "China tends to raise prices one to two weeks slower... and it's more incremental," with the government forcing state-owned enterprises to absorb short-term profit hits. This cushions the immediate blow to consumers and manufacturers.

## The Consumer Shift: EVs and Political Optimism

For the average Chinese citizen, the reaction is twofold. First, high gas prices are less pressing as the electric vehicle (EV) revolution accelerates. "Over 50% of new auto sales in 2025 were NEVs [New Energy Vehicles]," Rain states, highlighting companies like BYD, which just launched new battery technology enabling a full charge in about 10 minutes. He believes Middle East tensions will only push Chinese consumers to adopt EVs faster, further disadvantaging legacy automakers.

Politically, Rain observes a "slow increase of animal spirits and happiness" among Chinese consumers. The reason? A perceived diplomatic windfall from Trump's foreign policy. "He's pissed off the Canadians, the British, the French, the Germans... In the last three months... they've all come almost like beggars in a way to China," Rain says, describing a scenario where European leaders are seeking to repair economic relations.

## Geopolitics: Pragmatism Over Alliance

With Iran heavily reliant on China—70% of its oil exports go there—the question arises: is Tehran asking Beijing to intervene? Rain believes China will steadfastly avoid military entanglements, adhering to a policy of non-interference and sovereignty.

"I don't think Iran and China are as close as a lot of Americans think," he contends, dismissing the idea of an "axis of power." Instead, he frames China's relationships with Iran, Russia, and Venezuela as "a matter of survival" in response to U.S. containment efforts. "China says we don't have friends... They're being forced to get closer... not out of an axis of power, but out of a matter of survivability."

He emphasizes China's historical affinity for the U.S., noting that the children and grandchildren of Chinese leaders for decades have been educated at American universities like Duke, Yale, and Harvard. "This is a country that loves the United States," Rain asserts.

## The Grand Strategy: Independence Across the Board

The Trump-era trade wars that began in 2016 were a wake-up call. "China started to realize that the rhetoric from Trump and the rhetoric emerging from Western Europe made them realize they had to become more independent," Rain explains.

This sparked a hundreds-of-billions-of-dollars push across three key fronts:
1.  **Energy Independence:** The massive build-out of solar, wind, and nuclear.
2.  **Technology Independence:** Heavy investment in indigenous semiconductors and AI, reducing reliance on companies like Nvidia.
3.  **Food Security:** Developing domestic production for goods like beef and blueberries, once imported from Australia and Brazil.

"Across the board, China is saying we're getting a cold war 2.0. We need to focus on independence for literally every single sector," Rain summarizes.

## Economic Forecast: American Crisis, Chinese Buffer?

Rain expresses deep concern about the U.S. economic outlook. "I'm actually really worried about a deep financial crisis in the United States, depression like worse in 2008 when you have 39-40 trillion US of debt." He cites damaged alliances and questions about presidential leadership as compounding risks.

Should such a crisis occur, China is not immune but has built buffers. Its exports to the U.S. have fallen from 14.5% of the total in 2017 to about 11% today, with Southeast Asia now taking 18%. Concurrently, the internationalization of the Renminbi (RMB) progresses, with about 40% of China's cross-border trade now conducted in its own currency.

Rain also sees a capital flow shift: "So many international institutional investors are moving out of Japan, moving out of Korea, moving out of the United States and going back into China." He is "quite optimistic for the Chinese equity markets over the next 1 to 2-year period," particularly for tech and semiconductor firms buoyed by state bank loans and growing demand from the "global majority" wary of U.S. sanctions.

## The Taiwan Question: Not Imminent

Finally, does U.S. involvement in the Middle East make a Chinese move on Taiwan more likely? Rain, who is regularly consulted by hedge funds on this question, thinks not in the short term.

He points to turmoil within China's military leadership, where "seven of the nine members of the Central Military Commission have been arrested or detained." This, combined with the sheer difficulty of amphibious invasion—and America's own mixed record in recent conflicts—suggests the status quo will hold for now.

In conclusion, from Shawn Rain's perspective, China is navigating a turbulent Trump world not through direct confrontation, but by methodically building domestic resilience, capitalizing on Western diplomatic fractures, and positioning its companies as reliable alternatives for a world skeptical of American unilateralism. The strategy is one of calculated patience, betting that time and internal preparation are its greatest allies.
