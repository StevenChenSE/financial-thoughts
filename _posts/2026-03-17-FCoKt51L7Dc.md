In a recent interview, Shawn Rein, founder and managing director of the China Market Research Group, offered a detailed analysis of how China is navigating the economic and geopolitical turbulence sparked by conflict in the Middle East, while also positioning itself for a future less dependent on the West.

## Energy Security: A Strategic Cushion Against Middle East Volatility

The disruption of oil flows from Iran—which supplies roughly 20% of China’s oil imports—poses a clear challenge. However, Rein argues China is “going to be hit a lot less than the rest of the world.” This confidence stems from a decade-long, multi-pronged strategy:

*   **Diversified Energy Mix:** China has invested heavily in renewable energy, adding “380 gigawatts of solar technology last year… more than the rest of the world combined,” and leading in wind power installation for 15 consecutive years.
*   **Price Controls:** The Chinese government manages consumer impact by forcing state-owned enterprises to absorb short-term profit hits, leading to slower, more incremental price increases at the pump compared to the immediate spikes seen in the West.
*   **EV Adoption:** With over 50% of new auto sales in 2025 being New Energy Vehicles (NEVs), the average Chinese consumer is less vulnerable to oil price shocks. Rein believes Middle East tensions will “push the Chinese consumer to adopt NEVs even more,” further disadvantaging legacy automakers.

## The Trump Factor: An Unexpected Geopolitical Windfall

A surprising sentiment in China, according to Rein, is that “Trump is the best thing ever to happen to China.” The reasoning is that his policies have alienated traditional U.S. allies, driving them to seek closer ties with Beijing.

“In the last three months… you’ve seen Keir Starmer, [others] from Germany, they’ve all come almost like beggars in a way to China,” Rein stated, suggesting European leaders are now seeking to repair economic relations. This has fostered a “slow increase of animal spirits and happiness” among Chinese consumers, who perceive the U.S. as being weakened under Trump’s leadership.

## Iran and China: A Relationship of Necessity, Not Alliance

Despite deep trade links—with 70% of Iran’s oil exports going to China—Rein downplays the notion of a tight strategic alliance. He emphasizes China’s principle of non-interference.

“I don’t think Iran and China are as close as a lot of Americans think,” he said. “China basically gets closer to Venezuela, Iran, and especially Russia as a matter of survival.” He described it as a pragmatic response to U.S. containment efforts, not an “axis of power.”

China’s response to the conflict will likely remain economic, not military. “I think what they're going to do is say look we would just want to buy oil,” Rein explained, noting that Iranian forces are already allowing Chinese ships safe passage through the Strait of Hormuz.

## The Grand Strategy: Independence Across All Sectors

The trade wars that began in 2016 were a wake-up call. “China started to realize that the rhetoric from Trump and the rhetoric emerging from Western Europe made them realize they had to become more independent,” Rein noted.

This has sparked a national project of “independence for literally every single sector”:
*   **Technology:** Heavy investment in indigenous semiconductors and AI, reducing reliance on companies like Nvidia.
*   **Food Security:** Developing domestic production for goods like beef and blueberries, once imported.
*   **Financial Derisking:** Only about 11% of China’s exports now go to the U.S., down from 14.5% in 2017, with Southeast Asia taking an 18% share. The internationalization of the Renminbi (RMB), now used in 40% of China’s cross-border trade, provides a buffer against the U.S. dollar.

## Bullish on Chinese Tech, Bearish on U.S. Stability

Rein is optimistic about Chinese tech companies, particularly semiconductors and AI, buoyed by state bank loans and growing demand from the “global majority.”

“America's bullying and oppression… is going to come back to haunt them,” he argued, citing Malaysia’s pivot to Huawei chips after U.S. export bans disrupted its AI ambitions.

Conversely, he expressed deep concern about the United States. “I'm actually really worried about a deep financial crisis… worse than 2008,” citing the national debt, fractured alliances, and questioning President Trump’s mental acuity. “I think dementia has started to overtake some of his thinking processes,” Rein stated bluntly.

He posited that China could overtake the U.S. as the largest economy not solely through its own growth, but “more because of an implosion economically and politically in the United States.”

## Taiwan and the Military Calculus

On the heightened speculation about a Chinese invasion of Taiwan amid U.S. focus on Iran, Rein was skeptical in the short term. He cited a lack of readiness within China’s military leadership, where seven of nine Central Military Commission members are under investigation. “The entire leadership in the military right now is not ready for a land invasion of Taiwan,” he concluded.

---

**The Bottom Line:** Shawn Rein paints a picture of a China methodically building buffers—in energy, technology, trade, and finance—to insulate itself from global shocks and Western pressure. While challenges like the Middle East conflict present real economic hits, China’s long-term diversification strategy and the perceived geopolitical missteps of the U.S. are fostering a cautious optimism within the country about its own resilient, independent path forward.
