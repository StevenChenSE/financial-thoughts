**A conversation between Markus Brunnermeier and Brad Setser on the resurgence of global trade imbalances, the shift from a multilateral to a transactional world order, and the geopolitical implications of China’s export-led growth.**

## Introduction

The global economic landscape is undergoing a profound transformation characterized by what experts are calling the "Second China Shock." In a recent discussion with Brad Setser from the Council on Foreign Relations, Markus Brunnermeier explores how today’s global imbalances differ significantly from those preceding the 2008 Global Financial Crisis (GFC). While the pre-GFC era was defined by a rule-based multilateral order and mutual interdependencies, the current system is increasingly transactional, bilateral, and focused on minimizing geopolitical choke points. This shift has led to a new wave of global imbalances that are not only larger in scale but also distinct in their geographic concentration, underlying economic drivers, and financial financing mechanisms.

## From Multilateralism to Geopolitical Resilience

The first major distinction in the current era is the shift from a liberal, rule-based trading system to one driven by spheres of influence and resilience. In the previous order, mutual interdependencies created a balance of choke points among nations. Today, the focus is on minimizing reliance on others and emphasizing strategic autonomy.

Brunnermeier introduces the concept of "dual imbalances." Traditional metrics measure imbalances in goods and services, but a second, critical dimension involves geopolitical dependencies. A product is not just a good; it carries implications for strategic leverage. For instance, reliance on rare earth minerals or advanced semiconductors creates immediate or long-term comparative advantages for the supplier. Brunnermeier proposes a "resilience account" to measure these geopolitical dependencies, arguing that what matters is not just gross interdependence but whether the balance of choke points is equitable.

Furthermore, comparative advantage is no longer static. Through industrial policies and "learning by doing," countries can build dynamic advantages, particularly in sectors with increasing returns to scale. This has significant implications for exchange rate and trade policies, as distortions in one currency (like the Chinese yuan) can ripple through to others, exacerbating global imbalances.

## The Geography and Nature of Surpluses

Brad Setser highlights three key differences between the pre-GFC surplus expansion and today’s "Second China Shock." First, the geography of surpluses has shifted. Before the GFC, surpluses were widespread across emerging economies, including oil exporters and commodity producers. Today, the global surplus is heavily concentrated in East Asia, specifically China, South Korea, and Taiwan.

Second, the internal dynamics of the Chinese economy have changed. In the pre-GFC era, China experienced a broad-based boom with strong domestic demand. Today, the Chinese economy is bifurcated: the export sector is booming while domestic demand has stalled. This divergence means that regions like Europe, which rely on selling goods to China, are hit harder than in the past.

Third, the financial flows financing these imbalances have transformed. Pre-GFC, there was a rapid growth in official reserves and a shortage of safe assets like US Treasuries, leading to a search for yield in riskier assets like housing. Today, reserve growth has flattened, and the US is issuing large volumes of Treasuries to fund fiscal deficits. The financing now relies more on private flows and "quasi-official" institutions, such as the National Pension Service of Korea, which seek returns rather than just safety.

## East Asia’s Dominance and the Chip Boom

The concentration of surpluses in East Asia is unprecedented. China’s goods surplus is now roughly twice the size of Japan’s surplus in the mid-1980s relative to world GDP. In manufacturing terms, China’s surplus accounts for about 2% of world GDP, surpassing the historical peaks of both Japan and Germany.

This surge is partly driven by the AI and semiconductor boom. Taiwan, home to TSMC, has maintained a persistent current account surplus of over 10% of GDP for a decade, fueled by its dominance in chip manufacturing. South Korea, a major producer of memory chips, has seen its surplus jump from 5% to 20% of GDP due to shortages in memory chips and high demand. While these surpluses are partly driven by temporary price shocks and IP rents, they significantly distort international balances.

## China’s Stalled Domestic Economy and Import Substitution

A critical feature of the Second China Shock is the decoupling of Chinese exports from domestic demand. Since 2021, China’s imports have grown by less than 5% in real terms, despite nominal GDP growth. This is largely due to the collapse of the property sector, which has reduced investment by over 40%.

However, investment in manufacturing has surged, driven by a strategy of import substitution and technological upgrading. China is heavily investing in sectors where it previously relied on imports, such as chips, electric vehicles, and aerospace. This policy, aligned with "Made in China 2025" and the goal of "indigenous innovation," aims to reduce dependence on US and European choke points. Consequently, Chinese manufactured imports have stagnated for a decade, while exports have exploded, particularly in sectors where domestic demand is weak.

## Financial Flows and the Treasury Market

The financing of the US current account deficit has also changed. Before the GFC, central banks absorbed vast amounts of US Treasuries, creating a shortage of safe assets. Today, Asian central banks have largely stopped accumulating reserves, opting instead for riskier assets or sovereign wealth funds. This has led to a situation where Treasury issuance exceeds foreign official demand, relying more on private investors who are more sensitive to price and return.

This shift has implications for the stability of the US Treasury market. While the market remains deep and liquid, the reliance on leveraged private buyers, such as those engaged in basis trades, introduces new risks. The yield curve has become more volatile, particularly at the long end, reflecting the changing investor base.

## Measurement Issues and Currency Undervaluation

Setser points out significant statistical puzzles in China’s reported data. China’s investment income balance shows a persistent deficit, which is inconsistent with its large net creditor position and rising global interest rates. This discrepancy suggests underreporting of income from state banks and foreign direct investment.

Adjusting for these measurement issues reveals that China’s current account surplus is significantly larger than officially reported—closer to 5.5% of GDP rather than the reported 4%. This has profound implications for assessing currency undervaluation. While the IMF estimates an undervaluation of around 19%, a corrected model suggests it could be closer to 30%.

China’s currency is heavily managed, with the central bank setting a daily "fix" against the dollar. Correcting the undervaluation would require China to set a stronger fix, which would involve policy changes. While a stronger currency would benefit Chinese households by increasing purchasing power, it could hurt exporters and exacerbate domestic economic challenges. However, Setser argues that the current policy is driven by geopolitical considerations, aiming to maximize the world’s reliance on Chinese supply chains while minimizing China’s own dependencies.

## Conclusion

The Second China Shock represents a fundamental shift in the global economic order. It is characterized by concentrated East Asian surpluses, a bifurcated Chinese economy, and a new financial landscape where private flows dominate. These imbalances are not just economic but deeply geopolitical, reflecting a move away from mutual interdependence toward strategic resilience. As China continues to pursue import substitution and technological self-sufficiency, the rest of the world faces acute choices about how to manage trade relationships and mitigate the risks of one-sided dependencies. Understanding these dual imbalances—economic and geopolitical—is crucial for navigating the challenges of this new era.
