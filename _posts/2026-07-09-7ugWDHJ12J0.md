## Nomi Prins on Central Bank Reserves, Supply Chain Weaponization, and Investment Opportunities

In a recent session at the Rick Rule Symposium, geoeconomist and bestselling author Nomi Prins dissected the evolving landscape of global markets, highlighting a profound shift from financial distortions to tangible real-economy disruptions. Prins outlined how geopolitical tensions are reshaping supply chains, altering central bank reserve strategies, and redefining inflation dynamics. Her analysis points to a new era of "geopolitical commodity warfare," where nations prioritize supply chain resilience over global efficiency, creating significant risks and opportunities across gold, oil, treasuries, and critical minerals.

### The Great Reserve Shift: Gold Overtakes Treasuries

A central theme of Prins' analysis is the structural realignment of central bank reserves. Historically, the US Treasury market relied heavily on foreign central banks as primary buyers. However, this dynamic is rapidly changing. Prins noted that central banks are actively downplaying their Treasury purchases, with gold having officially overtaken US Treasuries as the number one reserve asset in their books.

The divestment from US debt is starkly illustrated by the People's Bank of China. Between 2014 and 2018, China held approximately $1.3 trillion in US Treasury bonds. By the end of last year, that figure had dropped to $750 billion, and it currently sits around $620 billion—less than half of its holdings from a decade ago. While China's gold reserves still represent only 5% to 6% of its total reserves, the trajectory is clear. A recent World Gold Council survey indicated that a record 45% of central banks plan to add gold to their reserves this year. Prins emphasized that as central banks monetize gold or use Treasury drawdowns to purchase it, the global financial system is implicitly moving toward a new gold-backed collateral environment, reminiscent of pre-1971 dynamics.

### Oil Markets and the Strait of Hormuz

The recent tensions in the Middle East initially triggered severe market distortions, with oil prices spiking to $138 per barrel due to fears surrounding the closure of the Strait of Hormuz. However, Prins argued that these fears were largely artificial and driven by paper-trading volatility. With the opening of strategic petroleum reserves by the US and Europe, alongside increased production from Middle Eastern nations, Venezuela, and Colombia, oil prices have normalized to a $70–$80 range.

While the Strait of Hormuz remains partially operational, it now functions with a de facto toll system, restricting tanker traffic to a fraction of pre-war levels. Prins explained that while volatility will persist, global oil supply mechanisms are mature enough to reroute distribution. The current price floor is supported by the time required to replenish strategic reserves and the additional costs of rerouting shipments, but a return to the $60 range is unlikely given these structural shifts.

### Fed Policy, Inflation, and Kevin Warsh

The US 30-year Treasury yield recently hit the 5% psychological level, signaling tighter conditions and waning demand for US bonds. Prins attributed this to increased debt issuance required to fund geopolitical conflicts and service interest payments, alongside reduced foreign demand. 

Regarding Federal Reserve policy, Prins expressed skepticism about the central bank's ability to control inflation through rate adjustments alone. She argued that inflation is primarily driven by supply chain movements and commodity availability, not marginal interest rate changes. With unemployment hovering around 4.2% to 4.3%, Prins suggested that raising rates would be a mistake, potentially stifling the economy without addressing the root causes of price increases. 

She also analyzed the recent comments by Federal Reserve Governor Kevin Warsh, which markets interpreted as hawkish. Prins viewed Warsh's brief, data-dependent statements as a strategic power move to assert independence rather than a commitment to aggressive tightening. Meanwhile, the Fed's balance sheet continues to grow by $40–$50 billion monthly through Treasury purchases, effectively continuing quantitative easing (QE) in the background despite public narratives of tightening.

### Geopolitical Commodity Warfare and Supply Chains

The global economy is transitioning from an era of efficiency-driven globalization to one focused on strategic resilience. Nations are actively ring-fencing critical supply chains, utilizing export controls as economic weapons. Prins highlighted China's recent restrictions on processed rare earth exports to US-backed companies like MP Materials and USA Rare Earth, as well as tungsten export controls that have impacted Japan's semiconductor industry.

This weaponization of commodities means consumers and downstream industries will face higher costs regardless of whether nations invest in rebuilding domestic infrastructure or pay premiums for restricted imports. The focus has shifted from cost minimization to securing control over essential inputs, fundamentally altering global trade dynamics.

### Investment Opportunities in Distorted Markets

These structural shifts have created notable investment opportunities across several asset classes:

*   **Silver:** Prins highlighted a massive distortion in the silver market, where paper trading volumes in major silver ETFs dwarf the annual physical production of 820 million ounces. This discrepancy creates volatility but also immense upside potential for those holding physical exposure or selective mining equities.
*   **Copper:** Unlike silver, copper has shown resilience due to its critical nature and lack of excessive paper trading mechanisms. Prins recommends junior copper developers outside regions dependent on restricted inputs like sulfuric acid, viewing them as potential takeover targets for major miners.
*   **Critical Minerals:** The rise of commodity warfare underscores the value of hard heavy metals and rare earths. Prins advised investors to allocate exposure to sectors involving tungsten, antimony, graphite, and gallium, as these materials become increasingly vital to national security and industrial independence.

### Summary

Nomi Prins' analysis underscores a pivotal moment in global economics. As central banks pivot toward gold and away from US Treasuries, and as nations weaponize supply chains through export controls, the era of frictionless globalization is ending. While the Federal Reserve attempts to navigate inflation through traditional monetary tools, real-world commodity dynamics and geopolitical strategies are taking precedence. For investors, understanding these distortions—from silver's paper-to-physical gap to the strategic value of critical minerals—is essential for positioning portfolios in an era defined by resilience, reserve diversification, and geopolitical commodity warfare.
