## Insights on Bond Market Dynamics, Bank Balance Sheets, and Systemic Risk from Former Fed Stress Test Lead Ben Chabot

With the 10-year Treasury yield surging past 5.3% and oil prices fluctuating around high marks, concerns across Wall Street have resurfaced regarding the stability of the global financial architecture. As borrowing costs escalate, questions inevitably arise: Could the United States face a run on the bond market? And are commercial banks prepared to withstand prolonged high interest rates, or are we on the verge of another regional banking panic akin to the 2023 collapse of Silicon Valley Bank (SVB)?

To assess these systemic risks, former Chicago Federal Reserve economist Ben Chabot—who led the systemwide team responsible for the Fed's Dodd-Frank bank stress testing models from 2010 to 2024—broke down the mechanics of sovereign debt, bank balance sheet resilience, and the true nature of bond market runs.

### Can the Treasury Control Long-Term Yields?

A central debate in macroeconomics is the extent to which the government can dictate bond yields. While policymakers can influence liquidity at the margin through debt management programs and small buybacks, the long end of the yield curve is ultimately an equilibrium price determined by market supply and demand.

Historically, direct Yield Curve Control (YCC) was implemented during World War II and the immediate post-war period. However, this framework was officially abandoned with the 1951 Treasury-Fed Accord. Capping long-term yields requires the central bank to print unlimited money to purchase debt whenever bond prices fall, inevitably generating runaway inflation. In modern economies governed by independent central banks targeting price stability, direct rate suppression at the long end is neither feasible nor desirable.

Similarly, Treasury buyback programs are designed primarily to improve market functioning and balance sheet capacity for intermediaries—exchanging less liquid "off-the-run" securities for "on-the-run" benchmarks—rather than altering the overall weighted average maturity (duration) of national debt.

### The Anatomy of a "Bond Market Run" and Multiple Equilibria

Unlike retail bank depositors rushing to withdraw cash, a run on sovereign debt in major developed nations manifests differently. Countries that borrow in their own fiat currency do not experience outright default runs, but they can experience what academic literature terms a "slow-moving run."

This phenomenon operates through game-theoretic coordination and multiple self-fulfilling equilibria:
* **The Good Equilibrium:** When investors are optimistic that a nation’s debt trajectory is manageable, they accept low yields (e.g., 2%–3%). Low yields keep interest servicing costs subdued relative to GDP, validating the debt's sustainability.
* **The Bad Equilibrium:** If bondholders anticipate that other investors will demand higher yields to hold sovereign debt, every individual participant becomes rational in demanding higher compensation (e.g., 7%–8%). Higher yields drastically inflate the fiscal deficit, forcing the government to issue even more debt, validating the original fear of fiscal strain.

Crossing psychological thresholds—such as 5.0% on the 10-year Treasury—can act as focal points that shift investor coordination. While the U.S. remains the undisputed global benchmark safe asset, excessive debt issuance and expanding term premiums increase the volatility of these dynamics.

### Bank Balance Sheets: Why Silicon Valley Bank Was an Outlier

The failure of Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023 raised fears that high rates would dismantle the broader banking sector. However, the structural realities of large, Systemically Important Financial Institutions (SIFIs) contrast sharply with SVB's unique vulnerabilities.

1. **Duration Mismatches:** SVB made an unhedged, concentrated duration bet on low interest rates. When benchmark rates surged, the fair value of their long-term securities cratered, wiping out capital as uninsured depositors fled.
2. **Deposit Betas and Net Interest Margins:** Standard banking institutions generally benefit from higher interest rate environments. While their floating-rate wholesale and commercial loans reprice upward immediately, deposit rates (the deposit beta) adjust sluggishly because many retail and corporate accounts are held for day-to-day transactional convenience at near-zero rates.
3. **Exploratory Stress Tests:** Federal Reserve exploratory stress tests conducted in 2023 and 2024 revealed that roughly 30 out of 31 major banks actually earned *more* revenue under rising rate scenarios due to widened net interest margins.

### Stress Testing Evolution and the Impact of *Loper Bright*

Under the Dodd-Frank Act, the Federal Reserve enforces capital buffers by subjecting large banks to "severely adverse" economic scenarios. Historically, traditional recession modeling assumed that interest rates drop as central banks ease policy. Because rising-rate stagflationary shocks were not standard baseline stress assumptions prior to 2022, SVB's vulnerability went unflagged by top-line regulatory models.

The Fed has increasingly introduced exploratory scenarios that test for sharp yield spikes and deposit migration into higher-yielding time deposits. However, regulatory model design faces an ongoing trade-off:
* **Model Transparency:** Following administrative law shifts and Supreme Court precedents such as *Loper Bright*, regulators face higher burdens to provide public notice and transparency around stress models. While academic scrutiny improves modeling rigor, public models allow institutions to optimize precisely to the test, potentially amplifying systemic blind spots.

### Treasury Auctions and the AI Duration Drain

Recent Treasury auctions have experienced notable "tails"—instances where the final clearing yield exceeds the pre-auction market yield—signaling hesitation among primary dealers and investors. 

Rather than a failure to find buyers, auction tails reflect dealers clearing balance sheet space and demanding higher yields to absorb massive duration supply. This supply dynamic is further compounded by the corporate sector:

* **The "Preferred Habitat" Theory:** Institutional buyers like pension funds and life insurers have natural long-duration liabilities and prefer holding long-term debt.
* **Crowding Out by AI Debt:** Massive corporate borrowing to finance artificial intelligence infrastructure and data centers has flooded the long end of the credit curve with duration. 
* **The Role of "Tourists":** Once natural duration buyers are saturated, issuers must attract opportunistic "tourist" investors who do not structurally require long bonds. These investors demand a substantially higher term premium to assume interest rate risk.

### Summary

Surging bond yields reflect a realignment of the Fed's policy path, expanding term premiums, and immense private and public supply pressures. However, this does not automatically signal an imminent 2008-style banking crisis. America's largest financial institutions remain heavily capitalized with strong interest rate hedges and structural advantages in deposit pricing. The primary risk ahead lies in long-term debt sustainability and the self-fulfilling psychology of sovereign bond markets if structural deficits and capital demands keep benchmark yields elevated.
