## The AI Paradox: Market Engine or Future Liability?

The bull market's foundation rests almost entirely on artificial intelligence—and that concentration should make investors uncomfortable, according to Sam Burns, chief market strategist at Mill Street Research.

"The big driver of equities has been the sort of tech and adjacent sectors that are tied to AI," Burns explains. "That's been the big driver of corporate earnings growth and of market gains."

The numbers paint an even starker picture. Take away AI-related companies, and the S&P 500 is essentially flat for the year. Nearly all the index's performance traces back to one sector. This isn't diversification—it's concentration dressed up as a bull market.

What makes this particularly volatile, Burns notes, is the leverage embedded in these positions. "There's been a lot of leverage," he warns. "Trading has been going on. People have leveraged positions in a lot of these names, and that can unwind quickly—as in the same way that it's gone up quickly."

The wild card isn't valuation metrics. Burns recalls the late 1990s, when Alan Greenspan warned about "irrational exuberance" in 1997, yet the market didn't peak until early 2000. "These things can go on much longer than people anticipate," he cautions. The real question: what happens when AI spending expectations get revised downward?

## The Rotation Nobody Noticed

The leadership has already shifted beneath the surface. The "Magnificent 7" has been lagging for months. "A lot of them are the ones that are spending the money, whereas the ones that are making it are the hardware makers—the memory makers and hard drives," Burns observes.

This rotation reveals something important: the AI trade is maturing. Companies building infrastructure are profiting now. The ones spending billions hoping that infrastructure pays off later face a tougher road.

Burns remains overweight technology—"that's where a lot of the earnings growth still is"—but acknowledges the easy money phase has passed. "You're going to get into the choppier, more volatile part of the market cycle coming up in the next 6 to 12 months."

For portfolio construction, he suggests maintaining tech exposure while adding defensive positions: healthcare, selective financials, and industrials. "You want to have some exposure to it for now, because that's where the earnings are coming from. But you need to be ready for that volatility."

## Why the Fed Isn't the Villain—Yet

Here's the counterintuitive reality about inflation and stocks: inflation itself isn't the enemy.

"Inflation is a problem for stocks because it causes the Fed to raise rates," Burns explains. "Inflation itself is not really bad for stocks—at least relative to bonds. Corporate earnings go up along with inflation. Companies just pass along those price increases."

The Fed has been sitting on the sidelines since December, and that's precisely why elevated inflation hasn't derailed markets. "The Fed isn't going to be the party pooper," Burns says. "If the Fed isn't going to raise rates aggressively, then inflation is not great for consumers, but the stock market and earnings aren't necessarily any worse off."

The June CPI prediction markets show traders expect inflation to cool from 4.2% down to the 3.6–3.9% range—still above target but moving in the right direction. The key driver: oil prices.

## Oil's Diminished Sting

Burns expects oil to stay in the $70 per barrel range unless there's a major geopolitical shock. Getting back to $110—where prices were months ago—would "take a lot."

Why aren't oil markets panicking over Middle East tensions? Two reasons:

First, there's more oil available through alternative routes than markets expected. Second, China built massive stockpiles before the conflict escalated and has been running those down while cutting import demand.

"Oil finding other ways to get where it needs to go besides the Strait of Hormuz," Burns says. "Inventories of crude oil globally are higher than people had expected."

The economic impact matters less than people think. "The percentage of income spent on oil-related products is near historical lows," Burns notes. "Even with a bump, it's still not going to be back to where it was 10 years ago."

This is fundamentally different from the 1970s. An oil shock then meant economic devastation. An oil shock now means $80 gas and political headaches—not a recession trigger.

## The Consumer Paradox: Spending Without Growth

The American consumer appears strong—retail sales up 7% year-over-year. But look closer, and the picture gets troubling.

"Real disposable income growth is about zero year-over-year," Burns says. "There's basically been almost no increase in real disposable income."

People are spending more because they're saving less. The personal savings rate has dropped to around 3%—back to the lows of 2022 and well below the historical norm of 5–6%.

Where does this spending power come from? Higher-income households drawing down stock market gains and home equity. Meanwhile, wages are roughly matching inflation—no better, no worse. For the average worker, real income growth has stalled.

Retail sales growth is partially illusory. "A lot of that nominal growth is inflation," Burns notes. Higher prices, not higher volumes, drive the headline numbers.

## What Could Actually End This Market

Burns identifies the real trigger: a reevaluation of AI's economic payoff.

"The question is if the earnings estimates themselves—which are very good right now—get too extreme and have to slow down," he explains. "Is that going to provoke a more negative response if there's a lot of leverage in the system?"

If investors conclude that hundreds of billions in AI capital expenditure won't generate commensurate returns, the chain reaction starts with tech spending, then ripples through the economy.

"Everything else that's coming from that slows down as well," Burns warns.

This is the real risk—not interest rates (most AI spending is equity-financed anyway), not inflation (unless the Fed acts), not oil (impact remains contained). The trigger is a simple question: is all this AI spending worth it?

## Key Takeaways

- **AI dominance masks market breadth weakness**—the S&P minus tech is flat
- **The Fed remains sidelined**, making inflation's direct market impact limited
- **Oil's economic punch has diminished** due to lower energy intensity and stockpiles
- **Consumer spending defies fundamentals**—savings rates at historical lows
- **The real risk is AI spending disappointment**, not traditional financial catalysts

The bull market won't end from the usual suspects. Watch for corporate earnings revisions on artificial intelligence—and act accordingly.
