The United States is not merely stumbling through geopolitical turmoil—it is a "flailing and failing empire" using military force to prop up a role it can no longer afford. That's the stark assessment from Doug Casey, legendary investor, bestselling author of *Crisis Investing*, and founder of the Crisis Investing Newsletter. In a wide-ranging interview, Casey delivered a sobering analysis of where the world stands: a potential war with Iran that neither side can gracefully exit, a dollar destined for debasement, bond markets entering a generational reversal, and a global order that may never return to the stability of the past century. The solution, Casey argues, isn't found in Washington or Wall Street—it's in hard assets, geographic diversification, and a fundamental rejection of the state's claim to manage your wealth.

---

## The Strait of Hormuz Crisis: How Did We Get Here?

The interview opens with immediate urgency: the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 20% of the world's oil flows, has become a tinderbox. At the time of recording, WTI crude had climbed back above $90 per barrel after spiking toward $100. The reopening and closing of the strait has become a daily game of geopolitical roulette, with oil prices swinging based on morning tweets and midnight announcements.

Casey's assessment is unsparing: "I don't see how this is going to end peaceably, entirely apart from the fact that Trump is acting like a schizophrenic. On again, off again, closed again. Everything's going great. We're going to blow up your civilization tomorrow morning. How can you trust or negotiate with a person such as that?"

The core problem, according to Casey, is that both sides have painted themselves into corners. The Iranians cannot back down after sustained attacks—they're now justified, in their view, in demanding reparations for unprovoked aggression. Meanwhile, Trump, Casey argues, used previous negotiations as cover for a sneak attack, destroying any basis for trust.

### The Historical Context Nobody Discusses

Casey is critical of how little Americans understand about Iran:

- In **1953**, the CIA helped overthrow Iran's popularly elected Mossadegh government
- The Shah who replaced him, while implementing some reforms, ran a regime with a notorious secret police
- That regime was then overthrown by the current dictatorship

"So we don't understand what's going on in Iran," Casey says. "The best thing to do is not launch a war against a place that you don't understand. It's not going to end."

### The New Economics of War: $7,000 Drones vs. $3 Million Missiles

The nature of modern warfare has fundamentally changed, yet the US military industrial complex hasn't adapted. Casey highlights the absurdity of current defense spending:

- **Shahed drones** (Iranian): approximately $7,000 per unit, including payload
- **US interceptors**: $1–3 million per missile
- **Ford-class aircraft carrier**: $10.5 billion, plus $5 billion for its air wing
- **US defense budget increase under Trump**: 50%

"We're buying very expensive weapons," Casey notes. "Those aircraft carriers that we send over there can't go anywhere near Iran because when the Ford went near there, they said they had a laundry problem—their toilets weren't working—so they had to run away. But at the same time, there are indications that Iranian missiles or drones did some serious damage to it."

The US is now asking Ford and GM to help manufacture weapons—a first since World War II. But Casey questions what they'll produce:

"The whole character of war is changing. It's like that movie where the computer came up with the answer: the only way to win is not to play."

### Oil's New Premium: A Permanent Toll on the Strait?

The host suggests that WTI and Brent crude may now permanently incorporate a "Hormuz toll" of roughly $1 per barrel. Casey dismisses this as insignificant on a $90 product ("a rounding error"), but he sees oil itself as **underpriced** at current levels.

Here's why:

- Refining capacity has been destroyed throughout the Gulf region
- Pumping infrastructure has been severely damaged
- The longer this continues, the harder recovery becomes
- The longer this continues, the higher prices will go

## Gold, Silver, and the Return of Hard Money

The interview pivots to investment strategy, and Casey remains characteristically bullish on precious metals. The book *Crisis Investing*, published in the late 1980s, recommended gold—and Casey notes that those who followed that advice have been rewarded.

But the reasoning has only strengthened:

### Why Gold Remains the Ultimate Safe Haven

- Gold is the only financial asset that is **not simultaneously somebody else's liability**
- The dollar is "the unsecured liability of a bankrupt government"
- Paper currencies have no intrinsic floor; governments can print unlimited quantities
- Gold's unique characteristics make it "uniquely well suited to be money"

Casey is unruffled by gold's short-term volatility following the February 28th strikes:

"I'm not worried about fluctuations. Gold at $4,800 is not the same asset that it was at $40 an ounce. It's not the same thing. An investment is something that produces more wealth. Gold doesn't itself produce more wealth—it's money."

### The Forced Speculation Problem

Here's the uncomfortable reality Casey identifies: because of inflation and the destruction of traditional savings vehicles, **people are now forced to become speculators just to stay in the same place**.

- Bonds: losing value to inflation faster than interest payments compensate
- Real estate: floats on a sea of debt; higher interest rates crush it
- Cash: loses purchasing power at 5–10% annually
- Stocks: at or near all-time highs by most measures, offering no margin of safety

"Most people confuse speculating with gambling," Casey warns. "They're two very different things, although they look the same to the tyro—to the beginner—which most people are. Most people playing around in Robin Hood accounts have not taken the time to study the market. They're using it as a casino, not as a vehicle for creating more capital."

### Where Casey Is Putting His Money

For those with longer time horizons and tolerance for volatility, Casey suggests:

- **Copper mines**: Despite capital intensity (billions per mine) and government predation, the supply-demand dynamics favor higher prices
- **Silver**: Has been in supply deficit for 5–6 years; it's the most reflective and most conductive mineral—critical for high-tech applications
- **Tungsten**: A "war mineral" that few have considered; Casey mentions a specific tungsten mine he's examining
- **Gold**: Position maintained; expects return to gold-backed monetary systems eventually

## The Death of the 60/40 Portfolio and the Bond Market Reversal

Casey is emphatic: **US government bonds are a relic of the past**. He would short them.

The long-term chart tells the story:

- Interest rates peaked in the early 1980s at 15–18% for US government paper
- They fell for four decades, reaching near-zero (and briefly negative) in 2022
- That was the bottom; rates have been rising ever since
- Casey's prediction: "We're going to see interest rates go back to the levels they were in the early '80s and beyond"

### Why Bonds Are Now Speculative Vehicles

The 60/40 portfolio doctrine—60% stocks, 40% bonds—assumed that bonds provided stability and negative correlation to equities. That assumption is broken:

- Bonds are denominated in dollars, and the dollar will continue losing value at 5–10% annually
- When the 10-year Treasury yield hit near-zero, there was nowhere to go but up
- The bond market is now entering a **secular bear market**
- Even before accounting for inflation, bond interest payments often don't keep pace

"A lot of these bonds are going to be defaulted on," Casey predicts, "either directly because corporations can't pay them, or indirectly—which is what the US government is going to do by inflating its debt out of existence."

### The Mechanism of Government Survival

Casey frames this through a ruthlessly realistic lens: "The prime directive of every living thing, from amoebas up to governments—and especially governments—is to survive any way that it can. And the way governments survive is by taxing. Inflation is a form of taxation, in addition to direct taxation."

The implication: **taxes and inflation will both continue rising** as governments—the US included—attempt to service and inflate away their debt.

## The Failing Empire: Where Does America Stand in 20 Years?

The host poses the fundamental question: What does the world order look like in 20–30 years? For 70 years, American hegemony enforced stability and allowed global trade. Is that ending?

Casey's answer is immediate: "Yes."

"The US can't [police the world] anymore because the US government and large sections of US society are actually bankrupt. They're like Wile E. Coyote—it's walked off the cliff but hasn't fallen yet."

The comparison to Wile E. Coyote is apt: the US is still executing the motions of global dominance while the ground has already disappeared beneath it.

### East Asia: The New Center of Gravity

Casey's geographic prescription is clear:

- **China will continue to rise**, along with East Asia generally
- Tax situations in these countries are better than in the West
- Debt situations are dramatically better
- No massive welfare states creating unfunded liabilities
- Sociologically homogeneous populations
- Not importing millions of migrants from incompatible cultures

Contrast this with Western Europe, which Casey believes is in even worse shape than the US, and the trajectory becomes clear.

### The Cultural Dimension

But it's not just economics. Casey identifies a deeper crisis:

"The values of Western civilization are being washed away and denigrated and disparaged. A lot of this comes through the education system, where people absorb these Marxist values. I think the current trend we're seeing in the world is going to continue and accelerate for some time."

The implication is that even if the US manages its debt crisis, it faces a civilizational one.

### Why the Pentagon's War Production Is a Symptom

The fact that the Pentagon is mobilizing Ford and GM to produce weapons is, to Casey, evidence of decline:

"This is very disturbing because it shows that the US government is looking for more wars and producing massive weapons to fight them. And it's amazing—having raised the defense budget by 50%, who are they going to use those weapons on? Just Iran? Iran is an isolated third-world country. Iran is Israel's problem. And it's like the Trump regime is trying to make Israel the 51st state of the US, which makes no sense."

## The Real Enemy: Why State Power Is the Problem

Throughout the interview, Casey circles back to a core philosophical point: the enemy is not Iran, China, or any foreign power. It's the institution of the state itself.

"One thing that *Crisis Investing* recommended is that the cause of the problems we're having is the expansion of the state. War is the health of the state—when any war is fought, the government grows. It sets up new agencies, new controls, new dictates in the national interest."

100 years ago, the US government lived primarily on excise taxes on alcohol and tobacco, plus import duties. Now it "basically controls the economy." This is true of virtually all governments worldwide.

"Canada is in even worse shape than the US, even though it doesn't have this giant military to feed."

### The Nuclear Proliferation Paradox

The war with Iran may actually accelerate nuclear proliferation—the opposite of its stated goal:

"North Korea—the poorest country in the world—got nuclear weapons. Israel has had them for 60 years, against all dictates of international law. Pakistan has them. Now smaller countries will say, 'We need nuclear weapons. Otherwise, the big guys are going to walk all over us.' So this has been counterproductive."

Casey even raises the possibility that Trump, "as wild and crazy as he is, might actually counterattack with nuclear weapons against them. It's entirely possible."

## Looking Ahead: A Hard Road, But Not Hopeless

Casey ends on a characteristically paradoxical note. While his investment advice is predicated on crisis and disorder, he's not without hope:

"I look forward to the time—and I hope I'm still alive when it comes—when the world stabilizes and I can dump all this stuff and just buy common stocks and productive businesses. That's the way it should work. That's the way the world should work."

His prescription for weathering the transition:

- Get out of fiat currencies to the extent possible
- Diversify geographically—consider Uruguay, where Casey lives ("no obvious military targets, produces lots of food, and everybody wants food")
- Own real assets: gold, silver, copper, other commodities
- Avoid US stocks at current valuations ("the stock market is at or around an all-time high")
- Prepare for interest rates not seen since the 1980s
- Understand that saving in dollars is impossible—you must speculate or hold real assets

"I remain long oil stocks—not too involved in that region—and long gold and other commodity stocks. Commodities, relative to the financial end of the market, are very, very, very cheap, historically cheap. I like to buy things that are cheap and sell things that are dear."

## Key Takeaways

1. **The US-Iran standoff has no clean exit.** Both sides have escalated beyond the point where either can gracefully back down, and the economic costs will continue rising—oil prices included.

2. **The military balance of power has shifted.** Cheap drones and missiles have made expensive platforms like aircraft carriers obsolete, yet the US defense budget has increased 50%. This dynamic is unsustainable.

3. **Bonds are now in a generational bear market.** Interest rates that fell for 40 years have reversed. The 60/40 portfolio doctrine is broken. Investors should avoid long-term US government bonds.

4. **Gold and commodities are not speculative bets—they're preservation of capital.** With all traditional stores of value (bonds, cash, possibly real estate) impaired by inflation and debt, hard assets offer the only practical way to maintain purchasing power.

5. **The Western world order is in structural decline.** East Asia—particularly China and its neighbors—offers better demographics, better debt profiles, and better long-term economic prospects. Geographic and asset diversification outside the West is increasingly prudent.
