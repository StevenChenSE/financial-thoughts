## Hedgeye’s Sam Rahman breaks down the AI capex cycle, Mag 7 shifts, and market outlook

On Thursday, July 2nd, equity markets experienced a notable sell-off, with the Nasdaq dropping approximately 1.5% and the S&P 500 slipping slightly, while safe-haven assets like gold and Bitcoin posted gains. The immediate catalyst appeared to be a combination of profit-taking and a deeply disappointing June nonfarm payrolls report, which showed only 57,000 jobs added against a Wall Street consensus of 115,000. Amid this volatility, investors are questioning whether a broad rotation out of technology stocks is underway or if this is merely a short-term positioning adjustment within the broader artificial intelligence cycle.

Sam Rahman, a portfolio manager at Hedgeye Asset Management, joins the conversation to dissect the current market dynamics. Rather than signaling a broken tech thesis, Rahman interprets the recent price action as a mechanical unwind of heavily overweight semiconductor and AI-related portfolios. With money flowing into healthcare, consumer staples, and financials, the market is undergoing a classic sector rotation that often accompanies late-cycle positioning corrections. Understanding the distinction between a fundamental shift and a tactical rebalance is crucial for navigating the remainder of the year.

### The Tech Unwind and Semiconductor Positioning

The recent market decline is largely driven by an unwinding of extreme positioning in semiconductors and AI infrastructure. Over the past year, investors heavily concentrated their portfolios in chipmakers and memory stocks that serve as the foundational components of data center build-outs. Companies like Micron, SanDisk, Applied Materials, and SK Hynix have been the standout performers, significantly outpacing the broader index year-to-date. However, as these positions became crowded, the market experienced a sharp digestion phase. 

Interestingly, the Magnificent Seven (Mag 7) have actually been the underperformers within the tech sector this year. While they command headlines, these hyperscalers are primarily the "spenders" funding the AI infrastructure boom, whereas semiconductor manufacturers are the direct "beneficiaries" of that capital expenditure (CapEx). The recent plunge in South Korea’s Kospi index, which fell nearly 8% following Meta’s announcement of a deeper move into cloud computing, served more as a trigger for this positioning unwind rather than a fundamental threat to the AI cycle. Money is temporarily rotating into undervalued, defensive sectors, but the underlying CapEx trajectory remains intact.

### Big Tech’s Evolving AI Strategies

As the AI narrative matures, legacy tech giants are adapting their strategies to maintain relevance and profitability. Meta faces particular scrutiny, having pivoted from social media to the metaverse, and now aggressively pursuing AI and cloud infrastructure. Rahman notes that AI poses an existential engagement risk to Meta’s core advertising model, as AI agents on mobile devices could gradually replace traditional social media scrolling. Meanwhile, Alphabet and Google are proactively disintermediating their own legacy search business by integrating Gemini-generated AI overviews. Rather than cannibalizing ad revenue, this shift may increase the value of the top organic search placements, as fewer blue links will appear on the first page. YouTube remains a resilient asset, protected by the irreplaceable value of human-generated content over AI-synthesized video.

Apple, conversely, should be evaluated as an ecosystem and platform company rather than a pure hardware manufacturer. The iOS ecosystem boasts high-margin services that are growing faster than the hardware business, supported by formidable user lock-in. Looking ahead, Apple is poised to integrate advanced AI software features that could trigger a significant device upgrade cycle. Beyond smartphones, the company is reportedly developing home robotics and tabletop interactive devices, positioning itself to lead the next wave of consumer AI hardware.

### Macro Headwinds, Fed Policy, and Rate Sensitivity

The cooling labor market, highlighted by the sub-100k nonfarm payrolls print, raises questions about Federal Reserve policy and broader economic resilience. Rahman emphasizes that a moderating jobs market does not derail the multi-year AI CapEx cycle or the ongoing reshoring investment theme. These are structural, multi-year commitments that hyperscalers and industrial firms have already locked in. 

From a macro perspective, Hedgeye Macro anticipates that inflation and bond yields have peaked. As inflation cools through the remainder of the year, declining yields will alleviate borrowing costs and provide a tailwind for rate-sensitive industries, particularly housing and financials. This macro environment should support broader market stability, allowing growth sectors to recover from short-term overreactions. The Nasdaq 100, which has consolidated sideways since mid-May, is still expected to finish the year positive, supported by reiterated CapEx guidance from hyperscalers during the upcoming earnings season.

### The Future of AI Models and Trading

The competitive landscape for large language models (LLMs) is rapidly evolving. Companies like Anthropic and OpenAI continue to leapfrog each other with iterative model releases, but Rahman expects LLMs to eventually become commoditized as performance gaps narrow. The next major investment boom will likely stem from small language models (SLMs) or specialized language models. These proprietary systems will be trained on closed, industry-specific datasets—such as JPMorgan’s decades of transactional data—creating immense value for enterprises that can leverage internal information securely.

In trading and portfolio management, the rise of AI agents presents both opportunities and challenges. Algorithmic and systematic trading already dominate short-term price action, creating rapid positive feedback loops that can exacerbate corrections. However, human investors retain a distinct advantage on longer time horizons (one to three years). By focusing on durable competitive moats, identifying multi-year S-curves, and capitalizing on special situations like corporate breakups or management transformations, investors can navigate algorithmic volatility and capture structural growth.

### Summary

The recent tech sell-off reflects a tactical unwinding of crowded semiconductor positions rather than a broken AI investment thesis. While the Magnificent Seven have lagged year-to-date, the broader CapEx cycle driving data center infrastructure is expected to continue robustly into 2027. Legacy tech giants are adapting: Google is integrating Gemini to protect search monetization, Apple is leveraging its iOS ecosystem for AI-driven upgrades, and Meta is navigating an existential engagement shift. Macro conditions, including cooling inflation and peaking bond yields, will likely support rate-sensitive sectors and stabilize equities. Ultimately, the AI landscape will see LLM commoditization and a surge in proprietary SLMs, rewarding investors who focus on structural advantages, long-term S-curves, and the ability to capitalize on short-term algorithmic dislocations.
