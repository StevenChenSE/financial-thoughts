The world stands at a precarious juncture. Oil markets have spiked above $90 per barrel following the U.S.-Iran military escalation, the Strait of Hormuz remains a flashpoint that opens and closes with alarming frequency, and the rhetoric from Washington alternates between negotiating table and nuclear threat. Yet beneath the immediate crisis lies something more profound: according to legendary investor Doug Casey, we may be witnessing the terminal decline of American hegemony—and the beginning of a return to the only money that has ever truly held its value across millennia.

In a wide-ranging interview, Casey—who founded *Crisis Investing* and authored the bestselling *Crisis Investing* in the late 1980s—offered a stark assessment of where the world is heading. From his peaceful farm in Uruguay, far from the tensions consuming the Northern Hemisphere, Casey argued that the U.S. has become an imperial power using military force to maintain a position it can no longer economically sustain. The implications for investors, for global stability, and for the nature of money itself are staggering.

## The US-Iran Standoff: How Did We Get Here?

The current crisis did not emerge from a vacuum. Casey traces the animosity between Washington and Tehran directly to a pivotal moment in Cold War history: the 1953 CIA-orchestrated overthrow of Mohammad Mosaddegh, Iran's popularly elected prime minister.

> "We don't understand what's going on in Iran. The best thing to do is not launch a war against a place that you don't understand."

The U.S. replaced Mosaddegh with Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi, whom Casey acknowledges "did some good things for Iran, but was a nasty character with a nasty secret police." That regime eventually gave way to the current Iranian government—which, while Casey readily admits is "a criminal government," notes that "at least 20 or 25 other governments around the world are just as bad."

This historical context, Casey argues, is essential to understanding why Iran will not simply capitulate to American demands. The Iranians have suffered what Casey describes as "unprovoked" attacks, and they are now demanding reparations for the damage done.

## Why This Crisis Cannot Be Resolved Easily

Unlike previous geopolitical flashpoints that faded from headlines, Casey sees no easy exit from the current impasse. Several factors make this situation uniquely intractable:

- **Iran cannot back down** without appearing to surrender to Western aggression—a political impossibility for any government
- **The U.S. has become "unpredictable"** in its approach, with the Trump administration oscillating between peace offers and military threats
- **Trust has been destroyed**—Casey notes that the last round of "serious negotiations" served as cover for a sneak attack on Iranian targets
- **Escalation appears inevitable** as both sides become more deeply committed to positions they cannot abandon

Casey is blunt about the psychological state of current American leadership: "Trump is acting like a schizophrenic. On again, off again, closed again. Everything's going great. We're going to blow up your civilization tomorrow morning. How can you trust or negotiate with a person such as that?"

## The Strait of Hormuz: A Permanent Risk Premium?

The Strait of Hormuz represents one of the world's most critical chokepoints, with roughly 20% of global oil shipments passing through its narrow waters. When tensions flare, markets react violently—and Casey believes this dynamic has fundamentally changed.

One analyst estimate suggests that if Iran were to successfully impose tolls on tankers transiting the strait—charging roughly $1 million per vessel—it would add approximately $1 per barrel to global oil prices. At current price levels around $90, this represents a small "rounding error," as Casey acknowledges. But the real story may lie beneath the surface.

### Oil Market Analysis: Why Prices Will Rise Further

Casey offers a contrarian view of the oil market, disagreeing with the futures curve that shows oil trading cheaper several months out:

> "I think that oil is underpriced right now at $90 a barrel. Both Brent and WTI are in backwardation... which seems to be saying that the market thinks oil is going to get cheaper. I don't think so."

His reasoning centers on infrastructure damage:

- **Refining capacity has been destroyed** in the conflict
- **Pumping capacities throughout the Gulf** have been severely damaged
- **The longer this goes on, the harder it becomes** to restore production

Casey remains "long oil stocks" while avoiding companies too heavily involved in the Gulf region, and has increased his exposure to gold and other commodities, which he views as "historically cheap" relative to financial assets.

## The Return to Gold: Ancient Money in Modern Crises

The interview touched on a theme Casey has championed for decades: the inevitable return to gold as money. While the interview included a sponsor segment discussing precious metals investment vehicles (mentioning Priority Gold's services for adding physical gold and silver to retirement portfolios tax-free), Casey himself laid out the fundamental case:

### Why Gold Makes Sense Now

- **The dollar is being debased**: Casey sees the U.S. being forced to "print money" to finance its debt, with approximately $10 trillion in existing debt requiring rollover plus $2-3 trillion in new deficits
- **Interest rates will rise dramatically**: Casey predicts rates returning to early 1980s levels of 15-18%—levels that would devastate bondholders and real estate markets
- **War destroys currencies**: Historically, major conflicts have been followed by currency debasement, and Casey sees no reason for this pattern to break

> "When real economic conditions deteriorate and policy cannot respond, gold and silver have historically been where sophisticated investors go."

The dollar weakness created by monetary expansion, combined with geopolitical uncertainty, creates exactly the environment where precious metals historically thrive. Casey explicitly differentiates between "paper gold" (ETFs and derivatives) and "physical metal you actually own," indicating a preference for the tangible asset.

## America's Military Overreach: Expensive Weapons That Don't Work

One of Casey's most provocative arguments concerned the current state of American military capabilities. With the defense budget increased by 50% under the Trump administration, the spending surge has not translated into military dominance. Instead, Casey sees a fundamental mismatch between American weapons procurement and modern warfare.

### The Drone Problem

The Iranians have demonstrated that relatively inexpensive technology can neutralize expensive military assets:

- **Shahed drones** reportedly cost approximately $7,000-$35,000 per unit depending on estimates
- These drones have proven "very effective" against sophisticated military installations
- The U.S. responds with **"$1 million or $3 million missiles"** to destroy them

Casey calculates that the cost-exchange ratio is "unsustainable" in any extended conflict. Aircraft carriers—each costing approximately $10.5 billion plus air wing—cannot operate near Iranian waters. The USS Ford, sent to the region, reportedly experienced mechanical failures and withdrew, with some reports suggesting Iranian missiles or drones may have caused damage.

> "We have all this expensive junk which can't be used anywhere—not with the new method of warfare, drones and missiles."

### The Ford and GM Question

The Pentagon has reportedly approached Ford and General Motors about producing weapons components for the first time since World War II. Casey finds this development "very disturbing" for several reasons:

- It suggests the government is preparing for additional conflicts
- The defense budget has already been increased 50%—where is this leading?
- American automakers would produce conventional weapons while adversaries field cheap, effective drones
- Further military spending "is a good way to bankrupt further an already bankrupt government"

## The Greater Depression: Economic Outlook for the Coming Years

Perhaps the interview's most sobering segment concerned the long-term economic outlook. Casey has long predicted what he calls "the Greater Depression," and current conditions have only reinforced his conviction.

### Interest Rates: The Coming Nightmare

Since the early 1980s, interest rates have followed a general downward trajectory, reaching near-zero levels in 2022. Casey believes this trend has permanently reversed:

> "In the coming years, we're going to see interest rates go back to the levels they were in the early 80s and beyond."

This prediction has profound implications across asset classes:

- **Bonds**: Prices move inversely to interest rates. Rising rates destroy bond values, and bonds also carry currency risk (dollar debasement) and default risk (particularly for non-government issuers)
- **Real Estate**: "Real estate floats on a sea of debt." When interest rates rise and credit becomes expensive, real estate transactions freeze and values decline
- **Stocks**: The combination of rising rates, government profligacy, and potential economic depression is "not good for stocks"

Casey explicitly warns: "When the tide goes out, everything goes out with it... if we have another [crisis] like we did in 2008, you can lose 50% of your capital easily."

### Why the Average Standard of Living Will Decline

Casey offers a fundamental explanation for economic deterioration:

- **The world has been living above its means**, borrowing from past production and mortgaging future production through accumulated debt
- **The debt has reached a point where it's hard to service and hard to pay back**
- **People are not saving**—the "average guy is living paycheck to paycheck"
- **Standard of living requires people to produce more than they consume**, which is the opposite of recent behavior

> "I'm a technophile. I love technology. But even with good things happening, I think the general standard of living of the average guy is going to head down."

## The Empire's Decline and What Comes Next

Casey frames the current moment in civilizational terms. The United States, he argues, has transformed from "America"—a unique nation founded on principles of "free minds, free markets"—into an imperial power willing to use military force to maintain global dominance.

> "I see the US as a flailing and failing empire doing destructive things to try to maintain its role at the top of the world using the military more than anything else."

This transformation has global implications. The worldwide rearmament occurring across Europe, Asia, and the Middle East is not, Casey argues, a rational response to a specific threat like China, which he describes as "concentrated on building its economy and minding its own business." Instead, it reflects a generalized instability as the American security umbrella retracts and nations feel compelled to prepare for a more chaotic world.

### Where to Invest in This Environment

Casey's prescription for navigating this environment remains rooted in hard assets and contrarian positioning:

- **Commodity stocks**: These are "historically cheap" relative to financial assets
- **Physical precious metals**: Gold and silver as protection against currency debasement
- **Avoid American financial assets**: The U.S. stock market sits near all-time highs by most metrics, making it "dear" rather than attractive
- **Geographic diversification**: Casey remains contentedly ensconced in Uruguay, which he considers relatively insulated from global conflicts due to its status as a food-producing region with no obvious military targets

## Key Takeaways

1. **The U.S.-Iran crisis may be unresolvable**: Historical grievances, broken trust, and domestic political constraints on both sides make a clean resolution unlikely. Investors should price in a permanent risk premium for Middle Eastern oil.

2. **Oil prices will likely rise further**: Infrastructure damage in the Gulf, combined with limited spare capacity globally, suggests the $90+ price level may prove a floor rather than a ceiling.

3. **Gold and silver are positioned for a major bull market**: The combination of dollar debasement to finance government debt, rising interest rates, and geopolitical uncertainty creates the traditional conditions for precious metals outperformance.

4. **The American military edge is eroding**: Expensive conventional weapons are increasingly vulnerable to cheap drones and missiles, potentially making the 50% defense budget increase ineffective while accelerating fiscal deterioration.

5. **Prepare for "the Greater Depression"**: Rising interest rates threaten both bond and real estate markets, while the stock market's elevated valuations leave it vulnerable to severe correction. Hard assets and geographic diversification offer the best protection in this environment.
