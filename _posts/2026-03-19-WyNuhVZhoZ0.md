*An interview with Gary Wagner of The Gold Forecast at the Vancouver Resource Investment Conference*

---

**Host Daryl Thomas:** Hello everyone. Welcome to VRIC Media, your most trusted voice in metals and mining. Today we have the pleasure of interviewing Gary Wagner of The Gold Forecast. How you doing today, Gary?

**Gary Wagner:** I'm doing excellent. It's a great time to talk about the metals, talk about what's going on and the reality of dollar strength and why that is, and kind of make sense of that to our viewers.

**Daryl:** So, dollar strength—let's start there. Obviously, we've had a huge conflict that has broken out in the Middle East. Typically, people will flock to the safe haven assets. Gold has been the safe haven asset over the past however many years. Central banks have been buying it and such. But we've seen an uptick in the dollar. So why do you think people are headed to the dollar rather than the metals?

**Gary:** They're both considered safe havens. And you're correct, typically they would move to gold before the dollar because the sense was that the dollar is a fiat currency. It's backed by faith in the government, but very little else. And we've seen a shift to the dollar. I think one of the main reasons is that with the recent weakness we've seen in US equities, the dollar is liquid. It's the definition of liquid. You can use it for things. Whereas gold, if you need that capital for something else, you need to convert it to dollars. And that's an additional step.

If you're under the gun—let's say you're on a margin call in futures, equities need a little bit of shoring up in terms of capital—dollars are available to do that. Whereas gold, you have that step of converting gold into dollars and then using the dollars for whatever those needs are. Absolute liquidity will always be in the currency itself. Whether it's fiat or not, that's what you need to use if you're on margin call, if you want to add to a stock position, anything you need real capital for. If it's in gold, yes, it's got that safe haven. It doesn't produce interest, but neither does the dollar sitting blank. But it becomes more available, accessible, and because of that, we've seen people flock to the dollar away from gold.

We're seeing a little bit of that in the central banks. Realize that central banks have been accumulating gold on a very aggressive long-term stance. In terms of China, up to the beginning of this year, they had 15 straight months of adding to their gold reserves. We don't know what they're doing with it now, but we don't have the extent—there's a lag between when that's reported to us. But we have seen a shift, not only with consumers and investors, but central banks.

**Daryl:** Got it. On the topic of the dollar, many people that watch the show—I consider myself part of the crowd—we believe the dollar is toilet paper in a lot of ways. But it's useful, you know, and especially when you have a liquidity crisis. Assets are selling off. Even gold sells off when people need liquidity. The dollar still has a role to play. Do you see this trend of dollar rotation continuing as we have this type of volatility, or do you see it selling off again at some point?

**Gary:** At some point, I think the former balance of gold to dollars in terms of investments will return. Right now there's a great need for liquidity. You can call it toilet paper—I'd go a little less stress on that and say it's fiat, meaning that it doesn't really have intrinsic value—but it is the recognized world currency in which businesses transact their trade, their purchases, their sales. It is the major currency for so many things. Whether or not it's fiat is a topic for another time, but it's still the go-to currency if you want to buy, if you want to accumulate. That's what they're doing it in, is the dollar, more so than other currencies.

Currencies, I believe at least 95% of them, if not more, are not backed by a gold standard. Back in the 30s, we had a gold standard. For every dollar we printed, we backed that with an amount of gold. Then it became modified in the 40s and 50s. The final nail in the coffin was Nixon when he actually took the country off the gold standard. If you look at what gold did from that point forward and what the dollar did, you see the massive effect it had. The dollar is always going to play a significant role, a major role in transactions. But the fact that it is fiat puts a damper on it. You still need to use something to barter. I'm not going to trade a cow for a horse. It's going to be dollars to buy that horse. Dollars are the accepted medium for transactions, for accumulation of wealth, for liquidity.

**Daryl:** Speaking of Nixon, I was recently doing some more research on Charles de Gaulle. He brought ships over and repossessed France's gold. That must have been a sight to see.

**Gary:** Very true. That's not the only country that's been moving gold out of our reserves to their country. Poland moved a lot of it. Germany moved a lot of their gold. The central bank storage for gold used to be the go-to place for other countries to store their gold. I remember when I was younger, you would see images where they would show the storage and they would go, "This is Germany's gold and this is Italy's gold." Not so much anymore. Many countries have moved a portion of their gold reserves that are stored in the US to have it stored locally in their own country.

**Daryl:** That speaks to the trust between nations. That trust seems to have been eroding on a global scale. Let's jump into the gold chart. What's your current forecast for the yellow metal?

**Gary:** Gold has been an incredible performer. We're looking at a daily chart from the beginning of 2025, sitting at around $2,800. From there to 2026, it went up to about $4,400. This year we saw something we haven't seen in a while. A spike from around $3,500 in September 2025 to just over $4,400. But look at these retracements, these corrections as gold moved up.

It wasn't until we hit the top on Thursday, the 29th of January this year, when it topped very briefly over $5,600 an ounce. That, of course, was not sustainable. On a candlestick chart, the real body—the rectangle—is what I look at with utmost importance. The wicks are a sign of volatility. Now we're back around $5,000. To me, that's a fairly critical area. We did break, I believe, $4,970. I am looking at April futures, for those wondering—spot gold isn't at $5,000, but the most active April futures contract is.

Below $5,000, you've got a little bit of support at about $4,900 and major support at $4,800. But to me, this is really a line in the sand. Gold needs to hold. It needs to have a sustained level above $5,000.

**Daryl:** Tell us about these wicks. I'm looking at the big selloff at the end of January. You have a super small body and these huge wicks, and then a huge red candle the next day.

**Gary:** This is massive liquidation. It occurred on the 30th of January. The day prior was the highest it's ever been, just above $5,600. From there down to three days later at $4,400, we saw a correction of well over $1,000. That's huge. I haven't seen that before.

Consider this: On December 31st, the first trading day of January, gold was at $4,376. From there up to $5,600 took almost the entire month of January. Then, in three trading days, it retraced almost the same amount. What took nearly a month to achieve, it gave back in three trading days. That is about the quickest and strongest retracement I've seen.

What that tells me is this run-up was overly optimistic. The price point was unsustainable at that point, and that's why we saw it sell off so quickly. A lot of this has to do with geopolitical events and Fed policy. Right now, we're in a full military stance with Iran. Geopolitically, we're definitely living in a period of severe military action.

**Daryl:** When we saw the big sell-off, some people attributed that to market manipulation, speculators. What are your thoughts on that? Also, leading up to the conflict with Iran, we saw gold start to rise sharply. It went above $5,400 but didn't close above it, and since the conflict started it sold off—reminiscent of the Russia-Ukraine performance of gold in 2022.

**Gary:** I hear that term a lot. Is there manipulation? Probably almost definitely yes, but not in the way many think. It's not large institutional traders forming a huddle and conspiring. Rather, it begins like a domino effect. A large order is placed to liquidate a long. The market reacts, and other large institutional players see that, so they either cover their long positions or initiate shorts.

To me, manipulation would be when the largest holders get together and coordinate. I don't think there's that openness. These large traders don't want their competitors to know their game plan. What we're seeing is a domino effect. Is that manipulation? To me, it's large institutional traders with massive holdings reacting to a major move in the market by another trader.

When we saw that wick near the top—where the low was $5,135 and the high was $5,600—that's just under a $500 daily range. That's pretty intensive. We are getting exaggerated ranges, large market moves, and I think it's because gold is at such a high value that it doesn't take much to have a big swing.

**Daryl:** So the run-up leading to the attack on Iran—do you just consider that noise? Does that detract from the gold bull market we're currently in?

**Gary:** Let me compress the chart. Looking back to 2024, you had gold at about $2,300. There's been very few deep corrections all the way up. This is absolutely a bull market. Right now, we are going into oversold territory on the stochastics indicator. We are still absolutely in a bull market. We haven't really had any technical damage. Technical damage would have to take it much lower considering how fast and how much gold has moved up. There is absolutely no sign of real technical chart damage which would convey a major pivot from bullish to bearish. What I am seeing are corrections in the market, which are so needed because the market gets overextended.

The key level people need to watch is $5,000. It's a key psychological as well as technical number. Gold needs to hold above that level to maintain its bullish structure.
