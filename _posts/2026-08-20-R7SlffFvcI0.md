### The US Treasury's Emergency Move to Cap Long-Term Yields and Its Impact on Global Markets

## Introduction
On August 19th, the US Treasury announced a significant shift in fiscal policy, committing to more than double the size of its government debt repurchases. This "Operation Twist" strategy aims to cap the long end of the interest rate curve amid substantial market stress, where yields had surged to levels not seen in nearly 20 years. The announcement triggered an immediate "risk-on" response: interest rates fell sharply, with the 30-year yield dropping 1.6%, while gold soared 3% to $4,500 and Bitcoin climbed 5.5% toward $70,000. In this analysis, Adrian Day, President of Adrian Day Asset Management and portfolio manager of the Euro Pacific Gold Fund, examines the implications of these moves for the precious metals sector, the broader equity market, and the future of global debt.

## The Treasury's "Operation Twist" and the Bond Market
The Treasury’s decision to double debt buybacks is a direct response to a lack of "real buyers" for long-duration Treasuries. Day explains that this move is a modern version of "Operation Twist," designed to push down long-term rates while potentially increasing short-term rates. The urgency stems from a structural weakness in the bond market: even as issuance at the long end has declined, demand has fallen even further.

Day notes that while foreign participation appears to have risen, much of this is attributed to hedge funds in the Cayman Islands buying for short-term trades rather than long-term holding. The traditional buyers—pension funds, insurance companies, and foreign governments—are increasingly disinterested. This lack of demand reflects fundamental fragility in the system, prompting the US and Japanese governments to intervene in currency and bond markets to prevent a total breakdown in the debt market.

## Gold’s Defiance and Bullish Signals
Despite a strong dollar, rising yields, and high oil prices—typically headwinds for gold—precious metals have rallied significantly since late July. Day argues that this counter-trend movement is a powerful bullish signal, indicating that the market is pricing in fundamental fragility and the likelihood of monetary easing.

Two key factors support the current gold bull market:
1.  **Resilience Against Headwinds:** Gold continued to rise even as the dollar strengthened and yields hit 20-year highs, suggesting that investors are looking for protection against systemic risk.
2.  **Silver and Junior Miners:** Silver has outperformed gold on several timeframes, and the junior gold mining sector (GDXJ) is performing equally well with the majors (GDX). This breadth in the market, where more speculative assets keep pace with conservative ones, is a hallmark of a strong, positive market environment.

Day confirms that he has been buying heavily, noting that his fund has been accumulating gold and gold stocks for months. He views the current rally as a continuation of a bull market that began in 2015, rather than a "dead cat bounce."

## Addressing the "Dead Cat Bounce" Concern
A common skepticism in the market is whether the current rally is a false top, similar to the rallies seen in 2011 or after the 1980 peak. Day argues that the current environment lacks the "mania" that typically characterizes a major top.

In 2011, physical gold ETFs and gold stock ETFs traded at significant premiums for months, indicating extreme speculative fervor. In contrast, while there have been recent inflows into the GDX, the overall participation has been "muted" compared to historical tops. Day suggests that the February peak this year was not a major top because it lacked the public participation and speculative excess that accompany the end of a cycle. Therefore, he maintains a bullish stance, believing the bull market is still intact.

## Inflationary Implications of Debt Buybacks
The Treasury’s buyback program is inherently inflationary if not offset by a reduction in short-term issuance. Day explains that by buying back long-term debt and continuing to issue short-term debt, the money supply continues to expand. This is not a sign of fiscal health, as seen in corporate debt buybacks, but a necessary measure to manage a debt burden that is already under pressure.

This expansion of the money supply is a primary driver for gold. As global fiscal deficits continue to grow—exemplified by recent announcements in Britain of rising unemployment and inflation—investors are increasingly turning to gold as a hedge against the devaluation of fiat currencies.

## The Shift to Copper and Base Metals
While Day remains bullish on gold, he suggests that the current phase of the commodity cycle is shifting toward base metals, particularly copper. In a typical commodity cycle, gold leads at the beginning and the end (due to its monetary nature), but in the middle of the cycle, industrial metals like copper and oil often outperform.

Day points to BHP’s record copper production and profits as a sign that copper has become a primary profit driver. He argues that the supply picture for copper is extremely tight. With new projects taking five years from approval to production, the supply available three to five years from now is already known to be insufficient to meet demand. This structural deficit, combined with the electrification and solar industries, makes copper a compelling investment. For investors who are already overweight in gold, Day suggests it may be a strategic time to rebalance portfolios toward copper and oil.

## Conclusion
The US Treasury’s emergency debt buybacks have provided a catalyst for a broader risk-on rally, but the underlying drivers for precious metals remain strong. Adrian Day’s analysis highlights a market that is pricing in fiscal fragility and monetary easing, with gold and silver showing resilience against traditional headwinds. While the gold bull market appears to be in its middle phase, the shift in leadership toward copper and base metals suggests a broadening of the commodity rally. For investors, the key takeaway is that the current environment favors assets that protect against currency devaluation and those with tight supply fundamentals, such as gold and copper.
