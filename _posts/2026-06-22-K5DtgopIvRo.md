## The Billionaire Investor's Stark Warning About Where Interest Rates Are Heading

Grant Cardone, CEO of Cardone Capital and one of the most prominent real estate investors in America, has issued a alarming prediction about the global economy. In a recent interview, Cardone stated that if the 10-year Treasury yield hits 6%, "the entire planet has a 1930 depression." His warning comes as the long end of the yield curve continues to climb, with HSBC calling the current 4.6% level the "danger zone."

The statement is stark: "Nobody can afford a six," Cardone explained. "If it goes to six, dude, the world's over." His assessment is unequivocal—this isn't hyperbole but a fundamental view of how the global financial system would collapse under such rates. The 10-year yield serves as the benchmark for everything from mortgages to corporate borrowing, making it arguably the most important interest rate in the world.

## Why the 10-Year Yield Matters More Than You Think

Understanding why the 10-year Treasury yield holds such power requires recognizing its role as the foundation of the entire financial system. Cardone emphasized that this particular rate determines the cost of borrowing across the economy. When it rises, virtually every loan—from mortgages to business loans—becomes more expensive.

The United States, Cardone argues, should maintain the lowest interest rates in the world 24/7, 365 days a year. The reasoning is straightforward: the US dollar is backed by "the greatest Navy on planet Earth, equal to none," with no competition for global reserve currency status. This structural advantage means America should leverage cheap rates to help citizens own homes and operate businesses.

## The Real Estate Crisis: It's Not What You Think

Perhaps the most counterintuitive point Cardone made concerns the nature of America's housing crisis. Most people assume the problem is affordability—homes are simply too expensive. Cardone disagrees fundamentally: "We do not have an affordability crisis. We have a mortgage crisis."

This distinction matters enormously. Housing affordability can't be solved by policy because it involves immovable costs: cement, labor, titles, permits, regulations, and time all contribute to the price of housing. You can't simply snap your fingers and make these cheaper. However, interest rates can be changed immediately, and that's where the real leverage exists.

## Canada vs. The United States: Two Very Different Mortgage Systems

The interview took a global turn when discussing Canada's housing market, which Cardone called a "cataclysmic event happening there that cannot be reversed." The key difference lies in mortgage structure: Canada primarily uses three-year and five-year variable rate mortgages, while America uses 30-year fixed-rate mortgages.

In America, approximately 60% of all mortgage debt is locked in at 4% or lower. This creates a situation where homeowners have no incentive to sell: "Why would somebody with a three-and-a-half percent loan sell in a 7% environment?" Cardone asked rhetorically. The answer is obvious—they wouldn't. This mortgage lock-in effect has essentially frozen the housing market.

Canada faces a different but equally severe problem. With three-year terms, Canadian homeowners face refinancing at current market rates every few years, creating massive uncertainty and potential payment shock.

## Is Housing Actually a Good Investment?

Despite his prominence in real estate, Cardone delivered a surprising verdict on residential housing as an investment: "Home is a terrible investment anyway." His reasoning is based on historical data: the average American home has only appreciated about 1% per year over the last 100 years when adjusted for inflation.

Owning a home comes with substantial ongoing costs that eat into any appreciation: roof maintenance, plumbing repairs, floor replacements, termite damage, mold issues, and water damage. "Housing does not produce cash flow," Cardone emphasized. "It's not a business."

This doesn't mean people shouldn't have a place to live. Rather, Cardone's philosophy distinguishes between consuming housing services (renting) and investing in cash-flow-producing real estate assets. "Rent where you live and own real estate that provides you with cash flow."

## The Millionaire Problem: Why $1 Million Isn't Enough

The conversation turned to SpaceX, which has created over 4,800 employee millionaires through its stock programs. Cardone used this as a teaching moment about what it actually means to be wealthy.

"Having a million dollars puts you as close to broke as possible," Cardone stated. Using a hypothetical SpaceX employee named Helen as an example, he walked through the math: if she received $1 million in stock and sold it, she'd pay approximately $400,000 in taxes, leaving $600,000. If she lost her job at age 35 and had 30 years of life ahead, she'd need to stretch that $600,000 across three decades—roughly $20,000 per year. "She dies 17 years before she's supposed to," Cardone concluded.

## The Real Wealth Strategy: Cash Flow Over Cash

Cardone's central thesis centers on cash flow rather than accumulating large sums of money. "You need cash flow," he explained. "That's independent from situations from Iran and inflations." Positive cash flow provides income regardless of what happens in the broader economy or geopolitical landscape.

This philosophy extends to liquidity preferences. Cardone advised against holding liquid assets: "People shouldn't have liquidity. People should have investments that are not liquid that they're sure, with 10 years, 20 years, 30 years from now, are going to 10x or 20x."

Making 6-7% on your money today, according to Cardone, cannot be considered sufficient when governments worldwide are printing money. You need returns that outpace the printing press.

## Cardone Capital's Bold Move: Buying at 2017 Prices with 2026 Rents

In a revealing disclosure about his firm's strategy, Cardone announced that Cardone Capital recently acquired a $600 million portfolio from a major institution. The deal involves adding approximately 2,000 units to their existing 15,000-unit portfolio—an 18% increase.

The purchase price is remarkable: "We're buying this all under replacement cost, about 30% below replacement cost." More specifically, they're acquiring properties at approximately 2017 prices while collecting 2026 rents. This creates an extraordinary margin advantage.

The firm plans to finance the acquisition with Fannie Mae debt and add Bitcoin to the purchase—approximately 1,000 BTC. "We'll be cash flow positive with a real estate Bitcoin fund at 2,000 units, 1,000 Bitcoin cash flow positive," Cardone explained.

## Bitcoin: A Tool, Not an Investment?

On the subject of Bitcoin, Cardone clarified his position: "Am I worried about Bitcoin? The price? No, I'm not worried about it. I'm worried about how much I can get of it."

His logic is binary: if you believe Bitcoin will go to zero, no price makes sense. But if you don't believe in zero, then current prices represent opportunity. Cardone doesn't think Bitcoin is going to zero, and he's positioning his portfolio accordingly—treating it as a real asset that serves as a hedge against inflation and a store of value.

## Looking Ahead: The Fed and Interest Rate Outlook

The interview touched briefly on Federal Reserve policy, specifically the new Fed chair Kevin War, who was about to speak at his first FOMC meeting at the time of recording. Cardone described War as someone who "knows how to walk the line" and believes he's "a Trump ally."

Trump, Cardone noted, "hates interest rates" and would prefer rates at zero if it were his decision. Historically, Trump pushed for lower rates and the Fed complied before geopolitical tensions with Iran caused rates to spike.

Cardone expressed hope that War would prioritize the middle class: "Despite the spike in oil and gas, we have a responsibility to the middle class America to reduce these rates."

## Key Takeaways

Grant Cardone's interview offers several critical insights for investors and anyone concerned about the economy:

First, the 10-year Treasury yield reaching 6% would be catastrophic for the global economy, potentially triggering a depression comparable to the 1930s. Second, America's housing crisis is fundamentally a mortgage problem, not a housing affordability problem—interest rate policy is the real lever to pull.

Third, residential real estate is a terrible investment when viewed purely as appreciation play. The real money comes from cash flow, not price appreciation. Fourth, true wealth isn't measured in millions of dollars but in sustainable cash flow. A million dollars might be "close to broke" when spread across a lifetime.

Finally, during times of market disruption, opportunities exist for those with capital and conviction. Cardone Capital is deploying billions buying at 2017 prices with 2026 rents, using creative financing and adding Bitcoin to the portfolio.

Whether you agree with every point or not, Cardone's contrarian perspectives offer a framework for thinking about wealth, investment, and economic risk that differs sharply from conventional wisdom.
