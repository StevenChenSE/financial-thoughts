## Why Gold Is Rising—and What Comes Next

In a wide-ranging conversation with Danny on Capital Gains, economist and portfolio manager Michael Pento broke down his outlook for gold, interest rates, US fiscal dynamics, and global market fragility as of August 2026. He laid out a scenario where gold’s rally is driven by shifting inflation expectations, softer rate-hike fears, and weakening labor data—while warning that the broader financial system is increasingly vulnerable to a credit-fueled correction.

## Gold, Rates, and the “Imminent Deal” Effect

Pento argued that gold’s recent breakout—trading near $4,280/oz with silver above $61—is not about inflation surging, but about nominal rates no longer facing as much upward pressure.

Key drivers he highlighted:
- Energy and commodity prices tend to roll over when markets price in an imminent geopolitical deal or memorandum of understanding (e.g., around tensions involving the IRGC and the Red Sea/Persian Gulf).
- When energy prices drop, inflation expectations soften, reducing upward pressure on the Fed funds rate.
- Recent labor data reinforced this: a weak nonfarm payroll report, with the household survey showing 507,000 job losses and 1.7 million lost so far in 2026. Pento noted the household survey typically leads the establishment survey.
- In this environment—weak jobs, falling energy prices, easing inflation expectations—both nominal and real interest rates are pressured lower, which he calls “rocket fuel for gold.”

He distinguished clearly between nominal and real rates:
- Historically, gold surges when real interest rates fall.
- Even if nominal rates rise, gold can rally if inflation rises faster, pushing real rates down.
- The most powerful setup occurs when central banks cap nominal rates while monetizing debt, creating inflation and driving real rates sharply negative.

## The US Debt Tsunami and Long-Term Yields

While short-term rate pressure may be easing, Pento was sharply focused on the supply of fixed income and its implications for long-term yields.

He pointed to:
- A $2 trillion annual deficit and $1.2 trillion in annual interest payments (about $43 billion per week).
- National debt at roughly $40 trillion, about 123% of GDP and 720% of government revenue.
- A massive credit bubble: $1.6 trillion in private credit, $1.4 trillion in collateralized loan obligations (CLOs), $1.5 trillion in junk bonds, and roughly $570 billion in AI-related debt.
- A US savings rate of just 2.7%, at historic lows.

His logic was straightforward: with enormous debt issuance and limited savings to absorb it, bond prices are pressured lower and yields higher. He noted the 10-year Treasury yield around 4.67% and the 30-year yield briefly pushing past 5.25%, breaking a long-term resistance line before retesting it.

## South Korea’s Crash as a Warning

Pento used South Korea’s KOSPI meltdown as a cautionary example:
- The index fell 40–45% in roughly four weeks.
- The market was overly concentrated in a couple of mega-cap stocks with extreme leverage.
- He described leverage as a “prescription for forced selling,” and warned that similar dynamics—record margin debt, record nominal debt-to-GDP—exist in the US.

He stressed that the KOSPI correction may not be over, pointing to what he saw as a bear flag pattern, and cautioned that investors who “fall asleep for a few weeks” could end up “holding the bag” in the US as well.

## Scott Bessent, the Yen, and Fragile Global Plumbing

Discussing reports that US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent noted plans to buy $5–10 billion in Japanese yen, Pento framed this as a symptom of systemic fragility:
- A weakening yen forces Japan to sell Treasuries, buy yen, and support its currency.
- The US wants to avoid foreign selling of Treasuries given its own debt dynamics and thin savings base.
- Pento characterized the move as a “Band-Aid,” noting the limited scale and the complications of using euros to buy yen, which raises questions about ECB comfort and sustainability.

He tied this to a broader point: a sinking yen could feed higher US yields, exposing how fragile the global financial system has become.

## Fed Policy, M2, and the Warsh Era

Pento discussed Federal Reserve policy under Kevin Warsh, who he said had already printed around $34 billion since taking office—effectively continuing quantitative easing after Jerome Powell’s aggressive expansion.

Key themes:
- The Fed’s balance sheet drives base money, which fuels bank lending and asset markets.
- M2 is showing signs of accelerating from a new inflection point, while money velocity is topping out after recovering toward pre-pandemic levels—suggesting diminishing returns from monetary expansion.
- If Warsh pulls back on balance sheet growth to combat inflation, liquidity drains, reserves fall, and money markets/repo/commercial paper can freeze—triggering a credit crunch.
- If Warsh keeps printing, inflation becomes persistent, pressuring the long end of the bond market and fueling “hyperstagflation” risks.

Either path, Pento argued, poses serious risks to the current asset bubbles.

## Triple Bubble: Stocks, Housing, and Credit

A central pillar of Pento’s thesis is the unprecedented coexistence of three major bubbles:
- An equity bubble (market cap at 235% of GDP, price-to-sales far above historical averages).
- A housing bubble.
- A credit bubble (private credit, junk bonds, CLOs, AI capex debt).

He noted:
- The market is pricing in 25–30% annual earnings growth—an AI capex-driven narrative that historically only appears in the immediate post-recession rebound, not year after year in normal conditions.
- The ISM Services employment subcomponent had shifted into contraction territory, and ADP showed only 44,000 jobs added, signaling weakness in the largest part of the economy.
- In a downturn, bond ballast may fail to offset a 50% or greater plunge in equities.

He referenced historical “lost decades”:
- 1930–1949: average annual stock market return of 0.6%.
- 1969–1984 (stagflation): 0.5%.
- 2000–2012: 0.8%.

In each period, large drawdowns coincided with very low long-run returns.

## Is Pento Bearish? Clarifying His Stance

Pento pushed back against the label “perma-bear”:
- He is permanently bearish on the structural foundations of the US economy—interest rate repression, money printing, debt, asset bubbles, and the hollowing out of the middle class.
- But he is not investing for a permanent apocalypse. He described himself as net long and managing portfolios actively.
- His last net-short positioning was from early 2022 through October 2022; since then, he’s been net long.

He emphasized active management over passive buy-and-hold:
- He uses a 12-point model incorporating credit spreads, financial conditions, real rates, and forward inflation to gauge when to reduce risk.
- He warned that the next recession will be harder to manage than prior ones, given debt-to-GDP at 123% versus roughly 60% before 2007 and 2000.

## How He Would Position for a Crisis

When asked how he would position if he turned net short, Pento outlined a clear playbook:
- Heavy cash and short-term Treasuries (T-bills).
- Physical gold (not gold miners).
- Dollar shorts and market shorts.
- Minimal to no long equities.

He explained that in a deflationary recession or depression phase, correlations tend to converge toward one as everything is sold off, making cash, short-duration Treasuries, and physical gold the most resilient instruments.

## Final Thoughts

Michael Pento’s message to investors in August 2026 is straightforward:
- Gold’s strength reflects easing rate-hike fears, softer inflation expectations, and weak labor data.
- The US faces a structural debt and liquidity problem that will force higher long-term yields or deeper money printing—and both paths are dangerous.
- A triple bubble in equities, housing, and credit, combined with record leverage and thin savings, sets the stage for a severe correction.
- Passive indexing may not protect investors in the next downturn; active management and crisis-ready positioning are essential.
- He is not calling for a crash tomorrow, but he insists the risks are real, verifiable, and growing—and investors should have a plan before conditions turn.
