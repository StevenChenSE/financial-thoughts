The recent release of September CPI data has sent shockwaves through financial markets, with headline inflation exceeding forecasts at 3.7% year-on-year. While some commentators argue that core inflation remains tame and that energy shocks are isolated, Professor Steve Hanke of Johns Hopkins University offers a starkly different perspective. Speaking with David Lin, Hanke argues that the "inflation genie is out of the bottle," driven primarily by excessive money supply growth and exacerbated by a severe global diesel shortage that threatens to persist until 2027.

## The Monetary Roots of Inflation

At the heart of Hanke’s analysis is the monetarist view that inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." He contends that focusing on core inflation—which strips out volatile energy and food prices—is a form of economic cherry-picking that obscures the underlying problem.

According to Hanke, the broadest measure of money supply, the Divisia M4, is growing at approximately 7.5% per year. This significantly exceeds what he terms the "golden growth rate" of roughly 6%, which is consistent with hitting the Federal Reserve’s 2% inflation target. Because the money supply is growing faster than this threshold, all inflation measures, regardless of how they are sliced, are running above target.

"The inflation genie is clearly out of the bottle," Hanke states. "If you start cherry-picking around in the index... the overall thing went up because overall money supply is going up. That's what keeps it up."

## The Diesel Crisis: A Logistical Nightmare

While monetary policy is the engine of inflation, a specific logistical crisis is driving the current spike in energy costs, particularly for diesel. Hanke points to a severe shortage in storage and a complex web of choke points that keep physical oil prices significantly higher than futures prices.

### Physical vs. Paper Prices
Hanke highlights a critical disconnect between the futures market and physical reality. While futures prices (paper prices) may appear stable or even in backwardation (where future prices are lower than spot prices), the physical cost of oil is much higher. He estimates that physical oil prices are 35-40% greater than the quoted futures prices due to logistical bottlenecks.

### The Choke Points
The crisis stems from two main choke points:
1.  **The Strait of Hormuz:** While Middle East crude exports have recovered to about 85-90% of pre-war levels, the recovery has been uneven, with significant drops in April and August before rebounding in September.
2.  **Logistical Bottlenecks:** Once oil leaves the strait, it faces tanker shortages and high leasing rates. Transferring oil from smaller to larger tankers and routing it to refineries adds significant time and cost.

### Geopolitical Implications
Hanke is particularly critical of the Trump administration’s handling of the diesel crisis. He notes that promises made to European allies and Russia were largely theatrical. European diesel releases were already scheduled since March, and while Trump lifted sanctions on Russian refineries, approximately 45% of Russia’s refining capacity has been destroyed by Ukrainian strikes, which Hanke attributes to U.S.-provided targeting coordinates. Furthermore, China, despite having excess refining capacity, cut diesel exports after a meeting with President Xi, effectively ignoring U.S. pleas for relief.

The International Energy Agency (IEA) confirms this outlook, predicting that the diesel squeeze will last until 2027, with global diesel exports dropping by 700,000 barrels last month.

## Impact on Consumers and the Economy

The convergence of monetary inflation and energy costs is hitting consumers hard, particularly in sectors reliant on diesel.

*   **Transport and Agriculture:** Diesel prices are up about 70% since the start of the war. For farmers currently harvesting and truckers on major highways like I-95, filling a tank can cost over $1,000. This cost inevitably trickles down to food prices, though there is a lag in the CPI data.
*   **Gasoline:** Gasoline prices rose 9.1% in September alone, accounting for more than half of the entire monthly CPI increase. Hanke notes that gasoline is up 34% year-on-year, meaning consumers feel the pinch immediately despite headline CPI being lower.
*   **Real Wages:** Perhaps most concerning is the Atlanta Fed’s wage growth tracker, which shows that real wages (adjusted for inflation) have been falling for six to seven months. This erosion of purchasing power is driving public anxiety and negatively impacting political polling for the incumbent administration.

Despite these pressures, consumption remains robust due to the "wealth effect." As stock portfolios rise, consumers tend to spend a portion of those gains, keeping demand high. Additionally, the AI investment boom is fueling credit demand. Hyperscalers and tech firms are borrowing heavily for data centers, and since bank loans create deposits (money supply), this activity further inflates the money supply, insulating it from interest rate hikes.

## Global Perspectives: The Lesson of Dollarization

To illustrate the severity of monetary mismanagement, Hanke contrasts the U.S. situation with countries facing hyperinflation. Venezuela currently holds the world’s highest inflation rate at roughly 250% per year, while Iran sits at about 140%.

Hanke advocates for **dollarization** as the only viable solution for such economies. He cites his involvement in dollarizing Montenegro (1999), Ecuador (2000), and advising on Zimbabwe (2008-2014). The process involves:
1.  Freezing the issuance of new local currency for 30 days.
2.  Letting the local currency float to establish a fair exchange rate.
3.  Officially adopting the U.S. dollar as legal tender for all government and private transactions.

In Venezuela, the economy is already spontaneously dollarized because citizens refuse to use the bolivar for significant purchases, opting instead for dollars or commodities like building materials. Formal dollarization would crush inflation and restore stability, a lesson Hanke implies the U.S. should heed by controlling its own money supply.

## The Fed’s Next Move

With the FOMC meeting approaching, market expectations have shifted. According to Chicago Mercantile Exchange data, the probability of a rate hike has fluctuated, currently sitting at a 32% chance of a hike and a 67% chance of no change.

Hanke argues that interest rates will remain high until the money supply growth slows. However, the insatiable demand for credit from the AI sector and the government’s fiscal policies make a slowdown unlikely in the near term. He warns that recessions occur when money supply growth becomes anemic, a scenario not currently in sight, which suggests the economy may continue to run hot while inflation persists.

## Summary

Professor Steve Hanke’s analysis underscores that the current inflationary environment is not a temporary blip but a systemic issue rooted in excessive money supply growth. The Divisia M4 at 7.5% growth ensures that inflation will remain sticky. Compounding this is a structural diesel shortage driven by geopolitical conflicts, logistical bottlenecks, and policy missteps, with relief unlikely before 2027. As real wages fall and energy costs soar, the political and economic affordability crisis deepens, challenging the Federal Reserve’s ability to manage expectations without a significant tightening of monetary conditions.
