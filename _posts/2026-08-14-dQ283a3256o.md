## Why This Metals Rally Is Real

Gold has surged above $4,400 and silver is trading above $66 per ounce, marking a sharp breakout from recent ranges. Many market watchers remain skeptical, fearing another fake-out after past disappointments. However, a confluence of macroeconomic and geopolitical forces suggests this rally is legitimate. In a recent episode of Capital Cosm, host Danny and guest Eric Yeung dissect the drivers behind this move, highlighting China’s aggressive gold accumulation, the internationalization of the RMB, shifts in US monetary policy, and the emerging role of silver as a strategic mineral.

## The Japan Yen Unwind and Fed Flexibility

One key catalyst is the unwinding of the Japanese yen reverse carry trade. Scott Bessent’s administration has activated a FEMA swap line with Japan, allowing the Bank of Japan to pledge US Treasuries at the Federal Reserve in exchange for dollar liquidity. This mechanism enables Japan to support the yen without dumping Treasuries, stabilizing global markets.

Eric Yeung interprets this as a form of quantitative easing (QE), which historically supports gold prices. Meanwhile, US inflation data has come in “on target,” giving Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Walsh room to cut interest rates at the next meeting. Lower rates reduce the opportunity cost of holding non-yielding assets like gold and silver, further supporting their prices.

## China’s Gold Strategy: A Five-Year RMB Internationalization Plan

The most significant driver, however, is China. Chinese gold ETFs have set a record high in physical reserves, reaching 258 metric tons—up 35 metric tons in just one month. This surge reflects strong inflows and a strategic push to build gold-backed confidence in the RMB.

Beijing has unveiled a five-year plan to further internationalize the RMB. To achieve this, China is using physical gold as collateral and a neutral reserve asset, encouraging trading partners to hold RMB-denominated assets backed by gold. This “RMB-gold milkshake” model mirrors the US’s historical use of the dollar and Treasuries to facilitate global trade and skim liquidity.

Hong Kong’s new gold hub, positioned as a competitor to COMEX and LBMA, is central to this strategy. As liquidity shifts from Western exchanges to Hong Kong, gold’s price discovery is increasingly influenced by Asian demand.

## Brazil’s RMB Bonds and the Erosion of Dollar Dominance

Brazil is considering issuing RMB sovereign bonds instead of dollar-denominated debt. This move makes economic sense: RMB bond yields are around 2%, significantly lower than US Treasury yields, reducing Brazil’s debt servicing costs. Additionally, Brazil relies heavily on Chinese imports like EVs, solar panels, and electronics, making the RMB a more practical reserve currency.

This trend could spread among emerging markets seeking cheaper financing and reduced dollar dependency. However, political considerations—such as avoiding US retaliation under the Trump administration—may temper some nations’ enthusiasm.

## Silver’s Breakout: Strategic Mineral Status and Supply Chain Reshoring

Silver has not yet confirmed a clean breakout like gold, but its underlying fundamentals are strong. Global silver mining is in deficit, and the US government is moving to establish floor prices for critical minerals, including silver. Eric Yeung forecasts silver reaching $80–$100 within 12 months, with longer-term targets of $200–$1,000 if it overshoots.

The US aims to reshore silver refining, currently dominated by China, which controls about 70% of global capacity. Higher silver prices would incentivize domestic refining projects, such as the new facility in Tennessee. Institutional investors are already positioning for this shift, with ETFs like SIVR and SILJ seeing rising ownership.

Interestingly, higher silver prices could hurt China’s industrial output, as silver is a key input in electronics and green tech. This creates a geopolitical tug-of-war: the US benefits from higher silver prices to rebuild its supply chain, while China faces cost pressures.
