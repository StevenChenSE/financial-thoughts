As Americans head to the polls for the midterm elections, market observers are scrutinizing the potential economic fallout of a divided government. Steve Hanke, professor of applied economics at Johns Hopkins University, offers a stark warning: the probability of a financial crisis is rising significantly due to an overheated AI investment bubble and political gridlock.

## The Rise of Divided Government

With prediction markets indicating a 75% chance that Democrats will win control of the Senate alongside their existing House majority, a "blue sweep" appears likely. Hanke notes that while he previously anticipated Republicans might hold the Senate, recent trends suggest otherwise. This shift is largely driven by voter disenchantment with the current administration’s economic policies and foreign entanglements.

President Trump has signaled his intent to become "Mr. Veto" if Democrats take Congress, a strategy Hanke views as economically beneficial in the short term. "Divided government is in my view the optimum thing," Hanke states. Historically, the U.S. economy performs better when the presidency and Congress are controlled by different parties because legislative gridlock slows down the creation of regulations and interventions that can hinder growth.

However, this stability comes with a significant risk. If the economy deteriorates under divided government, presidents often turn to foreign policy to distract from domestic issues. Hanke warns that Trump could escalate conflicts in the Middle East or against Russia, potentially leading to greater geopolitical instability than currently exists.

## The K-Shaped Economy and Affordability Crisis

The shift in voter sentiment is rooted in what Hanke describes as a "K-shaped economy," where wealth gains are concentrated among the top earners while median families struggle. Data from the Federal Reserve’s Survey of Consumer Finances reveals a stark disparity:

*   **Median Family Net Worth:** Increased by only 2% between 2022 and 2025, compared to a 37% increase between 2019 and 2022.
*   **Top 10% Wealth:** Rose by 31% from 2022 to 2025.
*   **Youth Net Worth (Under 35):** Declined by 23% since 2022, while those over 75 saw a 37% increase.

This disparity is exacerbated by inflation affecting daily essentials like gas and groceries, which Hanke links to the economic blowback from conflicts such as the war in Iran. Furthermore, public sentiment toward tariffs has shifted historically; for the first time, the majority of voters are against Trump’s tariff policies, viewing them as direct attacks on American consumers.

## The AI Bubble and Financial Crisis Risks

A central theme of Hanke’s analysis is the overwhelming dominance of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in current GDP growth. He points out that 96% of the recent 3.4% annualized real GDP growth in Q3 was driven by AI investment. This concentration creates a fragile economic foundation.

Hanke highlights a dangerous disconnect between AI capital expenditure and revenue generation:
*   **Investment Scale:** AI investment is projected to account for over 3.6% of GDP, far exceeding historical infrastructure booms like railroads (2.2%) or telecommunications (1.1%).
*   **Revenue Shortfall:** There is a 58% shortfall between projected revenues and the cash flow needed to justify current investment levels at a 12% rate of return.
*   **Circular Financing:** Approximately 55% of AI investment is circular, financed by hyperscalers lending to each other, similar to vendor financing in the auto industry.

According to research published in *The Journal of Finance* by Robin Greenwood and colleagues, the combination of a credit boom and exploding asset prices significantly elevates the risk of financial crises. Hanke estimates that this pattern has raised the probability of a U.S. financial crisis from a normal baseline of 7% to **40%**.

## The Wealth Effect and Consumption Collapse

If the AI bubble bursts, Hanke predicts a scenario reminiscent of the dot-com crash of 2000-2002, where the NASDAQ fell approximately 75%. A correction in the tech sector could lead to a 40% drop in the overall market. This would trigger a severe "wealth effect" on consumption.

"The wealth effect works through consumption," Hanke explains. For every $100 decline in wealth, consumption drops by about $3. Since consumption accounts for two-thirds of GDP, a significant market correction would drag down the entire economy. Unlike the 2008 housing crisis or the dot-com bust, this potential collapse is underpinned by massive government debt and global liquidity issues.

## Global Liquidity and European Instability

Hanke also draws parallels between U.S. risks and emerging crises in Europe, particularly France and Germany. In France, bond yields have risen higher than those of Greece, signaling deep investor concerns about fiscal sustainability. With combined public and private debt reaching approximately 225% of GDP, France is highly leveraged. Hanke warns that Japanese pension funds, which hold significant amounts of French debt, may begin selling to repatriate capital into domestic yen-denominated assets, spiking yields further.

In Germany, "economic suicide" policies—such as abandoning nuclear power and cutting off cheap Russian energy imports—have led to de-industrialization and high manufacturing costs. With gas storage at only 62% full and political elites disconnected from public struggles, social unrest is rising. Hanke predicts a high probability of recession for both Germany and France in the coming year.

## Monetary Policy and Money Supply

Regarding U.S. liquidity, Hanke argues that there is currently "too much" money in the system. The M4 money supply is growing at 7.4%, exceeding his "golden growth rate" of 6% consistent with a 2% inflation target. Additionally, money velocity is accelerating.

According to the quantity theory of money ($MV = PY$), increases in both money supply (M) and velocity (V) drive up nominal GDP ($PY$). This has resulted in elevated inflation and real growth figures that mask underlying structural weaknesses. Hanke suggests that tighter monetary policy is required, either through raising Fed funds rates by 275 basis points (a Keynesian approach) or slowing money supply growth to 5-6% (a monetarist approach).

## Conclusion

The upcoming midterm elections represent more than a political shift; they are a potential catalyst for broader economic instability. With AI investment creating bubble-like conditions, government debt soaring globally, and consumption vulnerable to wealth shocks, Hanke’s assessment is clear: the probability of a financial crisis has risen dramatically. Investors should watch not just election results, but the interplay between foreign policy escalation, monetary tightening, and the sustainability of tech-driven growth.
