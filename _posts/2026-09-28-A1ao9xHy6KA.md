## The Sovereign Debt Squeeze and Yield Pressures

The discussion opened with technical observations on the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield, noting the emergence of a multi-year "cup and handle" chart pattern. While technical formations can be altered by Federal Reserve intervention, the underlying fiscal arithmetic presents severe constraints.

With the U.S. national debt crossing $40 trillion, debt servicing costs have reached critical proportions. At higher interest rates, every incremental 100-basis-point increase in debt refinancing costs absorbs approximately 9% of total federal tax receipts (based on roughly $4.5 trillion in annual receipts). When debt rolls over into higher rates:

- Significant portions of national revenue are diverted solely to pay interest before accounting for mandatory spending, healthcare, Social Security, or defense.
- Policymakers are caught in a trap between letting market rates rise to intolerable servicing levels or resorting to yield curve control and money printing, which risks debasing the currency.
- Alternative sovereign playbooks include financial repression, revaluing gold reserves to create a neutral asset buffer, or running inflation moderately hot to gradually erode real debt burdens.

The conversation highlighted historical precedents such as the freezing of Russian foreign reserves by the European Union and the U.S., framing asset freezes as de facto selective defaults that reshape counterparty risk in a multipolar world.

## The Mechanics of the Global Diesel Crisis

The most acute real-economy bottleneck identified is the structural shortage of refined diesel fuel. Doomberg emphasized that market commentary often conflates raw crude oil with finished petroleum products.

```
Crude Oil (Feedstock) ──> [ Refinery Processing ] ──> Gasoline / Diesel / Jet Fuel
```

Crude oil possesses no direct economic utility until it is processed by refineries. Consequently, when refining capacity is knocked offline—such as recent Ukrainian strikes on Russian refinery facilities—the supply of finished diesel drops sharply while local crude demand softens, temporarily depressing raw crude prices even as refined product margins soar.

### The Mathematics of Competitive Diesel

To understand why localized supply shocks cause global price surges, Doomberg outlined the breakdown of global diesel flows:

1. **Total Oil Market:** ~100 million barrels per day (bpd).
2. **Global Diesel Production:** ~25 million bpd.
3. **Domestic Consumption:** Approximately 75% of diesel is consumed within the nation where it is refined.
4. **Contestable Export Market:** Only ~7 million bpd of refined diesel represents the global seaborne "jump ball" auctioned internationally.

Because the contestable seaborne market is limited to roughly 7 million bpd, a loss of 800,000 bpd from Russian exports removes more than 11% of the contestable global supply. Combined with shipping bottlenecks, elevated Very Large Crude Carrier (VLCC) freight rates, and disruptions around the Red Sea and Strait of Hormuz, the global pool of tradeable diesel faces severe tightness.

## Policy Options: Banning vs. Toggling Exports

With diesel prices soaring ahead of agricultural harvest seasons, potential presidential emergency powers could be invoked to stabilize domestic supply. Doomberg clarified that a complete diesel export ban would disrupt global alliances and domestic operations, whereas an **export toggle mechanism** presents a viable regulatory alternative.

Under an export toggle:
- Refiners would be mandated to allocate a defined volume of diesel to domestic markets to satisfy local baseline demand before accessing high-margin export markets.
- Complementary administrative actions, such as temporary waivers for the Jones Act, could assist coastal delivery logistics.

- As supply expansions gradually come online and geopolitical tensions eventually normalize, capacity often outstrips normalized demand, triggering sharp price reversals.
