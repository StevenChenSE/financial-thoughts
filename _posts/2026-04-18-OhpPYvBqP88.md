## The numbers don't lie—and they show a government drowning in red ink

The US economy stands at a precarious inflection point. The University of Michigan's consumer sentiment index has hit an all-time historical low. Inflation is ticking upward again. Oil prices hover around $100 per barrel as tensions in the Middle East intensify. And beneath it all, a fundamental question looms: Is the American government solvent?

That's precisely what Professor Steve Hanke—applied economist at Johns Hopkins University—argued in a controversial Fortune magazine article co-authored with David M. Walker, former Controller General of the United States. Their headline was unambiguous: *The Treasury Just Declared the US Insolvent.*

The article went viral on social media. It also drew scrutiny from fact-checkers at FactCheck.org, who pushed back on the characterization.

"The Treasury did declare insolvency because they produce financial statements," Hanke explained during a recent appearance on the David Lin On Markets show. "The assets of the US government come in at $6.1 trillion and the liabilities are $136.2 trillion. By definition, you're insolvent."

FactCheck.org acknowledged that the experts they consulted broadly agreed with Hanke and Walker's core claims—namely, that the government's fiscal policy is imbalanced and in need of change. But they disputed the framing that the Treasury had "declared" anything new. The government's liabilities have exceeded its assets in Treasury annual reports going back decades.

"The fact checkers are simply displaying their ignorance and stupidity more than anything else," Hanke responded bluntly. "The Treasury's financial statements make it unambiguously clear that they are insolvent. They don't have to announce it. They are."

To be clear, Hanke isn't suggesting the government has filed for bankruptcy. His point—lost in the fact-check shuffle—is that the numbers tell the story whether anyone acknowledges it or not.

"These financial statements come out every year, mandated by law," Hanke noted. "Unfortunately, most fact-checkers can't read financial statements. It requires a great deal of skill."

## What Happens When the Government Goes Bust?

For ordinary Americans, insolvency conjures images of bankruptcy courts and destroyed credit scores. The federal government operates under different rules entirely.

"The government is indifferent because there's one big difference," Hanke said. "They can run the printing press and produce money."

Many developing nations have gone insolvent and simply refused to pay their debts. The United States, as a global superpower with reserve currency status, has a more attractive option: print its way out. The dollar would devalue, but obligations could theoretically be met.

"The statements are what the statements are, and things will keep plunking along as they have been over the years," Hanke observed. But he warned that the Congressional Budget Office—a bipartisan organization—shows deterioration accelerating.

"We've got fiscal folly taking place in Washington DC, particularly over the last three administrations," Hanke said. "Trump one, Biden, and Trump two—all have engaged in fiscal folly, running huge deficits and massive increases in government expenditures."

The brutal arithmetic: deficits are simply deferred taxes.

"Those will be paid by future generations," Hanke emphasized. "They'll either be paid by increasing taxes on April 15th, or they'll be paid with an inflation tax. One of the two."

Hanke and Walker have proposed a constitutional amendment to impose debt limits, harkening back to Adam Smith's principles from *The Wealth of Nations*—published in 1776, the same year as the Declaration of Independence. Smith's dictum was simple: no government spending unless there are taxes to finance it.

"We're spending a lot more money than we're taxing, creating a deficit that ultimately is going to have to be paid by somebody in the future who isn't even voting on it today," Hanke said. "They're not even alive today. So we can argue the financing of this is fundamentally immoral."

## The Iran War Price Tag: A Trillion Dollars—and Counting

While the Treasury's insolvency has been brewing for decades, a new crisis threatens to accelerate the decline. The White House budget director, Russell Vought, recently testified before the House Budget Committee that his office could not estimate the cost of the Iran war.

"We're not ready to come to you with the request," Vought said. "We're still working on it. We're working through to figure out what's needed."

Harvard University has attempted to fill that gap. Professor Linda Bilmes, a public policy expert at the Harvard Kennedy School, estimated the conflict could cost upwards of $1 trillion.

"They're entering a war and they have no idea what the cost is going to be," Hanke said. "That's how irresponsible this government is. They're talking about the budget. They don't know how much more insolvent this will make the country."

Hanke suspects Harvard's $1 trillion figure may be conservative—a narrow focus on direct budget costs rather than broader economic consequences.

"I can assure you the cost, broadly measured, will be much, much greater than a trillion," he said.

The professor cited several factors that will compound costs:

- **The Strait of Hormuz blockade** is disrupting global supply chains. A third of the world's fertilizer passes through the Strait, and with supplies choked off, agricultural input costs have surged over 50%. Farmers are cutting purchases; food prices will follow.

- **Oil infrastructure damage** is severe. Approximately 80 refineries have been damaged, with a third severely impaired. Repairs alone will take roughly two years even if hostilities cease immediately.

- **The blockade isn't even hurting Iran as intended.** The country has approximately 180 million barrels of oil currently in transit, with 90% destined for China. Even with the blockade in place, that oil will reach its destination at high prices with minimal discounts—sustaining Iranian revenue through at least July.

"This blockade would have to stay until the end of July to actually start denting the finances of Iran," Hanke noted. "So all these damage costs—which I don't think were included in that Harvard number—all of these costs would have to be included in any global cost of the war."

Hanke drew parallels to previous military adventures: Iraq, Vietnam, Libya. Each entered without clear understanding of costs or benefits.

"You make a decision to do something and you don't know what the benefits are and what the costs are," he said. "That's delusional."

## Inflation: The Genie Is Out of the Bottle

As if geopolitical turmoil weren't enough, inflation is reasserting itself. The latest headline inflation number came in at 3.3%—the highest reading since 2024.

Hanke wasn't surprised. He'd been warning about rising inflation since late fall.

"The money supply, broadly measured by M2, has been accelerating," he explained. "That's why I said the Fed was not going to be able to get the inflation genie back in the bottle."

Many commentators attributed the recent uptick to oil prices spiking due to the Middle East conflict. Hanke dismisses this explanation entirely.

"Oil price spikes cause relative prices of oil to go up, but they do not cause the overall price index to go up," he insisted. "The only way the overall index can go up is if the money supply accelerates prior to whatever date you're reading the inflation number for."

His proof: Japan in 1979. The world faced an oil crisis. Oil prices skyrocketed in Japan. Yet Japanese inflation *declined* from 4.1% in 1977 to 3.7% in 1978.

"Why? Because the rate of growth in the money supply from 1973—when they had the last oil price spike—was cut in half between 1973 and 1979 in Japan," Hanke said. "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon."

When asked about the Fed's balance sheet—which is still contracting at around 0.5% year-over-year—Hanke redirected attention to where the real action is: commercial banks.

"The Fed only contributes about 20% of the growth in the money supply," he explained. "The rest is privately produced money by commercial banks. If you look at commercial bank credit, that's growing at about 6.7% year-over-year right now. That is above my golden growth rate—about 6%—which is consistent with hitting a 2% inflation target."

Bank earnings are surging, which builds capital, which expands lending capacity. Deregulation under the Trump administration is further freeing up reserves. Every loan creates a deposit; every deposit adds to the money supply.

"All things are looking like this is going to continue," Hanke warned. "The money supply acceleration will continue, and they will not be able to contain and get the genie back in the bottle."

## Consumer Sentiment at Rock Bottom

The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index has hit its lowest level in the 70-year history of the survey. Americans are feeling the pinch of higher prices, cutting back on spending, and worrying about their personal finances.

"There's a gap between where inflation expectations are and where actual financial concerns are," Hanke observed. "People are cutting back. The inflation expectations tick downward, but high-price concerns for personal finances keep climbing."

He predicted that gap will close—with inflation expectations rising to meet reality.

"I am concerned about inflation," Hanke said. "Given the course the money supply has been taking, the inflation genie is not going back in the bottle."

The compounding pressures are significant: gas prices elevated by supply disruptions, food costs rising due to fertilizer shortages, and a housing market where mortgage rates compound affordability challenges.

"Everything is funneling through into affordability," Hanke said. "And I don't think Trump's advisors anticipated any of this. They didn't see the consequences of going to war. The budget director can't even give a cost number. They were flying blind."

## Hanke's Misery Index: Where Does the US Rank?

Each year, Hanke publishes his own ranking of national economic misery. The methodology is straightforward:

- **Add:** Inflation rate + Unemployment rate (doubled) + Bank lending rate
- **Subtract:** GDP per capita growth rate

Higher scores mean more misery. In 2025, where does the United States land?

"119," Hanke said. "They're not very good today."

The least miserable country in the world? Taiwan—low unemployment, low inflation, low lending rates, and solid GDP growth per capita.

The United States ranks below Cameroon (120) but above Canada (98)—though Hanke noted Canadians are suffering from severe affordability crises in housing and groceries.

"Southeast Asian tigers do pretty well," Hanke added. "Singapore, Hong Kong—where is Hong Kong? 158. Slightly less miserable than the US."

Hanke believes the US trajectory is downward.

"The US continues to deteriorate," he said. "It's been deteriorating for some time, and I think it will continue to deteriorate."

## The AI Stock Hype Problem

In closing, Lin asked Hanke about a curious market phenomenon: companies rebranding or pivoting to AI and seeing their stock prices soar by triple-digit percentages overnight. One shoe retailer, Alberts, announced a pivot to AI compute infrastructure—and its stock exploded. A social media company rebranded as MYMI and saw shares surge from $260 to $16.

"This is hype," Hanke said flatly. "There's no way their profits are going to increase by as much as implied by those huge increases in stock prices."

The stock market, he explained, is a present-value machine—it calculates the present value of future free cash flow.

"There's no way free cash flow is surging by that amount just because of the adoption of AI," Hanke said. "If I was following through, those two stocks would be prime shorts. I think they're primed for shorting because there's just no way you're going to get that kind of improvement in valuation just because of adoption of AI."

Hanke acknowledged he uses AI himself—as a research assistant that can accomplish in an hour what would take a human assistant a week or more. But he emphasized knowing what you're doing.

"You've got to know what you're asking, and you've got to know what you're doing," he said. "And it's pretty easy to detect errors, too."

Aggregate productivity data tells the real story: 3% annual productivity growth in 2024, dropping to 2.1% in 2025, and actually declining last year.

"All of this hype around AI and people adopting it and increasing productivity—if you aggregate and look at the economy as a whole, it's not comporting with what we're getting out of Silicon Valley hype," Hanke said. "Or as I call it, Silly Valley hype."
