## Professor Steve Hanke Debates Treasury Solvency, War Costs, and Economic Doom

The American economy stands at a critical juncture. Consumer sentiment has plummeted to historic lows. Inflation is climbing again. And the ongoing conflict in the Middle East—particularly the naval blockade of the Strait of Hormuz—is driving oil prices upward, compounding an already precarious fiscal situation.

To make sense of it all, we turned to Professor Steve Hanke, the applied economist from Johns Hopkins University known for his unflinching analysis of government finances and monetary policy.

---

## The Insolvency Controversy

The conversation began with a recent media firestorm touched off by an article Hanke co-authored with David M. Walker, former Comptroller General of the United States. The piece, published in Fortune magazine under the headline "The Treasury Just Declared the US Insolvent," went viral on social media—but not everyone was convinced.

Fact-checkers at FactCheck.org weighed in, arguing that the headline was misleading. They noted that the Treasury hadn't literally declared bankruptcy, and that government liabilities exceeding assets had been a feature of Treasury annual reports for decades.

The experts they consulted broadly agreed with the underlying diagnosis: the government's fiscal policy is imbalanced and in need of change. But they pushed back on the characterization.

Hanke's response was swift and unsparing.

"The fact-checkers are simply displaying their ignorance and stupidity more than anything else," he said. "The Treasury did declare insolvency because they produce financial statements, and the former controller of the United States Dave Walker and I analyzed those. They're a huge volume of these financial statements that come out every year, mandated by law."

The numbers are unambiguous, Hanke explained. The assets of the US government come in at $6.1 trillion, while liabilities total $136.2 trillion.

"By definition, you're insolvent," he said flatly. "Anyone watching this program must be getting a laugh out of the fact-checkers."

The issue, Hanke suggested, is that reading these financial statements requires specialized skill that many self-appointed fact-checkers simply don't possess.

---

## What Happens When Government Goes Bust?

But isn't government insolvency different from a business bankruptcy? After all, the US government doesn't file Chapter 11.

"The government's indifferent because there's one big difference," Hanke replied. "They can run the printing press and produce money."

While many developing countries have defaulted on their debts, the United States—a global superpower with reserve currency status—has the ability to print its way out of obligations, however devalued that currency might become.

"Technically, they could print their way out of a wet paper bag if they had to," Hanke said. "They would continue to pay their obligations, of course, in devalued dollars."

But the financial statements tell a troubling story, and the Congressional Budget Office projections are even more alarming. According to Hanke, things are deteriorating badly.

"We've got fiscal folly that's taking place in Washington DC, particularly the last three administrations," he noted. "You got Trump one, then you got Biden, and then you've got Trump two. All three of those administrations have engaged in fiscal folly—running huge deficits, huge increases in government expenditures."

The core problem, in Hanke's view, is that deficits are simply deferred taxes. "Those will be paid by future generations, and they'll either be paid by increasing the taxes paid on April 15th, or they'll be paid with an inflation tax. One of the two."

---

## A Constitutional Fix?

To address the structural imbalance, Hanke and Walker have proposed amending the US Constitution to include debt limits—a move that would restore the original intent of the nation's founders.

"The founders never wrote it into the Constitution, but the practice was to follow Adam Smith and *The Wealth of Nations*—published in 1776, the same year as the Declaration of Independence," Hanke explained. "Smith's principle was: no government spending unless there were taxes to finance it, with taxes matching expenditures."

Under such a framework, government spending would require equivalent taxation. Deficits over the business cycle would be prohibited.

## The $1 Trillion War Nobody Budgeted For

The conversation then turned to the ongoing conflict with Iran—a topic that has profound fiscal implications.

Russell Vought, the White House budget director, recently testified before the House Budget Committee that he could not estimate the cost of a potential war with Iran. "We're not ready to come to you with the request," he said. "We're still working on it. We're working through to figure out what's needed."

Meanwhile, Harvard University's Linda Bilms, a public policy expert at the Harvard Kennedy School, has projected that such a war could cost upwards of $1 trillion.

For Hanke, the admission was remarkable.

"They're entering a war and they have no idea what the cost is going to be," he said. "That's how irresponsible the government is. They're talking about the fiscal cost, and that's what we were talking about with regard to the solvency of the United States. It's going to make the US more insolvent, but he doesn't know how much more insolvent."

Hanke drew stark historical parallels.

"How about George W. Bush when he went to war in Iraq, another big disaster?" he asked. "They didn't know what the benefits were. Well, there weren't any benefits. The benefits were negative to start with, and then you pile on trillions of dollars worth of cost on top of that. How about the Vietnam War? How about our overthrow of Gaddafi in Libya? That was another gem."

Libya, in particular, represents a cautionary tale. After years of chaos and civil war, the country remains in a state of anarchy, with the United States now reportedly supporting the incumbent government against Russian-backed rebels.

"We've established a lot of stability and democracy and rule of law," Hanke said sardonically. "It's just one disaster after the next."

He believes the broader costs of an Iran conflict would far exceed the Harvard estimate.

"I can assure you the cost, broadly measured, will be much, much greater than a trillion," he said.

## The Morality of Deferred Debt

Beyond the economic calculations, Hanke sees a moral dimension to the fiscal recklessness.

"We're actually spending a lot more money than we're taxing, creating a deficit that ultimately is going to have to be paid by somebody in the future who isn't even voting on it today," he argued. "They're not even alive today. So we know that future generations, or young people now who are below voting age, are going to have to pay for this stuff."

The interviewer raised a counterpoint: proponents argue the war makes future generations *safer* by eliminating Iran's nuclear ambitions and disrupting its support for terrorism.

"You just perfectly articulated the propaganda that's been coming out of Israel for 47 years," Hanke replied. "The fact of the matter is they have repeatedly indicated, at least when the Supreme Leader before they assassinated him, that they would not be producing these weapons."

The notion that Iran is on the verge of nuclear capability and imminent threat to the United States is "ridiculous on the face of it," Hanke maintained. "It's not a serious argument."

## Inflation: A Monetary Phenomenon

Shifting to domestic concerns, the discussion addressed the resurgence of inflation. The latest figures show headline inflation at 3.3%—the highest since 2024. Consumer sentiment has fallen to an all-time low in the University of Michigan's 70-year survey, with respondents increasingly citing high prices as a strain on personal finances.

Hanke has been consistent in his diagnosis: inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon.

"The money supply broadly measured by M2 has been accelerating," he explained, "and that's why I said the Fed was not going to be able to get the inflation genie back in the bottle."

Critics have attributed the recent uptick to oil price spikes from the Middle East conflict. But Hanke rejects this framing.

"Oil price spikes cause relative prices of oil to go up, but they do not cause the overall price index to go up—at least not in a sustained way," he said. "The only way the overall index can go up is if the money supply accelerates prior to whatever the date is you're reading the inflation number for."

He cited Japan's experience during the 1979 oil crisis as a natural experiment.

"Prices of oil skyrocket in Japan, and inflation in Japan actually came down," Hanke noted. "Why? Because the rate of growth in the money supply from 1973, when they had the last oil price spike, was cut in half between 1973 and 1979."

The lesson, he argues, is that oil shocks raise *relative* prices but not the overall price level unless preceded by monetary expansion.

"If you have a fixed supply of money and the price of gasoline and diesel fuel goes up and people have to spend more on that, they have less money to spend on other things," he explained. "So the prices of those things don't go up as fast, and the overall price index remains stable."

The common confusion about 1970s stagflation stems from a misreading of causation, according to Hanke. While oil shocks and inflation coincided, the root cause was monetary expansion that preceded both crises.

---

## The Real Driver of Money Supply

When the conversation turned to the Federal Reserve's balance sheet—which has been contracting, though at a slower pace—Hanke clarified that the Fed is only a partial contributor to overall monetary conditions.

"The Fed is a very small contributor to the overall money supply, M2," he said. "They only contribute about 20% of the growth. The rest is privately produced money by commercial banks."

The key indicator, in Hanke's view, is commercial bank credit, which is growing at approximately 6.7% year-over-year—"above Hanky's golden growth rate of about 6%," which is the rate consistent with a 2% inflation target.

Moreover, this growth is likely to accelerate. Bank earnings have been robust, which increases bank capital and creates more capacity for lending. Deregulatory moves by the Trump administration and the Fed are also freeing up reserves.

"When banks make loans, what happens?" Hanke asked. "The credit goes into the checking account of whoever they're making a loan for, and that becomes a liability of the banks—a checking deposit counted as part of M2."

The result, he predicts, is continued monetary expansion and persistent inflation.

## Strait of Hormuz: Economic Time Bomb

Beyond the immediate conflict costs, the naval blockade of the Strait of Hormuz is creating additional economic headwinds.

"A third of the world's fertilizer comes through the Strait of Hormuz," Hanke noted. "It hasn't been coming through, and fertilizer prices are up over 50%. So farmers aren't buying fertilizer. We're going to have food problems. Food prices will go up."

The blockade itself may be less effective than its architects anticipated. Iran has approximately 180 million barrels of oil currently in transit—90% of which is destined for China. Even with the blockade in place, these shipments will continue reaching their destination through July, meaning the economic pressure on Tehran is delayed.

Meanwhile, strikes on oil facilities—by Israel and the US, as well as Iranian counterattacks—have damaged roughly 80 refineries, with a third severely affected.

"It will take about two years just to repair those facilities," Hanke said. "So we have a two-year runway now, even if nothing else happened today, before things come back to where they were prior to the war."

## Hanke's Misery Index: Where Does the US Stand?

Looking at the broader global picture, Hanke pointed to his annual Misery Index—a composite measure that ranks countries by economic distress.

The index combines four variables:

- **Inflation rate** (higher = more misery)
- **Unemployment rate × 2** (weighted more heavily)
- **Bank lending rate** (higher borrowing costs = more misery)
- **Minus GDP per capita growth** (higher growth offsets misery)

According to the 2025 rankings, the United States sits at **119** on the list—among the most miserable economies in the world. Canada fares even worse at **98**, while the least miserable nation is **Taiwan**, followed by other Southeast Asian economies like Singapore and Hong Kong.

"The people of Taiwan are happy," Hanke observed. "Unemployment's pretty low, bank lending rates are low, inflation is low, and GDP per capita is growing at a pretty good rate."

The US trajectory, in Hanke's assessment, is one of continued deterioration.

## The AI Stock Mania

Before wrapping up, the conversation turned to a curious market phenomenon: companies pivoting to "AI" have seen their stock prices soar by triple-digit percentages. One example: Alberts, a shoe retailer, announced a pivot to AI compute infrastructure and saw its stock price surge. Misium, a social media company rebranded as MYMI, saw similar gains.

"This is hype," Hanke said without hesitation. "There's no way their profits are going to increase by as much as implied by those huge increases in stock prices."

The stock market, he explained, is a present-value machine that calculates the present value of free cash flow. Without a corresponding surge in actual earnings, these valuations are unsustainable.

"Productivity in the United States was 3% per annum in 2024," Hanke noted. "In 2025, it was 2.1%. Last year, it actually went down. All this hype around AI and people adopting it and increasing productivity—if you aggregate and look at the economy as a whole, it's not comporting with what we're getting out of Silicon Valley hype, or what I call Silly Valley hype."

His verdict on the two companies in question? "Prime candidates for shorting."

## Conclusion

Professor Steve Hanke's assessment is stark: the US government is insolvent by any reasonable measure of its financial statements, and political leaders show no signs of addressing the structural imbalance. Deficits continue to accumulate, wars are being waged without cost estimates, and the Federal Reserve's ability to control inflation is limited by forces beyond its direct control.

The silver lining—if it can be called that—is that the US retains the unique power to print its way out of crisis, at least for now. But the costs, whether paid through higher taxes or the silent inflation tax on savings, will be borne by generations that had no say in the decisions being made today.
