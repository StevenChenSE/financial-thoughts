## The Economics Professor Breaks Down Government Finances, War Costs, and the Inflation Threat

The American economy stands at a precarious inflection point. With the University of Michigan consumer sentiment index hitting an all-time historical low, inflation ticking upward, and geopolitical tensions escalating in the Middle East, questions about the nation's fiscal health have never been more pressing. The US naval blockade of the Strait of Hormuz is amplifying already volatile oil prices, with WTI crude hovering around $100 per barrel. So what does this mean for the Treasury's finances? Is the government insolvent?

To find answers, we turned to Professor Steve Hanke, professor of applied economics at Johns Hopkins University, for his unvarnished assessment.

---

## The Insolvency Question: Setting the Record Straight

Recent weeks have seen Hanke at the center of a fact-checking controversy. The economics professor and David M. Walker, former Controller General of the United States, co-authored a Fortune magazine article with the provocative headline: "The Treasury Just Declared the US Insolvent."

The piece went viral on social media—and drew scrutiny from fact-checkers at FactCheck.org, who argued the headline was misleading. Their position: the Treasury hadn't *declared* insolvency; it simply published financial statements showing liabilities exceeding assets, a condition that has persisted for decades.

Hanke's response to the fact-check was characteristically direct.

"The fact checkers are simply displaying their ignorance and stupidity more than anything else," he said. "The Treasury did declare insolvency because they produce financial statements and the former Controller of the United States Dave Walker and I analyzed those. They're mandated by law and come out every year."

The numbers tell a stark story. According to the Treasury's most recent annual consolidated financial statements, the government's total assets come in at $6.1 trillion against liabilities of $136.2 trillion.

"By definition, you're insolvent," Hanke concluded. "It's not hyperbole. It's a conclusion drawn directly from the Treasury Department's own consolidated financial statements for fiscal year 2025."

The fact-checkers' defense—that this isn't new information—actually supports Hanke's point.

"That's true. It isn't anything new. The government's insolvent. It's just a fact," he acknowledged. "The thing with the fact checkers is they can't read financial statements. To read these correctly requires a great deal of skill. Unfortunately, my colleague Dave Walker was the Controller of the United States and knows the nature of these kinds of government accounting documents. Once you get down to the bottom line—assets and liabilities—if the liabilities are many times greater than the assets, you unambiguously are insolvent."

Hanke didn't hold back his broader assessment of the fact-checking industry either.

"By the way, in general, fact checkers should just be put in the rubbish. You get some undergraduate who's majored at some third-rate college majoring in sociology or something like that, and they're doing the fact-checking. I mean, they can't read a financial statement."

## What Happens When a Government Goes Insolvent?

The key distinction between government and corporate insolvency, Hanke explained, lies in the government's unique ability to print money.

"The government's different because there's one big difference: they can run the printing press and produce money," he noted. "Technically, many developing countries go insolvent and do not pay off their debts. The United States does not—it's a great power. It does have the printing press and ultimately could print its way out of a wet paper bag if it had to. They would continue to pay their obligations, of course, in devalued dollars."

But this escape valve comes with consequences. As the Congressional Budget Office—a bipartisan organization—indicates, fiscal conditions are deteriorating badly.

"We've got fiscal folly taking place in Washington DC, particularly over the last three administrations," Hanke observed. "You got Trump one, then Biden, then Trump too. All three of those administrations have engaged in fiscal folly—running huge deficits, huge increases in government expenditures."

These deficits, Hanke emphasized, represent deferred taxes that future generations will ultimately pay.

"Those deficits will either be paid by increasing taxes on April 15th, or they'll be paid with an inflation tax. One of the two."

More concerning still, Hanke called the practice fundamentally immoral.

"We're actually spending much more money than we're taxing, creating a deficit that ultimately has to be paid by somebody in the future who isn't even voting on it today. They're not even alive today. So future generations—or young people now who are below voting age—are going to have to pay for this stuff. That's fundamentally immoral."

## The Iran War: A Trillion-Dollar Question

The conversation then turned to the escalating conflict in the Middle East. White House Budget Director Russell Vought recently testified before the House Budget Committee that his office could not estimate the cost of a potential war with Iran.

"We're not ready to come to you with the request. We're still working on it. We're working through to figure out what's needed," Vought admitted.

Meanwhile, Harvard University's Linda Bilmes, a public policy expert at the Harvard Kennedy School, offered her own projection: "I'm certain we will reach $1 trillion for the Iran war."

For Hanke, Vought's inability to provide cost estimates epitomizes the administration's fiscal irresponsibility.

"They're entering a war and they have no idea what the cost is going to be," Hanke said. "That's how irresponsible the government is. This is delusional. How about George W. Bush when he went to war in Iraq, another big disaster? They didn't know what the benefits were. Well, there weren't any benefits. The benefits were negative to start with, and then you pile on trillions of dollars worth of cost on top of that."

Hanke expressed doubt that even Bilmes's $1 trillion figure captures the full scope of the damage.

"If there's broader cost involved, the numbers are way greater than a trillion," he said. "Can the US afford a prolonged war with Iran? No. I don't think they should have gone into the war in the first place. Why do you make a decision to do something when you don't know what the benefits are and what the costs are?"

The economic consequences extend far beyond immediate military expenditures. With the Strait of Hormuz now blockaded, a third of the world's fertilizer supply has been disrupted, causing fertilizer prices to surge over 50%. The effects will ripple through global food markets.

"Farmers aren't buying fertilizer. We're going to have food problems. Food prices will go up," Hanke warned. "That's another sticker shock."

Oil infrastructure damage compounds the problem. Approximately 80 refineries have been damaged in the conflict, with a third severely affected. Even if hostilities ceased immediately, Hanke estimated it would take two years to repair the facilities and restore production levels.

"This stupidity of these strikes—these Israeli strikes mainly, but US strikes too on oil facilities, as well as the counterattack made by the Iranians against refineries—we've got about 80 refineries that have been damaged," he explained. "It's going to take about two years just to repair those facilities. So we have a two-year repair job ahead of us before things come up to where they were prior to the war."

## The Blockade's Limited Impact on Iran

Counterintuitively, Hanke suggested the blockade may not be achieving its stated economic objective of strangling Iranian revenue.

"The claim is that this is hurting the revenue flow coming into the Iranians," he said. "Well, the Iranians have 180 million barrels of oil that is in transit beyond the Strait of Hormuz. That will be supplied to China—China takes about 1.88 million barrels a day from Iran. That will be taken from these ships that are afloat right now. That means the revenue received by selling at high oil prices at low discounts will continue at least until the end of July."

The blockade would need to persist until the end of July to actually begin denting Iranian finances, Hanke explained—assuming no further disruption to the 180 million barrels already in transit.

## The Inflation Outlook: "The Genie Is Not Going Back in the Bottle"

On the inflation front, Hanke has maintained a consistent position throughout 2025—and been largely vindicated. The latest headline inflation number stands at 3.3%, the highest since 2024.

"I anticipated inflation would go up from 2.4% to 4% and I wasn't surprised," Hanke said. "If you looked at the 3-month annualized number for CPI in March, it was actually 3.3%—exactly where the February year-over-year number turned up."

The root cause, according to Hanke, is straightforward: money supply growth.

"Changes in the money supply are what eventually, with lags, cause changes in inflation," he explained. "For some time, the money supply broadly measured by M2 has been accelerating. That's why I said the Fed was not going to be able to get the inflation genie back in the bottle."

Critics have attributed rising prices to oil shocks from the Iran conflict, but Hanke dismissed this framing as superficial.

"The oil price spike causes relative price of oil to go up, but it does cause the overall price index to go up. The only way the overall index can go up is if the money supply accelerates prior to whatever date you're reading the inflation number for," he argued.

To demonstrate his point, Hanke invoked a historical natural experiment: Japan in 1979.

"We had an oil crisis in 1979. And what happened? The inflation rate actually went down from 4.1% in '77 to 3.7% in '78 in Japan because the money supply had been controlled," he noted. "Prices of oil skyrocket in Japan and inflation actually came down. They didn't go up when oil prices went up. Why? Because the rate of growth in the money supply was cut in half between 1973 and 1979 in Japan."

The broader lesson, Hanke insisted, is timeless: "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon."

The oil price changes merely alter *relative* prices, not the overall price level. When gasoline prices rise with a fixed money supply, consumers have less to spend on other goods—so those prices simply don't accelerate as much. The aggregate stays stable.

## The Real Driver: Commercial Banking, Not the Fed

When pressed about the Fed's balance sheet—which shows assets still contracting at -0.5% year-over-year—Hanke redirected attention to the bigger picture.

"You're getting your feet tangled up here," he told the interviewer. "The Fed is a very small contributor to the overall money supply. They only contribute about 20% of the growth in the money supply. The rest is privately produced money by commercial banks."

Commercial bank credit is growing at approximately 6.7% year-over-year—above what Hanke calls his "golden growth rate" of about 6%, the rate consistent with hitting the Fed's 2% inflation target.

"That's the big contributor to what's going on. That's the thing you want to keep your eye on," he emphasized.

Two factors will sustain this acceleration, Hanke predicted. First, recent bank earnings have been substantial, boosting bank capital and creating headroom for expanded lending. Second, the Trump administration and Fed are pursuing banking deregulation, which frees up reserves.

"When banks make loans, what happens? The credit goes into the checking account of whoever they're making a loan to," he explained. "And that's a liability of the banks—the loan is an asset, but the liability is the monetary liability because it's a checking deposit, and checking deposits are counted as part of M2, as part of the money supply."

The conclusion: M2 acceleration will continue, and the Fed cannot contain it.

## Consumer Sentiment at Historic Lows

The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index has reached an all-time low in its 70-year history. While year-ahead inflation expectations on their chart appear to be declining, the data reveals a troubling divergence: the percentage of consumers citing high prices as straining personal finances continues to climb.

For Hanke, this divergence signals that actual inflation will soon follow expectations upward.

"The inflation expectation on that chart is coming down. I think that's a false signal that'll start going up though," he predicted. "There'll be a convergence of those two things."

The war and blockade have compounded affordability pressures in ways the administration failed to anticipate.

"I think Trump's advisors never anticipated the consequences of going to war," Hanke said. "They didn't see anything coming. They were flying blind. Trump likes to brag about all these damn airplanes flying over and bombing everything. Well, he's flying blind when it comes to finance and economics. He doesn't know what he's doing. And the budget director's testimony proves the guy's clueless."

## Hanke's Misery Index: Where Does the US Stand?

Shifting to his signature measure, Hanke explained the methodology behind his annual Misery Index. The formula combines four variables:

- **Inflation rate** (higher = more misery)
- **Unemployment rate** (doubled, because joblessness causes more misery than price increases)
- **Bank lending rate** (the cost of credit)
- **GDP per capita growth** (subtracted, as growth mitigates misery)

**Taiwan** ranks as the world's *least* miserable economy—low unemployment, low inflation, low lending rates, and solid GDP per capita growth. Despite relatively modest wages by Western standards, Hanke observed: "The people of Taiwan, from my personal observation, they're happy."

**Venezuela** sits at the opposite end, ranking #1 in misery, primarily driven by hyperinflation.

**The United States** places at **119**—notably poor standing.

"By the way, Cameroon ranks 120, slightly worse than the United States," Hanke added, noting the date was April 16th. "Singapore, all these Southeast Asian countries—all the tigers do pretty well."

**Canada** surprisingly ranks at 98, worse than the US—ahead only of Cameroon and Venezuela among developed nations.

"Canada is more miserable than the United States," Hanke confirmed. "You look a little more miserable than I do."

The interviewer, based in Vancouver, confirmed anecdotal evidence of Canadian misery: "Canadians are miserable because of unaffordability. People here can't buy homes, we can't buy cars, groceries are getting more expensive."

Hanke agreed the lending rate plays a crucial role. "Even GDP per capita—if GDP per capita is rising rapidly, your income is rising rapidly, which means your income is less of a constraint on buying things."

The index is published annually in Fortune magazine and widely cited as a comprehensive gauge of economic well-being.

## The AI Stock Hype: "Prime Candidates for Shorting"

In closing, the conversation turned to a perplexing market phenomenon: companies pivoting to AI and seeing their stock prices soar by triple-digit percentages overnight.

Alberts, a shoe retailer, announced a pivot to AI compute infrastructure and saw shares surge. Misium, a social media company rebranded as MYMI, saw its stock soar 100% after embracing the AI narrative.

"Where have we seen this before?" Hanke asked dryly.

"I'm a skeptic on AI," he admitted. "I use it. If you know what you're doing, there are all kinds of AIs. I use different AI as research assistants. It is productive for me because I'd have to send an assistant over to the library— they'd spend a week or 10 days doing something I can do in about an hour on AI."

But the stock market response to corporate AI pivots is another matter entirely.

"These two companies—this is hype. It's clearly hype," Hanke declared. "There's no way their profits are going to increase by as much as implied by those huge increases in stock prices. The stock market is just a present-valuing machine. It calculates the present value of free cash flow coming into the company. There's no way free cash flow is surging by that amount just because of the adoption of AI."

Aggregate data supports his skepticism. US productivity growth was 3% annualized in 2024, falling to 2.1% in 2025.

"All of this hype around AI and people adopting it and increasing productivity—if you aggregate and look at the economy as a whole, it's not comporting with what we're getting out of Silicon Valley hype," Hanke said. "I call it Silly Valley hype."

His investment assessment: "If I was following through, those would be prime shorts. I think they're primed to short because there's just no way you're going to get that kind of improvement in valuation just because of adoption of AI. The only way it could happen is to have a massive increase in free cash flow. My conjecture is that it's hype overdone."
