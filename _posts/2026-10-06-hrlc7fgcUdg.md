## Macro Strategist Mark Skousen on Gross Output, Rising Yields, and the Threat of a Debt Crisis

While headline indicators paint a mixed picture of consumer confidence and high inflation, the underlying mechanics of the United States economy reveal significant divergence across sectors. In a wide-ranging discussion, economist and author Mark Skousen, macro strategist at the Oxford Club and editor of the *Skousen Report*, analyzed current economic growth, persistent inflation pressures, potential systemic risks in the government bond market, and portfolio allocation strategies.

---

### The Reality of Economic Growth: GDP vs. Gross Output

Discussions surrounding economic health typically center on Gross Domestic Product (GDP), which emphasizes final consumer spending. However, Skousen argues that focusing solely on GDP misrepresents the core drivers of economic vitality. Instead, he points to **Gross Output (GO)**—a measure that accounts for business-to-business (B2B) transactions across all intermediate stages of production.

> "GDP gives the impression—it's a myth—that consumer spending is the biggest sector of the economy, when in fact business spending is bigger than consumer spending by a substantial amount—almost two-to-one."

According to recent data:
* **B2B Spending Surge:** Business spending jumped by **13.8%** in the second quarter, leading to a **5% real increase in Gross Output**.
* **Leading Indicator:** Gross Output functions as a leading indicator, signaling that future GDP figures will likely reflect sustained economic activity.
* **Key Drivers:** Skousen attributes the rebound in supply-chain activity to the post-pandemic supply normalization, stabilization in trade policy, and aggressive capital expenditure into artificial intelligence infrastructure and data centers.

While consumer sentiment indices show lingering dissatisfaction due to high living costs, the business and production side of the economy continues to expand rapidly.

### Persistent Inflation and the Treasury Market Threat

Despite optimistic projections regarding core inflation, Skousen cautions that headline pressures—driven by energy shocks, persistent fiscal deficits, and military expenditures—remain entrenched. The government continues to run roughly **$2 trillion annual deficits** during periods of near-full employment, creating immense pressure on debt issuance.

The most critical long-term tail risk lies within the **US Treasury market**:

1. **Massive Refinancing Demands:** The Treasury must roll over an estimated **$7 trillion** in debt in a single year.
2. **Shifting Buyer Demographics:** Central bank demand for Treasuries has softened, leaving private equity and hedge funds as the primary buyers taking advantage of high yields.
3. **The Risk of a "No Bid" Scenario:** If institutional buyers demand significantly higher yields to absorb supply, the Treasury could face severe borrowing costs, reminiscent of past liquidity crunches like the 1980 yield spikes.

If bond yields spike uncontrollably to clear Treasury supply, risk assets could face sharp corrections. While Skousen does not view an immediate collapse as his baseline forecast, he views a potential **50% market retracement** as a credible tail risk if the debt market reaches a breaking point.

### Monetary Policy and Money Supply Dynamics

Addressing Federal Reserve policy, Skousen emphasized the direct historical relationship between inflation rates and long-term interest yields. For sustainable rate cuts to occur, central banks must achieve genuine price stability rather than settling for elevated baseline inflation.

Tracking money supply growth (M2), which has been expanding at roughly 5% annually, Skousen noted that a reduction in money growth paired with balance-sheet runoff is essential to curb structural price increases. Effective monetary leadership, comparable to the discipline demonstrated by Paul Volcker, remains necessary to re-establish long-term market confidence.

The US economy presents a striking contrast: robust supply-side production fueled by technology investment on one side, and severe fiscal deficits paired with debt-refinancing pressures on the other.
