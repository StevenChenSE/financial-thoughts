## Why gold’s breakout may be fake—and what comes next

In an August 2026 interview on Capital Cosm, analyst Simon Hunt of Visa V Strategic Services laid out a bold and interconnected view of macro markets, geopolitics, and resource risks. He argues that today’s gold breakout is a fake-out, that 2027 will bring a severe global equity correction and recession, and that these stresses are part of a longer cycle leading to a major conflict around 2030–2032. Alongside gold, he warns of surging food prices, fragile oil supply chains, and a financial system nearing limits.

### The gold price: fake-out, then a better entry

Hunt believes the current surge in gold is not the long-awaited breakout. He expects another correction that could take gold down to around $3,700—offering a cleaner entry point for long-term holders.

His reasoning centers on three factors:

- Potential late-2026 Fed tightening. Hunt sees a risk that the Federal Reserve may tighten policy later this year as oil and commodity pressures intensify.
- A 2027 global recession. He expects the US and most of the world to enter recession next year, which would trigger a “final correction” in gold prices.
- Falling long-term yields. He anticipates the US 10-year Treasury yield could drop to around 3% in 2027 as recession hits.

For investors, his guidance is straightforward:

- Be short-term in cash now, especially after an expected near-term equity correction.
- Stay long gold, but be ready to add at lower prices in 2027.

### Equity markets: 2027 mega-correction, then stimulus-fueled inflation

Hunt frames 2027 as a pivotal and difficult year for equities. His sequence:

1. A relief rally in Q4 2026. After a likely 6–8% equity correction around late 2026, markets may stage a typical fourth-quarter bounce, potentially lifting indices higher temporarily.
2. A 2027 mega-correction. Driven by recession and supply shocks, global equity markets could face a severe downturn.
3. Policy response and inflation. With 2028 as a US election year, central banks and governments would likely restart aggressive stimulus, reigniting inflation.
4. A broad asset bull market into 2030–2032. This inflationary push would lift equities, base metals, and precious metals before what Hunt calls the end of a 100-year bull market.

He sees this arc as consistent with longer economic and political cycles.

### Global liquidity: turning over, not expanding freely

Despite headlines about money printing and yen-buying interventions, Hunt is skeptical that global liquidity is on a clean upward path.

He cites Philip Michael Howell of CrossBorder Capital, an expert on global money supply, who argues that both US and global liquidity are in the process of turning over. In practical terms:

- The era of easy, unbounded liquidity expansion may be ending.
- Financial conditions could tighten organically even if central banks attempt targeted support.

This view underpins his expectations for recession and asset corrections.

### Oil, the Strait of Hormuz, and Gulf fiscal strain

Oil is at the core of Hunt’s geopolitical and economic outlook. He stresses that the Middle East conflict is far from over and that oil supply remains highly vulnerable.

Key points:

- The Strait of Hormuz is unlikely to reopen soon.
- Houthi forces are controlling vessel movements in the Red Sea and have struck major infrastructure.
- A key Saudi refinery near Jaan—about 45 kilometers from the Yemen border, with 400,000 barrels per day capacity—has been hit.
- On the Hormuz side, Iran has struck a pumping station on the pipeline that moves Saudi oil across the kingdom to the port of Yanbu.

With refining and export routes compromised, Saudi Arabia and the UAE face severe cash flow problems. Hunt notes that both countries borrowed heavily during the COVID-related revenue collapse and may still carry significant debt. Disrupted oil flows would exacerbate their fiscal strain and tighten global supply.

### A new Sunni alliance: Saudi–Turkey–Pakistan

Hunt examines the newly minted alliance between Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, and Turkey, with Egypt in a background advisory role.

Two common denominators:

1. All are Sunni-majority countries, together representing more than 420 million Sunni Muslims.
2. All remain, in one way or another, beholden to the United States.

He questions whether this is merely a “paper tiger” or part of a longer US strategy to use Sunni-aligned partners to manage regional conflicts. He also dismisses earlier rhetoric about “Turkey being next” as potentially a smokescreen. Israel’s real concern, he suggests, may be more about Turkey’s ability to control oil flows into Israel than about Turkey’s alignment with the West.

He sees rising friction between Turkey and Iran, with a major conflict possible in four to six years, and Syria as a flashpoint where the US, Turkey, and historical tribal ties intersect.

### 2030–2032: the next big war

Hunt’s macro-geopolitical view culminates in a forecast for a major war erupting around 2030–2032. He ties this to:

- Cyclical patterns in global politics and conflict.
- The sequence of recession, stimulus, and inflation from 2027 through the late 2020s.
- Escalating regional conflicts that coalesce rather than resolve.

He does not give a detailed military scenario but frames this as a “big war” involving major powers, emerging from the current web of proxy conflicts and resource rivalries.

### Iran’s shift: harder line, proactive attacks

Hunt argues that Iran’s posture has fundamentally hardened:

- A new leader of the Supreme Council, backed by the IRGC, has shifted power away from a president who previously sought accommodation with the West.
- The president could be forced out, leaving a tough-minded leadership with strong domestic support.
- Iran’s population, especially younger generations, feels it has endured 47 years of sanctions and pressure and now seeks revenge.

As a result, Iran is becoming proactive in targeting American assets, including vessels attempting to use the Hormuz and Oman routes. Hunt expects further US airstrikes on Iran, possibly accompanied by a ground operation to occupy one of its islands. He warns this would be highly dangerous and could trigger significant blowback.

### Debt markets, yields, and the limits of control

On fixed income, Hunt addresses the persistence of higher long-term yields and the fragility of US debt markets:

- The 10-year yield has been reliably above 4.6% for a stretch.
- Yen volatility has required intervention by the Bank of Japan and the US Treasury.
- Even if the Fed cuts the funds rate, it has limited control over the long end of the curve.

He outlines two likely developments:

1. Regulatory pressure on buyers. Pension funds and large institutions may be pressured or mandated to hold more US bonds.
2. Monetization. If debt service costs become unsustainable, the government may have no choice but to monetize its own debt, effectively printing money to fund liabilities.

A recession-driven drop in yields would, in his view, be temporary and would create refinance opportunities, but it would not solve the underlying structural issues.

### Food prices: El Niño, climate cycles, and shortages

Beyond financial markets, Hunt highlights rising food risks:

- A strong El Niño is expected to peak around December, bringing droughts to parts of Asia and excessive rainfall and storms to South America.
- A 99-year climate cycle that previously contributed to the US Midwest Dust Bowl remains a risk for 2027.
- Fertilizer shortages and high prices are likely to compound supply constraints.

His practical advice: expect food prices to surge. He suggests stocking up on staples and, if possible, converting part of any garden space to vegetables.

### Gold and silver miners: wait for the AI boom to burst

On mining equities, Hunt is cautious about current valuations:

- Much of the free capital is chasing AI stocks.
- When the AI boom bursts, capital will rotate into safer havens, supporting gold and silver miners.

He implies that miners could become more attractive after that rotation, once their all-in sustaining costs are comfortably covered by higher precious metal prices and investor sentiment shifts away from speculative tech.

### How to position: a broad outline

While avoiding personal portfolio specifics, Hunt’s general positioning guidance is clear:

- Near term: favor cash, expecting a correction followed by a Q4 2026 relief rally.
- Gold: stay long, but be prepared to add at lower prices during the 2027 correction.
- Equities: brace for a 2027 mega-correction, then a stimulus-driven inflationary rally into the early 2030s.
- Commodities and agriculture: expect higher prices in oil and food due to geopolitical and climate risks.
- Geopolitics: assume no quick resolution in the Middle East; prepare for escalation rather than de-escalation.

Simon Hunt’s outlook is stark but internally consistent:

- Gold’s current move may be a trap; a drop to around $3,700 in 2027 could offer a better entry.
- A global recession and equity mega-correction are likely in 2027.
- Aggressive stimulus in 2028–2029 would fuel inflation and a broad asset bull market into 2030–2032.
- That same period could see a major war emerge from the current tangle of Middle Eastern conflicts and great-power rivalry.
- Oil supply, Gulf fiscal health, and food production are all under serious stress.

For investors, his message is to prioritize resilience: keep dry powder, think long-term on gold, and prepare for a world where geopolitics, resource constraints, and macro cycles dominate market outcomes.
