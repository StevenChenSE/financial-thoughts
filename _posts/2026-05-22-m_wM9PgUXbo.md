For years, retail investors have speculated about mysterious patterns in Bitcoin spot ETF trading—particularly the so-called "10 AM dump" where some believe institutional players systematically sell off Bitcoin at a specific time each day, allowing retail to pile in afterward. But according to Christa Lynch, Senior Vice President of ETF Capital Markets at Grayscale Investments, many of these theories are overblown.

"People often assume that Grayscale is actively making a decision to buy or sell," Lynch explained during a recent interview. "We are really beholden to the end investor who seeks to buy shares and ultimately that makes its way to an authorized participant who may create or redeem."

The truth about Bitcoin ETF mechanics is far more nuanced—and far more interesting—than Twitter theories suggest. Here's what actually happens behind the scenes of the world's most-watched financial products.

## The 10 AM Myth: What Actually Drives Intraday Bitcoin Price Movements

While social media has latched onto theories about coordinated institutional selling at 10 AM, Lynch offers a more grounded explanation tied to financial instrument mechanics.

"Some of the futures for Bitcoin and other digital assets have a very important time at which their value is struck at London," Lynch explained. "I believe it's 10:00 AM here or 11:00 AM here."

Another critical timestamp is **4:00 PM US Eastern Time**, when Net Asset Value (NAV) is struck. This creates observable trading activity between 3:00 and 4:00 PM as market participants position themselves around this valuation point.

The takeaway? When retail investors see tokens moving in or out of Grayscale wallets, they're witnessing **settlement facilitation**, not active portfolio management. "We're just facilitating a settlement," Lynch emphasized. "There's not a lot of deeper meaning behind it."

## How Bitcoin ETFs Actually Work: The Creation-Redemption Lifecycle

One of the most illuminating parts of Lynch's interview involved walking through exactly what happens when you buy a single share of a Bitcoin spot ETF.

### Step-by-Step: From Order to Settlement

1. **Investor Purchase**: You and other retail investors buy Bitcoin ETFs on secondary markets through your brokerage accounts (Fidelity, Schwab, etc.)

2. **Market Maker Short Position**: The ETF market maker selling you those shares may not have them immediately—they're becoming short as they facilitate your purchase

3. **Authorized Participant Request**: The market maker bundles their short position and works with an **authorized participant**—an institution with specific licenses to engage directly with ETF issuers like Grayscale

4. **Share Creation**: The authorized participant approaches Grayscale requesting new shares to cover the market maker's short position

5. **Underlying Asset Purchase**: Grayscale's team translates the share quantity into Bitcoin equivalent and purchases that amount in the market

6. **Settlement**: The authorized participant delivers newly created shares to the market maker, completing the cycle

"The trade itself is very quick," Lynch noted. "It might take 5-10 minutes, and that's by design. We want to make sure that markets are not moving while the authorized participant is seeking their pricing."

The entire settlement process takes approximately one day, but the actual trading mechanics happen in minutes—intentionally designed to minimize market exposure during the creation process.

### Why Institutional Investors Sometimes Beat Retail

A common retail grievance centers on the apparent unfairness of institutional participants seemingly always winning trades. Lynch addressed this directly:

"When I go out and do that trade to buy Bitcoin, we often see their crypto desk winning that trade. And that's very much by design by that ETF market maker because they can collapse their risk that way."

If you hold ETF shares, you probably want matched Bitcoin exposure—and a market maker with both an ETF trading desk and a crypto trading desk can hedge their positions more efficiently by executing both sides.

However, Lynch argues retail investors *benefit* from this dynamic:

> "The end investor benefits from it actually by getting very tight spreads in market. When you or I go to buy a Bitcoin ETF, the spread—so the bid and the ask—is typically 1 cent. That's as tight as it can possibly get. That's single-digit BIPS. And that's very, very good pricing."

The ecosystem is designed so that large institutional block trades that retail investors wouldn't engage in directly support the tight pricing that benefits everyone.

## The Premium-Discount Mechanism: Lessons from GBTC

Understanding why ETFs trade at premiums or discounts to their NAV requires examining the unique history of Grayscale's Bitcoin Trust (GBTC)—which traded at discounts exceeding 20% before converting to an ETF structure.

### Why Premiums and Discounts Exist

ETFs trade in **two distinct markets**:

- **Secondary Market**: Where retail investors trade shares through brokers (Fidelity, Schwab)
- **Primary Market**: Where authorized participants create or redeem shares directly with the ETF issuer

"Premiums and discounts happen when that secondary market price does not equal the NAV, which is the primary market price," Lynch explained. "Typically, the authorized participant—really an ETF market maker who's working with an authorized participant—will keep those two very, very tight."

### The GBTC Exception

Before GBTC converted to an ETF, it lacked the primary market function. Market makers could only access one side of the market—they could buy shares on the secondary market but couldn't redeem them through creation.

"The premium or discount could be like 10-20%, which is really unheard of for ETFs," Lynch recalled. "The reason for that was because arbitragers could not access the primary market. They could only trade on the secondary market. So there was a surplus of shares in market that really had no outlet to be reduced."

The GBTC premium/discount became a **market sentiment indicator** for SEC approval probability. Every time positive news emerged about the potential ETF conversion, the discount narrowed as the market anticipated the primary market mechanism being enabled.

Today's Bitcoin spot ETFs maintain premiums and discounts in the **single-digit basis points**—essentially negligible—because the creation/redemption arbitrage mechanism functions as intended.

## In-Kind Creation: How Whales Are Joining the ETF Revolution

One of the most significant regulatory developments of 2024 was SEC approval for **in-kind creation and redemption** in Bitcoin ETFs. This fundamentally changed who can access primary markets.

### Breaking Down Primary Market Barriers

Traditionally, primary market access required working through authorized participants—something available only to large institutional counterparties. However, in-kind creation opened doors for sophisticated investors to participate directly.

"So behind the scenes, of course there's a lot going on, but yes, my team will actually work directly with whales," Lynch confirmed. "We will engage with our authorized participant partners or other third-party intermediaries to help them in-kind tokens into the product."

### Why Whales Are Flocking to ETFs

The incentive structure is compelling:

- **Tax Planning**: Moving tokens into ETF share format can offer more favorable tax treatment
- **Estate Planning**: US brokerage accounts provide established inheritance frameworks
- **Margin and Collateral**: Shares can be used as collateral for loans or margin positions
- **Operational Efficiency**: Institutional infrastructure for custody and compliance

"We thought that as a crypto native that would be kind of repugnant to get on the US rails via the ETF," Lynch admitted. "But the reason that it's compelling is because if you move your token into shares format, you can often get tax planning, estate planning, the benefits that these kind of US brokerage accounts allow."

## Staking in ETF Form: Generating Yield on Ethereum Holdings

Perhaps the most technically sophisticated aspect of Grayscale's operations involves its **staking-enabled Ethereum products**—a feature that required extensive regulatory navigation.

### How Staking Works in an ETF Structure

Staking involves pledging tokens to network validators who help secure the blockchain. In return, stakers receive additional tokens as rewards—essentially earning yield on their holdings.

"We work with third-party validators," Lynch explained. "You're contributing your token to make the overall protocol more safe and secure. Now as you have it pledged, it is locked up. It's not liquid."

This creates an obvious problem for liquid ETF products. Grayscale solved it through:

- **Dynamic Liquidity Modeling**: Mathematical models determining optimal staking percentages
- **Unstaked Reserves**: Maintaining immediately liquid portions of the fund
- **Delayed Settlement**: Partnering with liquidity providers on T+1 settlement terms
- **SEC Collaboration**: Extensive documentation demonstrating liquidity management frameworks

The result? Grayscale's ETH product offers **reinvested staking yields** that compound over time, while its Ethereum Classic product pays cash distributions. During Q1 2025 when competing Ethereum ETFs experienced $769 million in outflows, Grayscale's ETH fund attracted capital—likely due to this yield differentiation.

## The Future of Tokenization: From Stablecoins to Real Estate

Lynch sees tokenization following a predictable progression from simple to complex assets:

### The Adoption Roadmap

1. **Stablecoins as Tokenized Cash**: "I see stable coins as kind of tokenized cash, and I think that's the first place that we'll see banks and other really traditional finance institutions getting their foothold."

2. **US Equities**: Institutions like NYSE, NASDAQ, and DTC have already announced tokenization initiatives. "I think we'll see progress on that in the coming months."

3. **Real World Assets (RWA)**: Tokenized real estate and other physical assets represent the final frontier—likely years away from mainstream adoption.

### Ownership Rights: The Regulatory Frontier

Tokenization raises complex legal questions about ownership. Lynch drew parallels to ETF voting rights:

> "If I own shares of an ETF, do I get to vote to say that I want one of the shares within my S&P 500 of 500 shares? Do I get to make the managerial decision?"

Tokenized assets amplify these questions. If you tokenize a Stradivarius violin, do fractional owners have voting rights on performance decisions? When tokenizing a company, who actually owns the business?

The answer depends heavily on structure:

- **On-chain direct ownership** likely supports more owner discretion
- **IOU structures** (similar to European ETNs) may require institutional discretion for administrative purposes
- **ADRs and ETFs** provide existing regulatory frameworks to study

"This goes back to traditional finance analogies," Lynch noted. "Whenever there's ambiguity about a price—like an index—there's a robust set of rules for pricing waterfalls and dispute resolution. I think we'll ultimately have to go with something like tokenization as well."

## ETF Inflows, Outflows, and Price Correlation

With two years of Bitcoin spot ETF data available, Lynch addressed whether ETF flows predict price movements:

**The short answer: It's complicated.**

While creation demand typically correlates with rising prices and redemption demand with falling prices, the relationship isn't strictly causal or predictive.

"The ETFs are a really good and efficient way to express a view on risk very quickly," Lynch explained. "So you can see it go inflows quickly to outflows the next day, and I think that's because of the efficiency of the vehicle."

Correlation exists, but one doesn't reliably lead the other. "If anything, it might skew a little bit towards the price being what you see—a delayed reaction to with the ETFs—but it's not like you know it's going to happen because you're seeing one versus the other."

### The 21 Million Cap Question

With treasury companies accumulating Bitcoin and a fixed supply cap, could creation/redemption processes eventually face liquidity constraints?

"We get this question a lot," Lynch said. "Really, it should just push up the price of Bitcoin with basic supply-demand dynamics. It will not hinder the creation redemption process. It just means that buying Bitcoin might cost more, but that manifests in a higher price of Bitcoin and the NAV moves alongside that."

## Covered Call Strategies: Building Yield on Bitcoin and Ethereum

Beyond staking, Grayscale has pioneered **covered call strategies** using options overlays:

"You can go long, you can go short. And going short doesn't even necessarily mean that you think Bitcoin is done—it's just that you're taking an investment thesis and applying it through the Bitcoin ETF."

For yield generation specifically, "we're using options overlays in order to build yield on top of Bitcoin positions and Ethereum positions." This synthetic yield approach complements staking capabilities and offers investors income streams without direct protocol participation.

## Upcoming ETF Filings: What's Next for Institutional Crypto

When asked about future ETF launches, Lynch highlighted two protocols on her watch list: **BNB** and **Hyperliquid (HYPE)**.

The decision framework involves multiple layers:

1. **Regulatory Eligibility**: Following the SEC's generic listing standards framework covering approximately 15 tokens
2. **Research Diligence**: Grayscale's research team spends years understanding protocols, meeting teams, and developing investment theses
3. **Market Positioning**: "We now offer eight tokens in ETF form, soon to be several more"—requiring selectivity rather than "we can, so we will"

### The VC vs. Non-VC Question

Notably, Hyperliquid operates without traditional venture capital backing. Lynch addressed whether this affects consideration:

"Not necessarily. And I think that the whale point is very interesting for the ETFs because we've recently seen that whales have increasingly been interested in putting their tokens into the ETF."

## Institutional Adoption: Bitcoin's Maturation Story

The most significant theme Lynch emphasized throughout the interview was Bitcoin's institutional maturation:

"Just looking around the crowd today at Consensus, it's a lot more traditional banks. It's a lot more people in suits. And that's exciting in and of itself because it means that there's maturity of the asset class."

### Why ETFs Changed Everything

"The ETFs were a huge part of that for people that didn't know how to open a wallet, institutions that didn't have the risk infrastructure to manage a wallet or didn't want to build that out."

This infrastructure evolution—from self-custody to brokerage-based access—opened doors for pension funds, endowments, and traditional institutions previously excluded from digital asset allocation.

### A Federal Reserve Future?

Could the Fed someday purchase Bitcoin ETFs during market distress, similar to their corporate bond ETF purchases during COVID?

"I think it's possible. Don't say anything's impossible, but it seems to me like they'll still need more time to get comfortable with the asset class," Lynch said. "But you see pensions, endowments starting to do it, and I think that's kind of the first sign."

## Bitcoin vs. Ethereum: Dual Futures, Different Purposes

When asked about relative outlooks, Lynch offered nuanced differentiation:

**Bitcoin** represents accessibility and first-time investor entry: "A lot of people that are new to the investment in digital asset world, they just learn about Bitcoin, and I think that's what they gravitate to. It's like they might have heard of Bitcoin and they're not necessarily able to go down the token spectrum yet."

**Ethereum** represents infrastructure and smart contract utility: "The value or a lot of value is in these smart contract type builds. I think being able to use blockchain technology to improve our processes and codify—if you think about a fixed income bond, codify some of these back office functions, make things more efficient. I think that's really exciting."

Both have "very bright futures, but for slightly different reasons."

## The Quantum Computing Wild Card

Lynch acknowledged that quantum computing concerns are "coming up in a lot of cocktail conversations" at industry events:

"I've heard competing viewpoints. I've heard some that think that is going to be the biggest threat to Bitcoin. And then I've heard others that say these things are already happening—AI is already running, we already have these models in place."

Internally, Grayscale monitors both direct and indirect risks: "How could that affect our service providers? We not only need to view how it affects our immediate workforce, but how does it also affect some of our service providers like custodians, authorized participants—like could they suffer a hack?"

## Key Takeaways

1. **ETFs are settlement mechanisms, not active managers**: When you see Bitcoin moving in or out of Grayscale wallets, it's responding to investor demand for creation/redemption—not discretionary trading decisions.

2. **The 10 AM and 4 PM price movements correlate with financial instrument strikes**: Futures pricing and NAV determination create observable activity windows, but these reflect market mechanics rather than coordinated manipulation.

3. **In-kind creation has opened primary markets to whales**: SEC approval for in-kind creation/redemption enables sophisticated investors to access NAV pricing directly, bringing tax planning, estate planning, and collateral benefits to crypto-native large holders.

4. **Staking in ETF form required solving the liquidity paradox**: Grayscale's staking implementation involves dynamic modeling, T+1 delayed settlements with liquidity providers, and extensive SEC documentation proving the model won't harm investors during redemptions.

5. **Tokenization follows an adoption hierarchy: stablecoins → equities → real world assets**: Each stage requires solving distinct regulatory, technical, and ownership-rights challenges, with mainstream RWA tokenization likely years away despite promising early developments.
