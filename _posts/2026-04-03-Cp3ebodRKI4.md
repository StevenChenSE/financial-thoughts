Fernando's bearish sentiment is echoed by others who point to supply-demand dynamics. One observer notes that while physical markets are screaming a shortage, paper markets are "still pretending," creating a gap that keeps panic at bay for now. Another contributor highlights that within the next 12 months, the U.S. must refinance $10 trillion in debt. With an average interest rate on outstanding U.S. debt at 3.4%, every 1% increase in rates increases interest expenses by $100 billion. This creates a scenario where higher oil prices could exacerbate debt servicing issues, potentially forcing rate hikes that might dampen demand.

Additionally, some analysts look to historical parallels. For instance, comparing the current situation to the 1970s, gold rose 20x while the S&P 500 plunged 50%. Similarly, in the Great Depression, gold stocks returned 500% as the S&P plunged 80%. However, Andy questions the direct applicability of these historical patterns to current mining stocks, noting that in the 1930s, gold was forcibly repriced from $20 to $35 an ounce, which benefited mining companies like Homestake Mining.

A core pillar of Andy's argument is the relationship between money printing and oil demand. He challenges the notion that money printing doesn't drive oil demand, stating: "When you print money, it is literally demand for oil."

He explains the mechanism clearly: "If we're at war, we are burning fuel. We are We have people eating, and eating is burning fuel to create the food. The fossil fuels are in the fertilizers." Thus, any stimulus package or money printing intended to boost demand ultimately fuels the supply chain, which is heavily dependent on oil. "And people will tell me how wrong I am. 'Andy, that's not right. That doesn't make any sense. That's not, you know, when money printing is not demand for oil.' Okay, well, tell me what money printing is going to purchase."

Andy illustrates this with a historical parallel to the 1970s oil embargo: "This one here was money was coming into the system. It was coming through bank loans. And the bank loans expands the money supply. And that money supply expanding said, 'Feed me more things.' We hit peak conventional oil in America in the 1970. And that's what that big move is. We had an oil embargo and all this other things. It was money chasing a supply uh shortage of oil."

Looking ahead, Andy suggests a similar pattern is emerging: "Now, this circle here is going to be a bunch of money printing that occurs with a shortage on the supply of world production. They're going to print money at a faster pace than oil production can go up. If there is any. And if it goes down, it's going to amplify the move."
