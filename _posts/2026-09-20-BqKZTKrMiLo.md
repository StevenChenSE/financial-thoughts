### The 2026 Rate Hike Cycle and the Fiscal Inflation Trap

On September 16, 2026, the Federal Reserve raised the federal funds rate by 25 basis points to a target range of 3.75% to 4.00%, marking the first increase in three years. While the FOMC cited a need to support the "timelier return" to its 2% inflation target, the decision has sparked debate over whether this is a "one-and-done" hike or the beginning of a more aggressive tightening cycle.

Komal Sri-Kumar, President of Sri Kumar Global Strategies, argues that the economy is currently facing a "double whammy" of persistent inflation and rising interest rates that it cannot withstand. In a recent interview, Sri-Kumar detailed how political pressures, a massive fiscal deficit, and global supply shocks are complicating the Fed's mandate and keeping bond yields at historic highs.

## The Fed's Dilemma: Political Pressure vs. Economic Reality

The Fed's decision to hike by only 25 basis points was viewed by many as a cautious move, but Sri-Kumar contends that economic data would have warranted a 50 basis point increase. He points to a "political reason" for the smaller hike: the administration of President Donald Trump has consistently called for lower interest rates.

> "The reason for not doing it is political rather than economic in my mind," Sri-Kumar stated. "You have already upset the president by hiking when he wanted a cut in the interest rate."

Sri-Kumar noted that a larger hike would have likely triggered significant criticism from the President and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent. The 12-0 unanimous vote for the 25bp hike was seen as a strategic move to protect Fed Chair Kevin Warsh from political backlash, as any dissent would have provided ammunition for the administration to attack the Fed's independence.

## Why Bond Yields Are Sticking at 20-Year Highs

Despite the rate hike, the 10-year Treasury yield remains near 5.04%, a 20-year high. While the Fed's press conference cited "economic strength" and "competition for capital" (driven by AI hyperscaler spending) as reasons for rising yields, Sri-Kumar argues these are secondary factors. The primary drivers, he says, are the U.S. fiscal position and geopolitics.

### 1. The Fiscal Deficit
As of August 2026, total U.S. debt exceeds $40 trillion, with the deficit running at approximately $2 trillion annually. Sri-Kumar points out that interest payments have now surpassed defense spending as a major source of government expenditure.

> "The two major reasons for why the bond yields were higher were missed out in his [Fed Chair's] explanation... The US fiscal total debt is over $40 trillion... and interest payments have become a bigger source of spending than defense expenditure."

### 2. Geopolitics and Energy
The ongoing Iran conflict and the closure of the East-West pipeline in Saudi Arabia are keeping oil prices elevated. Sri-Kumar explains that this is not just about spot prices but "crack spreads"—the difference between crude oil and refined products that end up in consumer stores.

## The "Self-Correction" Fallacy

A common argument among some economists is that high oil prices will naturally lead to "demand destruction," effectively doing the Fed's job by slowing the economy without the need for monetary tightening. Sri-Kumar agrees that oil prices are self-correcting in the long run but argues that "politics does not allow politicians to pursue that path."

Instead of accepting slower growth, the government is injecting stimulus into the economy, which worsens inflation. Sri-Kumar draws a parallel to the 1970s, noting that when inflation is driven by supply shocks (like oil), the only way to squeeze it out of the system is through higher interest rates, even if it risks a short-term economic slowdown.

## The AI Capex Boom: A Double-Edged Sword

The Fed's statement described productivity growth as "strong" and capital investment as "robust." While productivity gains typically lower prices, Sri-Kumar warns that the current productivity boost is driven by massive capital expenditures in AI data centers.

> "The spending more than offsets the productivity improvement... These companies are competing with the US Treasury in the same market to borrow from investors."

This competition for capital is pushing bond yields higher, which in turn raises borrowing costs for consumers and businesses. Sri-Kumar fears that this dynamic could lead to a "financial accident," drawing a comparison to the 2008 crisis, where high interest rates damaged companies unable to service their debt.

## The Fiscal Loop: A Self-Fulfilling Prophecy

The U.S. Treasury's attempt to lower bond yields through a $10 billion buyback of 10-year Treasuries was criticized as a failure. Sri-Kumar notes that the 10-year yield rose 20 basis points *after* the intervention, suggesting the buyback was too small to move the needle in a $30 trillion bond market.

> "Spending $6 billion is a drop in the bucket. It didn't do much."

Sri-Kumar argues that the only way to break this cycle is for the Treasury to address the root cause: the fiscal deficit. This would require either tax increases or significant cuts to entitlement spending (such as raising the retirement age for Social Security). Without these measures, the government must continue to issue more debt to pay interest on existing debt, a "self-fulfilling" loop that keeps yields elevated.

## Outlook: What to Expect in 2026-2027

Sri-Kumar predicts that the Fed will hike rates again in December 2026, avoiding a hike in October due to the proximity of the midterm elections. He expects the Fed to focus closely on inflation data, which will likely remain elevated due to energy costs.

Key risks for the coming months include:
*   **Tariff Escalation:** The U.S.-Canada trade dispute and broader "US vs. the rest of the world" tariff wars are putting downward pressure on global growth.
*   **Housing and Consumer Spending:** With 30-year mortgage rates near 7%, housing is frozen. Consumer spending is being propped up by high-income groups, but this disparity is unsustainable.
*   **2027 Rate Outlook:** The Fed's dot plot shows no rate increases for 2027, but Sri-Kumar calls this "unrealistic." He expects the Fed to continue hiking into 2027 unless there is a breakthrough on fiscal policy.

> "The economy cannot deal with it. So, what they have to find out is to say if the United States wants to pursue the Iran war then they have to do it realizing that it's going to have a negative impact on inflation, consumer spending, and the economy."

## Conclusion

The U.S. economy is at a crossroads. The Fed is attempting to manage inflation in the face of political pressure and a fiscal deficit that is out of control. While productivity gains from AI offer some hope, the massive spending required to achieve them is keeping bond yields high. Without a credible plan to reduce the fiscal deficit, the "double whammy" of high inflation and high interest rates will continue to weigh on the economy, potentially leading to a sharper slowdown than the current GDP forecasts suggest.
