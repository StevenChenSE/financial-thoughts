### Economist Trevor Tombe analyzes the economic pain, the "sovereignty" debate, and the case for domestic reform over retaliation.

The United States-Canada trade war has entered a critical phase, with economic experts warning of significant domestic consequences. Following the collapse of trade negotiations in late August, the US imposed 50% tariffs on $20 billion worth of Canadian imports. In response, Canada retaliated with matching 50% tariffs on US goods. While political leaders from Mark Carney to Stephen Harper have framed this conflict as a necessary defense of national sovereignty, economist Trevor Tombe argues that the economic costs of retaliation may outweigh the benefits.

## The Economic Impact: 90,000 Jobs at Risk

Trevor Tombe, a professor of economics at the University of Calgary, has quantified the potential damage to the Canadian economy. His research, published by The Hub, estimates that the trade conflict could put approximately 90,000 Canadian jobs at risk.

Tombe breaks down this figure into two components:
*   **Direct Job Losses:** Approximately 50,000 jobs are tied directly to the sectors and products affected by the US tariffs.
*   **Supply Chain Effects:** An additional 35,000 jobs are at risk due to the ripple effects on suppliers. As exporters shrink, they purchase less from upstream companies in professional services, transportation, and manufacturing, leading to further job losses across the country.

While this represents a significant hit to specific sectors, Tombe notes that the affected products account for roughly 5% of total Canadian exports to the US. Consequently, the increase in the effective tariff rate is about 2%, raising the average tariff on Canadian exports to approximately 7%. This is estimated to slow employment and GDP growth by 0.4%, a measurable but not recession-inducing shock.

## Provincial Disparities

The economic pain is not evenly distributed across the country. Tombe identifies **Ontario** as the province facing the largest absolute job losses, estimating around 36,000 jobs at risk due to its size and manufacturing base. However, **British Columbia** faces the most severe relative impact, with 13% of its total exports subject to the new 50% tariffs, largely due to wood products and electronic components.

## The "Sovereignty" vs. Economics Debate

A central point of contention is the government's rationale for retaliation. Former Deputy Prime Minister Chrystia Freeland and Prime Minister Mark Carney have argued that Canadians are prepared to endure economic pain to protect their sovereignty, citing a survey where 75% of Canadians agree with this stance.

Tombe challenges this framing. He argues that Canada is not being uniquely singled out by the US; rather, the US is raising tariffs on all trading partners, with an average global tariff rate now around 12%. "I think we should view them more as US domestic policy changes," Tombe stated. He contends that retaliation is counterproductive because it:
1.  Further hurts the Canadian economy.
2.  Makes Canadian exports to other countries less competitive.
3.  Increases costs for Canadian consumers.
4.  Inflames political tensions without necessarily changing US behavior.

## Consumer Costs and Inflation

Beyond job losses, Canadians face immediate financial strain. Tombe estimates that the retaliatory tariffs will add approximately 0.25% to the Consumer Price Index (CPI). For an average family, this translates to an additional $170–$250 in annual costs, depending on household composition.

This tariff-induced inflation compounds existing pressures from rising energy prices. With oil prices up roughly 50% year-to-date (from ~$60 to ~$90 per barrel), the average Canadian family faces an additional $1,000 in annual expenses. Tombe argues that while the labor market is flatlining, the price side of the equation is the more immediate concern for most households.

## The Long-Term Growth Crisis

The trade war has occurred against the backdrop of a decade of sluggish Canadian economic growth. Tombe points out that Canada’s real GDP per capita growth has been the lowest in the OECD over the past 10 years. Had Canada kept pace with the US, the economy would be nearly 20% larger today—a difference of over $600 billion in annual income.

Tombe identifies three primary causes for this stagnation:
1.  **Commodity Price Decline:** The 2015-2016 drop in oil and commodity prices hit the resource-dependent economy hard.
2.  **Trade Uncertainty:** Sectors dependent on the US market have flatlined, while non-dependent sectors have grown, pulling down aggregate growth.
3.  **Domestic Policy:** Increased taxes and regulatory burdens have made Canada less attractive for investment and capital accumulation.

## Policy Recommendations

Rather than focusing on trade retaliation, Tombe urges the Canadian government to focus on domestic policy reforms to boost competitiveness and productivity. He proposes several specific measures:

*   **100% Capital Depreciation:** Allow firms to write off capital spending (machinery, equipment) 100% at the time of purchase, similar to operating expenses. This would effectively eliminate the tax on investment, currently estimated at 13%.
*   **Income Tax Reform:** Flatten the income tax schedule to attract skilled talent, potentially funded by raising the GST. Tombe highlights the "brain drain" to the US, noting that a 20 percentage point tax difference between British Columbia and Washington State significantly impacts talent retention.
*   **Regulatory Reform:** Make the streamlined permitting processes for "major projects" permanent and available to all investors, not just designated ones.
*   **Interprovincial Trade:** Automatically recognize professional credentials across provinces to facilitate the free flow of services.

## Conclusion

The US-Canada trade war presents a complex challenge for Canadian policymakers. While the political narrative emphasizes sovereignty and resistance to US pressure, economist Trevor Tombe argues that the most effective way to defend Canadian interests is through domestic reform. By improving the investment climate, reducing taxes, and easing regulations, Canada can offset the costs of higher US trade barriers and restore its long-term growth trajectory. As Tombe concludes, "We don't need to cede any of our sovereignty to another country. We just need to focus on what we can control ourselves."
