## A Deep Dive into the 8-4 Split and What It Means for Markets

The Federal Reserve's April FOMC meeting delivered more than a routine rate decision—it delivered the most divided vote in over three decades. With four dissenting members breaking ranks from the 8-4 decision to hold rates steady, the central bank signaled something far more consequential than policy paralysis: a fractured institution facing mounting pressure from multiple directions simultaneously.

Sri Kumar, President of Sri Kumar Global Strategies, joined CNBC to unpack what this historic split reveals about the Fed's internal struggles, the incoming chair's precarious position, and what investors should expect as the year unfolds.

"The Fed has essentially sideline its inflation mandate," Kumar observed. "They can focus on growth and that's not what the Fed is supposed to do."

---

## The 8-4 Split: What the Dissenters Really Want

The April FOMC vote marked the highest number of dissents since October 1992—a rare event that demands attention. The four dissenting members included:

- **Steven Meyerson** (departing governor)
- **Beth Hammack** (Cleveland)
- **Neel Kashkari** (Minneapolis)
- **Lorie Logan** (Dallas)

The dissents weren't反对 the hold itself but rather the easing bias embedded in the committee's forward guidance. These regional presidents objected to language suggesting rate cuts were "more likely" in the future, arguing the statement should remain neutral given current conditions.

"This tells me there is going to be a lot of opposition to Kevin Walsh in terms of his wanting to cut rates," Kumar explained. "Three regional bank presidents are going to stay, and since he will not have any other person joining him there, he is going to have a difficult situation getting a majority together to cut rates."

The implications are significant: any incoming chair hoping to deliver rate cuts will face immediate institutional resistance from these hawks—and potentially from remaining governors like Lisa Cook, whom President Trump has attempted to remove without success.

---

## Kevin Walsh's Impossible Position

The confirmation of Kevin Walsh as the next Fed chair introduces fresh complications to an already fragile situation. Walsh's personal connections to the President—married to Jane Lauder, daughter of billionaire Ronald Lauder, who has personally sponsored Trump campaigns—raise legitimate questions about the Fed's vaunted independence.

More troubling than the personal relationships, according to Kumar, is Walsh's track record of convenient policy reversals:

- In 2009-2010, during the aftermath of the global financial crisis, Walsh criticized the Fed for increasing its balance sheet and advocated for reduction—a hawkish stance
- By late 2024, Walsh reversed entirely, calling for rate cuts—a position that conveniently aligned with Trump's desires

"He has to convince everybody that it was not career advancement which led him to change his mind," Kumar noted. "He has yet to prove that point."

Trump himself has made no secret of his expectations. Reports indicate he chose Walsh specifically because he promised to cut rates—and reportedly would not have been nominated if he'd advocated for higher rates.

"The risk Walsh runs is that if he changes his tune again and says, 'I think I should keep interest rates high or even raise them with oil prices going up,' the President is going to use all kinds of epithets with him, call him stupid, as he did with the outgoing chairman."

## The Fed's QE Trap: Caught Between Contradictory Goals

The Federal Reserve faces an uncomfortable truth it rarely acknowledges publicly: its balance sheet tells a very different story than its interest rate policy. Despite maintaining elevated rates, the Fed has been quietly expanding its balance sheet by approximately $200 billion in recent months.

Kumar doesn't hesitate to call this what it is:

"I will call it QE, even though the Federal Reserve says they are not buying long-term issues. They are only buying short-term issues and therefore it is not QE. That makes no sense to me. Quantitative easing is when you increase the balance sheet of the Fed—no matter whether you do it with short-dated assets or long-dated assets. In either case, you put the equivalent amount of cash into the system and you make the situation easier."

The Fed's justification—that they're merely conducting "repurchase operations" to prevent cash shortages among banks—doesn't change the fundamental impact. When the Fed increases its balance sheet, it injects reserves into the banking system, which eases financial conditions regardless of the stated policy rate.

The Fed's dilemma is structural. Every attempt to reduce its balance sheet (quantitative tightening) has encountered problems:

- **September 2019**: Cash shortage forced reversal
- **March 2023**: Regional banking crisis emerged, requiring emergency liquidity injections

Each time the Fed has tried to exit its ultra-easy posture, market stress has forced it back in. This creates a trap: the central bank cannot genuinely tighten without risking systemic disruption.

"The Fed is also currently increasing the balance sheet, which is also an easing measure," Kumar observed. "As long as that continues, it is going to be difficult to convince everybody that the Fed is being hawkish."

## The Inflation Mirage: Why the Fed Should Be Hiking

Jerome Powell's press conference projected calm confidence. "I think our policy rate is in a good place," he stated, adding that the committee could move "in either direction" if needed. But Kumar sees through this rhetoric to a more troubling reality.

"The 2% inflation target is essentially a mockery. It has never been met. They keep repeating it every time as if they are somehow going to go back to it. Instead, inflation is moving away toward the 3 to 3.5% range."

The numbers support this critique. For five consecutive years, the Fed has failed to achieve its stated inflation objective. The Iran war and subsequent oil spike have worsened the situation, but Kumar emphasizes that inflation was rising well before geopolitical events:

"Even without the war, we had inflation going up because of the quantitative easing taking place and because interest rates were too low. The evidence suggests that inflation was rising before, and it says to me that the interest rate should have been increased even before the Iran war began."

The Fed's own record contradicts its current stance. At the end of 2025, the committee delivered three rate cuts, citing concerns about employment. "They reduced interest rates three times because Powell said at that time he was more concerned about employment than inflation," Kumar noted. "Turned out to be once again a wrong assessment. Inflation was the problem. Employment was not."

## The Neutral Rate Reality

When asked where the "neutral" federal funds rate should be—meaning the rate that neither stimulates nor restricts the economy—Kumar's answer was unambiguous:

"The neutral rate should be quite a bit higher than where it is. The federal funds rate today at between 3.5% and 3.75% range is not sufficiently restrictive."

His reasoning is straightforward:

- Employment remains relatively strong
- Economic growth continues
- The stock market has performed extraordinarily well based on corporate earnings

"If that is the case, you cannot argue that [rates] are restrictive," Kumar argued. "The stock market doing extraordinarily well also suggests that interest rates are not restrictive enough—or that the natural rate of interest should be higher than where it is today."

The stock market's resilience despite prolonged elevated rates is particularly telling. Typically, restrictive monetary policy weighs on equities. Instead, corporate earnings optimism has powered indices to all-time highs—suggesting financial conditions remain loose by historical standards.

---

## The Labor Market Paradox

The unemployment rate sits at 4.3%—low by historical standards. Yet economists describe the current environment as a "no hiring, no firing" phase, where employment remains stable but new job creation has dried up. Kumar sees structural problems that rate cuts cannot solve:

"With artificial intelligence coming and taking an important role in the economy, you're going to find the 20 to 25-year-old people who are trying to enter the job force are going to have a very difficult time. Legal assistants, computer assistants, medical assistants trying to get their first job are going to find AI replaces them, and many of those jobs are going to go away."

This creates a fundamental challenge for anyone hoping monetary policy can rescue the labor market:

"If you cut interest rates by one-quarter point or half a point, will you get the job that you didn't get before? My answer is that is a structural issue. When the structure does not permit you to get employment, lower interest rates is not going to make up for that."

Kumar places the responsibility squarely on fiscal policy:

"It has to be through a reduction in tariffs. It has to be through investment incentives—which are very different from cutting interest rates. Right now, the higher level of tariffs is affecting employment, and so the employer is not going to hire more workers just because interest rates went down by a quarter point."

## The Warsh Problem: Can He Defy Trump?

Kevin Walsh's approach to reconciling his campaign promise to cut rates with an inflationary environment has attracted significant attention. He has suggested that reducing the Fed's balance sheet could compensate for lower interest rates—essentially tightening through one mechanism while loosening through another.

Kumar finds this problematic:

"Every time the Fed has tried to cut the balance sheet, it happened twice before—once in September 2019 and a second time in March 2023—and they ran into difficulty with cash shortages, a regional banking crisis, and they quickly had to revert away from quantitative tightening to increasing the balance sheet again."

The March 2023 episode is particularly instructive. When Silicon Valley Bank failed, depositors were bailed out beyond deposit insurance limits, sending a clear message: the Fed will rescue the system when stress emerges. This implicit guarantee means financial institutions have become accustomed to easy Fed policy and will likely test its resolve again.

"Whether that is going to be increased or decreased with the passage of time... Kevin Walsh has said he will make up for the lower interest rate by reducing the balance sheet," Kumar noted. "All the financial institutions have got used to a very easy Fed, and they also know that they would get bailed out in case of difficulties."

## Market Outlook: What to Watch

### Bonds and the Yield Curve

The 10-year Treasury yield spiked immediately following the FOMC announcement before giving up gains by Powell's speech conclusion. Kumar sees this as a critical signal:

"The long end of the yield curve is not controlled by the Federal Reserve. So if in fact Walsh cuts interest rates in his new Fed, the yield curve is going to steepen—the short end comes down, and the long end goes up."

Even the 2-year yield, typically predictive of Fed policy expectations, rose during the press conference—suggesting markets don't believe rate cuts are forthcoming given oil prices and inflation dynamics.

Kumar's bottom line: "I will be watching his first meeting. I'll be watching for his press conference, and if he indicates he's ready to cut interest rates, then I think the long end is going to go up in terms of yield."

### Gold

Gold's behavior has been counterintuitive. It fell during the Iran war's early days rather than rising as a traditional safe haven. Kumar attributes this to the dollar's appeal:

"The reason gold was doing well in the second half of 2025 was the push at that time for interest rates to remain low indefinitely and the President's criticism of Powell for keeping interest rates constant. If Walsh shows a backbone and shows some strength, that will cause gold price to go down."

The mechanism: if Walsh signals independence from Trump's rate-cutting demands, the dollar strengthens as a competing safe asset, reducing gold's appeal.

### Stocks

The S&P 500 sits near all-time highs despite (or perhaps because of) chaos. Kumar draws uncomfortable parallels to the dot-com era:

"I lived through the 1999 situation when you had the dot-com boom, and people said any company with a '.com' after it—nothing can ever go wrong with it. Then you had the stock market collapse starting about March 2000, and we had the recession of 2001."

The current situation mirrors that excess: overinvestment in data centers and AI across the board. "Data centers are going to do well. AI is going to do well. But not everybody who calls himself a data center is going to do well. There's an excess of investment in data centers, excess of investment in artificial intelligence, and quite some of that is going to be wasteful."

This creates a critical distinction for investors:

- **Security selection** will matter enormously
- The entire sector won't rise uniformly
- Weak hands will be vulnerable to drawdowns

Kumar's sector recommendations:

- **Energy**: Oil prices likely to continue rising, benefiting the sector
- **Defense**: Ongoing conflict supports defense-related industries
- **Value stocks**: Basic healthcare and traditional value remain attractive
- **Technology/AI**: Proceed with caution—substantial gains create vulnerability

## The Economic Slowdown Is Coming

The Atlanta Fed's GDPNow estimate sits at 1.2% for Q1 2026—down sharply from previous readings. Chapter 11 filings have risen 37% year-over-year. Kumar doesn't expect rate cuts to rescue the economy:

"There is a slowdown that is coming, but this is happening because of tariffs. It is happening because of the Iran war. When you have those present, I don't think you can offset that simply by cutting interest rates."

The historical precedent is instructive—and alarming:

"What we tried to do in the 1970s: oil prices increased very sharply from 1973 onward. President Nixon along with Arthur Burns, the chairman of the Federal Reserve, wanted to follow easy monetary policy in order to counteract the impact of higher oil prices. Effectively, what happened was it pushed you into a recession which was very severe and at the same time pushed up inflation as well. That is the risk that we want to avoid in the current situation."

## Key Takeaways

1. **The Fed is deeply divided**: An 8-4 split hasn't occurred since 1992, and the dissenting regional presidents signal ongoing resistance to rate cuts from any new chair who might pursue an accommodating agenda.

2. **Kevin Walsh faces an impossible balancing act**: Between presidential pressure for rate cuts and institutional resistance from hawks, Walsh must either defy Trump or risk credibility on inflation.

3. **The Fed is already easing through the back door**: Despite holding rates steady, the balance sheet expansion represents quantitative easing—making the hawkish rhetoric inconsistent with actual policy.

4. **Inflation should be addressed, not ignored**: The 2% target hasn't been met in five years, and rate cuts risk embedding inflation expectations at 3-3.5% or higher.

5. **Structural problems require structural solutions**: Labor market weakness driven by AI disruption and tariff-related uncertainty cannot be solved by monetary policy alone—fiscal interventions are needed.
