When Professor Steve Hanke was asked what keeps him up at night, his answer was characteristically blunt: "Nothing." The Johns Hopkins applied economics professor and former advisor to governments around the world sleeps soundly—but he believes many in Washington should be losing sleep over a crisis that's largely gone unnoticed by the public.

In a wide-ranging interview covering geopolitics, oil markets, monetary policy, and commodity investing, Hanke painted a picture of an America that has weakened its own strategic position through a series of interconnected missteps, while China emerges as the unlikely winner of conflicts it didn't even fight.

## The Munitions Crisis America Isn't Talking About

The question came during a dinner conversation with a family friend: Could the U.S. actually run out of money fighting Iran? The intuitive answer—that a country can simply print its own currency will never face bankruptcy—misses the point entirely, according to Hanke.

"You're never going to hit the inflation target of 2% if you grow the money supply over my golden growth rate of about 6% per year," Hanke explained. "So that's one aspect. But the other aspect is that the bond vigilantes are out there and they've woken up. If you're spending a lot of money on defense and that causes the deficit to go up and you have to issue more treasuries—and those treasuries aren't monetized and sucked up by the Federal Reserve—they're sold to the non-bank public. And the non-bank public demands higher interest rates because they're worried that maybe the printing press will run."

But the more immediate problem isn't money—it's munitions.

"They've already pretty much run out of munitions," Hanke said flatly, referencing a Fortune magazine article he co-authored with Jeffrey Wang released just hours before the interview. The U.S. has supplied the vast majority of ammunition in its proxy war with Russia through Ukraine, along with much of the financing, intelligence, and target coordination. Now, with the U.S.-Israeli war in Iran, more munitions have been expended—and reports from the New York Times on down confirm the "cupboard is bare."

### The Raw Materials Dependency Nobody in Washington Considered

Here's where Hanke's analysis cuts deepest: replacing those munitions requires critical raw materials—and over 95% of those rare earths and critical materials come from China.

"In short, to replenish the stockpile of munitions, we really are going to have to get permission from Chairman Z," Hanke said. "These people in Washington haven't thought through the thing. If you're burning up munitions and you have to replace ammunitions, where do you get the raw materials to do the replacements? Well, the key ones all come from China. And end of story."

The implication is stark: before President Trump meets with China's leadership, "he'll be on his best behavior," Hanke predicted.

## The Strategic Chessboard: China Wins, U.S. Loses

When a reporter recently asked Trump if he was bothered by reports that China was supplying weapons to Iran, his response was telling: "Well, we're doing that with other countries, aren't we? So why should I be upset about that?"

Hanke sees truth in Trump's relativism—but argues the geopolitical outcome is unambiguous.

"Geopolitically, the war in Iran has strengthened China tremendously," Hanke declared. "China's a winner and Russia is a winner and the U.S. is a strategic loser. It's probably one of the most significant strategic losses the U.S. has ever had in a war."

He drew comparisons to past American defeats, then dismissed them: "We lost the war in Afghanistan, but okay. We lost the war in Vietnam. Yeah. We really didn't win anything in Iraq. Well, okay. But this Iranian thing is much different."

### Why This Conflict Is Different

The critical distinction, Hanke explained, is geography. Iran—directly or through allies like the Houthis—controls two of the world's most vital maritime chokepoints:

- **The Strait of Hormuz**: Through which roughly 20% of the world's oil passes
- **The Red Sea entrance**: Controlled by Houthi forces who are Iranian allies

"Add the Red Sea to it, and you've got two out of the eight chokepoints in the world under the control of basically Iran," Hanke said. The strategic implications extend far beyond oil—fertilizer, aluminum, sulfur, and enormous proportions of global trade flow through these passages.

Hanke also posed a question that strikes at the heart of modern warfare: "How does one win a war against a country that's able to supply and rearm itself at a much lower cost than you are and can probably hold out their positions longer than you can before you explode your deficit?"

The answer, he suggested, is that you often can't—witness the Muhammad Ali "rope-a-dope" strategy. "They take a pounding and wait for the big giant to become exhausted, and then they land a knockout blow."

## Oil at the Crossroads: Supply, Spikes, and Strategic Blocs

Brent crude briefly spiked to $126—the highest level since 2022—as geopolitical tensions escalated. Yet first-quarter GDP came in at a healthy 2%, leading some to wonder why higher oil prices hadn't pushed the economy into recession.

Hanke wasn't surprised—but he wasn't optimistic either.

"There's always a lag between these prices and the effects," he noted. "By the way, 2% is not that great. The year-over-year last year was just 2.1%. So it's not even as hot as the yearly average for last year. And forget the AI hype—the AI people said it was going to be 5% or 6%."

### The Economics of Oil Price Shocks

The short-term insensitivity of oil demand to price changes is well-established in economics. Hanke cited the price elasticity of demand at approximately 0.1% in the short run, meaning a 10% increase in oil prices produces only a 1% drop in quantity demanded.

"What that means is that in the short run, you're drawing down your inventories," Hanke explained. "The quantity demanded hasn't changed that much. Factories haven't shut down. When the inventories come to an end, maybe the price is really going to go up. There'll be a lot of factories that shut down."

His prediction from a March 28th appearance—that oil would spike at the end of April—proved prescient. "The stupidity of the American blockade in the strait," as he characterized it, combined with inventory depletion, creates the conditions for future price explosions.

### The UAE's OPEC Exit and Optimal Pumping Theory

Perhaps the most underreported story of the period was the United Arab Emirates' announcement that it would leave OPEC on May 1st. Hanke, who served on the UAE's Financial Advisory Council from 2008 to 2014, saw it coming—and connected it to his own theoretical framework.

The model Hanke developed—optimal pumping rates for in-situ resources like oil—suggests that when producers believe future supply will be disrupted, they should accelerate current production. The Iran war changes the calculus dramatically.

"If you think you're not going to have the oil in the future to pump, what do you do?" Hanke asked. "You increase a discount rate. You discount future potential oil revenues at a much higher rate, meaning those potential revenues aren't worth very much in present value terms. And you tilt your pumping—you accelerate your current pumping."

The UAE has chafed under OPEC quota restrictions for years while arguing for increased production. Now, with the Strait of Hormuz potentially vulnerable to Iranian control, the calculus has shifted.

"If they shut the strait, that's a problem for the UAE—they won't be able to ship out most of their oil," Hanke explained. "It's like having an expropriation of their oil fields if that happens. The other thing that could happen—they have drones and ballistic missiles, and they could clearly destroy the oil production capacity in the UAE."

The message to other OPEC members? "OPEC is basically kind of a dead letter anyway."

## The Federal Reserve's Monetarist Problem

Jerome Powell described the economy as "quite resilient," citing strong growth and what he called "the apparently insatiable demand for data centers all over the United States." Hanke's assessment was less charitable.

"Instead of his positive spin, I would say it's a very lackluster—it has its own merits, but it's about where the yearly average was last year, which wasn't all that great," Hanke said. "Remember the first year of Trump's presidency, the GDP declined. The rate of growth in GDP was lower in 2025 than it was in 2024. The rate of productivity increase was lower in 2025 than in 2024. So we're losing steam. We're not gaining—we're losing."

### Kevin Walsh and the Monetarist Opportunity

With Kevin Walsh ascending to a key position at the Fed, Hanke sees potential for change—but significant obstacles.

"Banks are the biggest contributor to the money supply—that's true," Hanke acknowledged. "About 80% of broad money is produced by commercial banks. And it can be controlled very easily by changing reserve requirements, changing bank regulations."

Hanke and co-author Matt Sukhatme laid out exactly how to implement monetarist principles in their book *Making Money Work*, released in May 2025. But the Fed's research staff presents a structural challenge.

"The research staff of the Fed system has over 750 economists, and the ratio of Democrats to Republicans is over 50 to 1," Hanke revealed. "They're almost all Democrats. Now if you're a Democrat, what economist has a target on his back? Milton Friedman—the dean of monetarism."

The political dimension is real. When Joe Biden was elected, one of his first statements was that "Milton Friedman isn't going to run the show anymore," Hanke noted.

"Forget about partisanship—just look at the ratio of Democrats to Republicans: 50 to 1," he said. "If you have a Republican president who's activist and wants to influence things at the Fed, they would want that ratio changed a little bit. Plus, most of the Republicans are old-timers and about ready to retire. So if you look at the younger demographic, the ratio would be way higher than 50 to 1."

## Investing in the Commodity Super Cycle

Hanke's investment advice for the period ahead was unambiguous: pivot from high-tech to hard commodities.

"We're entering a super cycle with the commodities," he declared. "Defense requires arms, and arms require raw materials—and that means commodities."

The data supports the thesis. Since December, a range of critical materials has surged:

**Selected Commodity Performance (Since December):**

- Ferrovanadium: +90%
- Tantalum: +133%
- Lithium spotamine: +52%
- Lithium carbonate: +39%
- Lithium hydroxide: +49%
- Molybdenum: +25%
- Niobium: +28%
- Aluminum: +22%
- Tin: +21%
- Steel: +16%

"Germany is rearming. Everybody's increasing the proportion of GDP they're spending on defense," Hanke noted. "You want to be long commodities. This won't be linear—prices will go up, spike, then pull back, then go up again. A super cycle in commodities always has spikes associated with it."

The connection to earlier themes is direct: "We're restocking the munitions. You want to be in hard assets, low obsolescence. That's the category you want to be in."

Oil plays a role in this super cycle through commodity indexes. "If you look at the Bloomberg commodity index, it's very heavily skewed to oil," Hanke explained. "Most commodity indexes are heavily weighted in oil."

## Gold's Path to $6,000–$7,000

Gold has long been a favorite of Hanke's, and despite a recent pullback consolidating around $4,600, he remains bullish on the secular bull market.

The inverse relationship between gold and Treasury yields has reasserted itself to some degree—but Hanke doesn't see rising yields as a death knell for the yellow metal.

"It takes a little steam out of the gold bull run," he admitted, "but I think the secular bull market will peak out. I've said on this show before—6,000 was the first thing I said. Then when gold really started taking off, I said, 'Well, God, maybe I better put a range on the thing. Six to seven thousand.' And that's still where I'm at."

## Key Takeaways

1. **The U.S. munitions crisis is real and strategically dangerous**: America has depleted its stockpiles in proxy wars, but replenishment requires rare earths and critical materials that China controls—giving Beijing effective veto power over American rearmament.

2. **China is the unexpected winner of the Iran conflict**: Through geographic control of critical chokepoints (Strait of Hormuz, Red Sea) and dominance over supply chains for military production, China gains leverage without firing a shot.

3. **The bond vigilantes are watching**: While the U.S. can technically "print money," doing so without Fed monetization risks inflation and higher interest rates—pressures already visible in the 10-year Treasury yield creeping toward 4.4%.

4. **A commodity super cycle is underway**: As nations rearm and seek supply security, prices for ferrovanadium, lithium, tantalum, and other critical materials are surging—presenting investment opportunities alongside short-term volatility.

5. **Monetarism faces an institutional uphill battle**: The Fed's research staff is overwhelmingly left-leaning (50:1 Democrat-to-Republican ratio), and monetarist principles—tracing back to Milton Friedman—face political headwinds that Kevin Walsh would need to overcome to implement meaningful reform.

For those seeking to understand the intersection of geopolitics, monetary policy, and commodity markets, Hanke's framework offers a coherent lens—one built on decades of economic analysis and a willingness to challenge conventional wisdom in Washington.
