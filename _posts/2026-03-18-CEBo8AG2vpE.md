**Recorded: March 18th | Ahead of the Federal Reserve Meeting**

Investors appear to be shifting sides rapidly. Markets are skittish, precious metals are selling off despite their safe-haven reputation, and uncertainty hangs in the air. Is this simple profit-taking, or a deeper change in sentiment? To decode the herd mentality, we turned to Jason Shapiro, author and analyst behind *The Crowded Market Report*.

### The Sentiment Swing: From Complacency to Concern

Shapiro pinpointed a notable shift in investor psychology surrounding global tensions. "When the war first broke out... there wasn't really a lot of concern," he observed, attributing initial market calm to "recency bias" from past, shorter-lived conflicts. The prevailing belief was that oil prices wouldn't stay elevated and stocks wouldn't remain depressed.

"That's kind of switched now," Shapiro said. "We're hearing a lot of people talk about how the war is getting worse... and how that's going to be for oil prices and then all the follow-ups: lower stocks, higher inflation." Ironically, as this bearish narrative has taken hold, "the stock market is acting better." For Shapiro, this sentiment gauge helps reveal what is—or isn't—being discounted into prices.

### The "Fed Put" and the Looming Stress Test

A central question is whether markets are again relying on the Federal Reserve to backstop any turmoil, especially in debt markets. Shapiro outlined mounting capital demands: voracious government borrowing globally, massive corporate funding needs for AI (noting that even cash-rich Meta is now borrowing and cutting staff), and the immense daily cost of war.

"Clearly a lot of issues there... You can't not fund the war. You can't not fund AI... You can't not fund the governments," he stated.

The critical stress test, in his view, will come if these pressures cause a market dislocation, particularly in fixed income. "Should the Fed... have to come in and start to do more QE... that's when it gets super interesting." He has long argued there is a limit to this dynamic, though its timing is unknown. The key signal will be the market's reaction: "If they come in and do QE in a big way... and the markets don't respond well to that... then we have a serious problem."

### Reading the Tea Leaves: Bonds, Gold, and Market Efficiency

With major indices largely stagnant since October, Shapiro sees a market lacking clear, crowded positioning—which often leads to directionless churn. The one notably crowded trade he identifies is in **long-term bonds**. "People have gotten pretty extended long in the 30-year bonds... We could get a big move down and that would be a big move up in interest rates."

Meanwhile, **gold**, the perceived "no-brainer" hedge during war, has confounded holders by trending downward. "Often times the no-brainer trade turns out to be not the no-brainer trade," Shapiro noted, adding he'd be bullish but doesn't "play alongside of markets that act terribly."

He sees today's market as a reflection of a divided society, where analysis is often filtered through political bias. "You can make either argument and people tend to make the argument right down that political line... I find it all net net to be useless because it's all biased."

### Data vs. The Tape: What's the Message?

Despite a worse-than-expected GDP revision and a hot PPI print of 3.4% (versus 2.9% expected), equities showed resilience. "The S&Ps are down 20 points. They haven't even given up yesterday's gains," Shapiro observed.

This divergence presents the classic puzzle: "The bulls will say because [the market] is giving the message... The bears will say, well, because people in the market don't realize what the hell is going on." Initially, Shapiro leaned bearish, thinking the market was underestimating risks. Now, he's "switching over to the idea... that it's a lot stronger than people think." However, the overarching theme remains range-bound action.

He theorized that the era of massive liquidity pumps may be giving way to a more "efficient market," especially with broad retail participation. "The more people that get involved the more efficient it becomes... and therefore the less net movement there is."

### The Bond Market's Critical Role

Shapiro emphasized that the recent reversal higher in bond yields (with the 10-year back to ~4.23%) is "not pretty." The real danger signal would be **rising yields coupled with widening credit spreads**. "If we get yields going up and spreads widening, we have a problem." This scenario could force the Fed's hand, making it perilous to be short. "Maybe nobody wants to be short because nobody wants to be caught up in that squeeze."

### Shapiro's Current Stance: Patience and a Single Trade

In this difficult environment for his contrarian, turning-point style, Shapiro is largely on the sidelines. "I have one trade on right now, which is **short the Canadian dollar**." His thesis is based on crowded long positioning and the currency's failure to rally on ostensibly positive news (like high oil prices). He recently covered a short Russell position as his data shifted.

His discipline stems from a 26-year process. "If my process is saying don't do anything then I don't do anything... I've been doing this long enough to realize that there are times when there's nothing to do." He manages expectations, acknowledging that not every year brings huge returns, but risk management keeps drawdowns in check.

### The Bottom Line

For Shapiro, the immediate puzzle is whether resilient equity markets are correctly discounting a stronger future or ignoring gathering storms. The bond market remains the critical canary in the coal mine, with its reaction to relentless capital demands and potential Fed intervention holding the key to the next major move. Until clearer crowding extremes or a decisive market message emerges, his advice for the herd might be summed up in his own strategy: careful observation, strict process, and the wisdom to sometimes do nothing at all.
