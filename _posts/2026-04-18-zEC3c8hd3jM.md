## A Conversation with Economic Forecaster Martin Armstrong on Commodity Culture

The current conflict between Iran, the United States, and Israel represents one of the most consequential geopolitical crises of our time—and according to economic forecaster Martin Armstrong, the world is woefully unprepared for its consequences.

In a wide-ranging interview on the Commodity Culture podcast, Armstrong, who has consulted for governments including the Reagan administration and Margaret Thatcher, pulled no punches in his assessment of the situation. His analysis weaves together military strategy, economic vulnerability, and a sweeping historical perspective that paints a troubling picture of where global instability is headed.

## "Netanyahu Has Been Trying to Sell This War for 45 Years"

The conflict, Armstrong argues, cannot be understood without examining the motivations of Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, whom he describes in stark terms.

"Netanyahu has been trying to sell this war on Iran to just about every president for the last 45 years," Armstrong said. "He made a big speech before the United Nations in 2010 or 2012 saying, 'Oh, we have to take them out.' He's, in my opinion, I think he is as deranged as the people he hates on the other side."

Armstrong's critique extends beyond simple disagreement. He sees the current military campaign as strategically bankrupt, arguing that there is no coherent endgame.

"All you do is think that this endless war as if you can conquer the other side and make it all better. I mean, you're just creating another generation of people that are going to hate you," he explained. "I just think he's honestly deranged or possessed or something."

## The Strategic Blunder Nobody Discussed

Beyond the political motivations, Armstrong points to what he considers a catastrophic oversight in the planning stages of the military campaign: the complete failure to address the strategic importance of the Strait of Hormuz.

The Strait of Hormuz is not merely a transit point for oil—it is the circulatory system of the global economy. More than 20 percent of the world's oil supplies pass through these waters, along with natural gas, critical minerals, and other commodities essential to modern civilization. But Armstrong highlights an aspect of the strait's importance that few in Washington apparently considered.

"There's another serious issue that people don't even understand," he said. "All the cables that connect the world financial system go through the streets of Hormuz."

Armstrong described firsthand experience with the region's infrastructure vulnerability. "I had offices there in UAE. They were attacking, you know, the AI, you know, project there, which was a $30 billion project. Amazon, OpenAI—all their systems are there. People don't know this. They attacked it. They did damage, not overwhelming, but the banking system there was down for seven days. My staff was there. I finally got them out."

The failure to secure the strait before initiating hostilities represents, in Armstrong's view, a fundamental lapse in strategic thinking. "I kind of was in an argument with some people, and I said, if I were going to do this, the first thing I would have done was secure the Strait of Hormuz before the first bomb went. I mean, I don't know. Are we being, is Washington full of just morons anymore?"

## Why Regime Change in Iran Was Never Realistic

The assumption underlying the military campaign—that Iran could be quickly neutralized—reveals a dangerous misunderstanding of history, Armstrong argues.

"Trump wrongly believed that because it was a Venezuela type thing," he said. "Let's face the facts. Nobody, not even the Roman Empire, was ever able to conquer Persia. It's a very mountainous area. You're not going to—it's not like Iraq, where you can just, you know, a flat plain that you can drive in tanks, etc."

Armstrong sees a pattern of hubris in American military thinking that consistently underestimates the difficulty of occupying or destroying determined adversaries. "The biggest always falls," he noted. "Persia was the biggest. They were defeated by the Greeks. Alexander the Great. Defeated by the Romans. Carthage was the big one. They were defeated by the fledgling Romans that came up. This is the biggest always falls. And it's usually because of hubris or arrogance."

The same arrogance, he suggests, led to the catastrophic miscalculation in Iraq. "He said the same thing about Iraq. Oh, weapons of mass destruction," Armstrong noted. "He's not trustworthy for anything that he says."

## The Economic Timebomb

While military strategists focused on regime change, Armstrong's analysis centers on the economic devastation that could cascade across the globe if the conflict escalates.

"If Iran really damages the refineries—and if Israel, which Netanyahu is completely insane in my opinion, if he arbitrarily goes after their production—they're going to take out everybody else's production," Armstrong warned. "That's going to create massive civil unrest on a global scale."

The vulnerability is particularly acute in Europe and Asia. The United States, Armstrong explains, imports only 3 to 5 percent of its oil from the Middle East. But for Europe and Asia, the situation is dramatically different.

"What our computer has been projecting—and I've said for the last couple of years—is that the US would go into a recessionary period, but Europe could go into a depression," Armstrong said. "Europe is dry. So you see things outside much worse."

The food security implications are equally severe. Approximately 30 percent of the world's fertilizer supply transits through the Strait of Hormuz region. Already, Armstrong notes, energy protests and farmer uprisings are occurring in Greece and Ireland due to fertilizer shortages. The situation could deteriorate rapidly.

"Even with the worst-case scenario aside, there's usually food shortages during war anyhow," Armstrong advised. "You had the victory gardens from World War II. You should have a couple years of food."

## A World at War with Itself

Armstrong's analysis extends far beyond the immediate conflict. His predictive model—the Socrates AI system developed over decades—portrays the current crisis as symptomatic of a broader global transformation, one characterized by the proliferation of conflicts rather than a single large-scale war.

"Our computer's been projecting that this would be World War III, but not like World War I or II, where it's one adversary you're facing," Armstrong explained. "It's pockets of uprisings everywhere. So, for example, you're seeing what our computer was warning about: Thailand versus Cambodia all of a sudden. India versus Pakistan. Pakistan versus Afghanistan. Then you got North Korea versus the South. China versus Taiwan. It's everywhere."

The pattern, Armstrong suggests, is not random but rather the predictable consequence of economic stress fracturing political structures. "When you turn the economy down like this, and we see it getting much worse going into 2028, all these disputes will rise to the surface."

## The Death of International Law

The current crisis, Armstrong argues, has exposed the complete hollowness of international legal frameworks. The International Criminal Court has found Netanyahu culpable for actions in Gaza that constitute genocide, yet there are no mechanisms for enforcement.

"What Netanyahu has done is far worse," Armstrong said. "His assassination tactics—if you call a peace conference, would you go when you know he uses that as a means to kill you? How do you negotiate? I mean, he has thrown international law out the window. He doesn't give about anything. He's obsessed."

The result is a world where might makes right, where international institutions exist on paper but lack any real authority. "Is international law just dead at this point?" Armstrong asked rhetorically. "Is this going to become a every country for themselves, do whatever you want sort of system?"

## Europe and NATO: A Path to War with Russia

Beyond the immediate Middle Eastern crisis, Armstrong sees European powers moving toward an even more dangerous confrontation—with Russia. The motivations, he suggests, are less about security than about economic survival.

"The EU cannot survive without war with Russia," Armstrong declared. "It's breaking up. And when you're—just like Netanyahu. Afraid of losing power, he pushes this war. Europe is afraid of losing power. Economically, they are a disaster."

French President Emmanuel Macron, Armstrong suggests, is operating from a deeply unrealistic assessment of European capabilities. "You have Macron who I think lives in La La Land and really thinks that they can conquer Russia, get the 75 trillion in natural resource assets, and then rise again like the Roman Empire to rule the world."

These ambitions have faced a rude awakening. "NATO came out—and Rutte came out—and he actually said, 'If you think that you can conquer Russia without the United States, you're dreaming.' So he publicly finally came out and said what I've been hearing for two years behind the curtain."

Germany's recent moves to restrict the travel of military-aged males and accelerate military preparations suggest that boots may indeed be headed for Ukrainian soil. "Oh, yeah," Armstrong responded when asked if he believed NATO troops would eventually deploy. "I mean, the EU cannot survive without war with Russia."

## The Collapse of the Current Order

Throughout the interview, Armstrong repeatedly returns to what he sees as the fundamental failure of the current political system—not just in the United States but globally. The conflict has laid bare the distance between democratic rhetoric and democratic reality.

"Congress was not even asked, no less the people," Armstrong noted. "And then they tell us we live in a democracy. This is total absolute BS. We don't get to decide anything. Vietnam War, the same thing. You're 18, old enough to get drafted. You weren't old enough to even have a drink. You had to be 21 to vote. So, oh, but that's democracy. No, it's not."

Armstrong draws a distinction between republics and true democracies, arguing that the republican system was a deliberate choice by the Founders to limit popular participation rather than expand it. "They chose a republic rather than democracy. They rejected the Greek model and took the Roman model," he explained. "A republic is—I can run for anything. And look, I know how politics works. I want to run for Congress. I can tell you anything you want to hear. I'll save the whales. I'll do this. Then I get there. The meeting comes. Great. You're here. This is how it works. Just look at all the votes down party line. The guys in the back room make the decision. You don't."

## 2032: The Predicted Turning Point

Armstrong's Socrates computer has been projecting a fundamental transformation of the global order by 2032—a date that represents both economic and political inflection points.

"The computer's been projecting that for 2032 anyhow. This is just how you get there," Armstrong said. "We do see that China will rise as the new financial capital of the world after 2032. The United States won't completely fall apart in that sense, but it'd be more like the British Empire. It'd be a memory more than anything else."

But the projection includes a more hopeful scenario: the collapse of republican systems and a return to direct democracy. "What hopefully is that these republic forms of government will come to an end and we'll go back more towards a direct democracy," Armstrong said. "Like we should be asked, 'Shall we go to war with Netanyahu against Iran?'"

The historical precedent, Armstrong argues, suggests that such transformations are possible. "Rome overthrew their Tarquin king in 509 BC and within six months Greece goes, 'Oh, that's a good idea.' They overthrow their tyrants and democracies born, right? And then sweeps the whole Mediterranean area. These are not unique events. They're part of humanity."

## The Path Forward

As the Iran conflict continues and its economic consequences ripple across the globe, Armstrong's message is clear: the current approach is not merely ineffective but potentially catastrophic. The failure to consider the Strait of Hormuz's critical importance, the hubristic belief in quick victory, and the complete disregard for global economic interdependencies have created a crisis that could reshape the world order for generations.

"The only benefit out of this has been it's made Russia and China stronger," Armstrong noted. "Trump is now hostage to this thing. Once he agreed, he's kind of stuck."

Yet within the darkness, Armstrong sees potential for transformation—though it will require the kind of fundamental rethinking of political systems that humanity has achieved before, and may be capable of achieving again.

"This time, I think what we're seeing is that everybody's just—as you said, this isn't working," Armstrong concluded. "The more people that understand that are going to demand a political change. That's what 2032 is about. And then hopefully at that time, we get a constitutional convention, per se. Maybe somebody will look at this podcast and say, maybe they were right. Maybe we should look at a direct democracy this time instead of a republic."
