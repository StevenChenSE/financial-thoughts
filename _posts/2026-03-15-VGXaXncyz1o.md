For over a century, the security of the Middle East’s oil-rich kingdoms rested on the power of a global protector. First, it was the British who, for more than a hundred years, “promised and delivered on protection for these little oil kingdoms, including Saudi Arabia.” That mantle later passed to the United States, which “agreed privately in the 1930s to protect the Saudi oil resources.”

But according to geopolitical strategist Peter Zeihan, that era of American guardianship has reached a stark and undeniable end. “What we found out is that we can’t do that,” he states bluntly. The evidence, he argues, is written in the recent failures across the region.

The capabilities expected of a global security guarantor have evaporated. “We can’t shoot down the missiles. We can’t bring the Iranians, so to say, to heal.” Perhaps most telling, Zeihan points to the conflict in Yemen. “We couldn’t even defeat the Houthis,” he notes, describing them as “this tribe of backward Arabs living in the mountains in southern Yemen that everybody dismissed out of hand as incapable.”

Yet this underestimated force achieved what seemed unthinkable: “They shut down the Red Sea. They shut down commerce to the Suez Canal.” This single action disrupted a critical artery of global trade, exposing a profound vulnerability.

Zeihan uses this failure to pose a devastating rhetorical question about American power projection. “Why did we think if we could not stop that and defeat those people, how could we possibly take on a nation of 93 million people the size of Western Europe?”

The implication is clear. If the U.S. cannot secure maritime chokepoints from a regional militia, its decades-old promise to shield the vast oil reserves of the Persian Gulf from a major state adversary is effectively void. The strategic foundation that has undergirded global energy markets and alliances since the mid-20th century appears to have crumbled, leaving a new and uncertain landscape in its wake.
