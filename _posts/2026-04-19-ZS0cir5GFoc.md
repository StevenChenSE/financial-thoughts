**The world's leading resource investor weighs in on energy markets, fertilizers, gold, and where the real opportunities lie**

---

The commodity landscape has shifted dramatically since early 2024. On the back of escalating conflict in the Middle East, oil markets have experienced significant volatility, leaving investors to wonder whether current prices adequately reflect the true supply risks. To get clarity on these dynamics, we sat down with Rick Rule—financial analyst, investor, speculator, and founder of Rule Investment Media—for his long-term, fundamentally driven outlook on the commodity complex.

What emerged was a wide-ranging conversation covering everything from oil and gas to helium, fertilizers, gold, and the junior mining sector. Rule's perspective, forged over four decades of investing in natural resources, offers a framework for thinking about these markets that transcends the daily headlines.

---

## Oil Markets: Complacency or Opportunity?

When asked about current oil prices—with WTI futures trading around $90-95 per barrel—and whether markets are too complacent, Rule's response is characteristically nuanced.

"I think markets may be too complacent, but I need to say energy is a very complex topic," he begins. "I remember the last time you and I talked and I was able to say, because nobody liked oil and gas stocks, that oil and gas was a no-brainer. I, of course, wasn't prescient enough to have anticipated war. That's not what I do. But I noted that on a global basis, the world was underinvesting about a billion dollars a day in sustaining capital investments. And I suggested to you that the oil price could easily be $90 a barrel in 2029. It turned out that the war frontloaded that move."

The critical distinction, Rule emphasizes, is between the headline futures price and physical market reality. While WTI trades around $95, the market isn't reflecting the true supply constraints facing certain regions.

"The headlines will have you believe—and it's true—that 20% of the world's crude flows through the Straits of Hormuz. The more troubling number is that well over 50% of the world's export crude flows through those same straits."

For consumers in the Western Hemisphere, particularly the United States and Canada, the challenge is manageable due to ample North American supplies. But for nations on the eastern rim of the Pacific basin, the situation is far more precarious.

"And it's worthy to note that the WTI market, which is commonly quoted at $95 a barrel, is a futures price. Cargos that are loaded today on tankers that are south of the Straits of Hormuz are commanding as much as a $40 a barrel premium. Which is to say the futures market at $95 doesn't correspond well with a tanker loading in Pakistan or Sri Lanka at 140 or 145 a barrel."

This is the crux of the complacency argument. If the conflict persists for two to three more weeks, Rule suggests we'll see what happens when oil must be rationed by price. The world hasn't truly experienced a shortage yet because of floating inventory and strategic reserves. The price move from $65 to $95+ reflects *anticipation* of disruption, not the disruption itself.

## A Historical Parallel

When asked about historical parallels for sustained supply disruption, Rule points to the Arab oil embargo of the early 1970s.

"This wasn't war related. Well, I guess it was war related. If you remember the Israeli conflicts with virtually all of their neighbors, but it was a political act really in response to that that so greatly disrupted oil trade," he recalls. "It's worthy to know that decades of underinvestment combined with the constriction of supplies from the Persian Gulf was—and I need to add back inflation—partially responsible for a move over the decade of the '70s of the crude oil quote from $3 a barrel to $30."

Rule isn't suggesting a move of that magnitude is imminent, but he's clear that the underlying structural issue—systematic underinvestment in productive capacity—has merely accelerated. The destruction of Qatari liquefied natural gas infrastructure and Iranian infrastructure on Kharg Island will take as much as five years to repair, even under the most optimistic scenarios.

"The real problem around oil supply, which was systematic underinvestment, has accelerated as opposed to decreased," Rule says. "So while in the very near term—which is to say 2026—you see some relaxation in price, the circumstance that we talked about nine months ago for late 2028-2029 gets exacerbated. Most people, even commodity investors, don't think in the two to five year time frame, which is why most commodity investors don't make money."

## What Resolution Would Mean

If the conflict were to end tomorrow, Rule believes we'd see a rapid decline in oil prices. Near-term factors support this view: approximately 200 tankers currently sit north of the Straits of Hormuz, unable to exit. The anticipation of disruption has been priced in, but the reality has yet to fully materialize.

The United States has already taken the unusual step of anticipatory desanctioning Russian crude, which would further ease supply concerns.

"I would suspect that if the war ended, you would see a fairly rapid decline in oil prices," Rule acknowledges. But he immediately adds the caveat that makes this more complicated: "The circumstance that caused me to be an oil stock buyer, which had nothing to do with the war—which had everything to do with the systematic underinvestment in sustaining capital—hasn't changed."

For investors, this creates a dilemma. Oil equities have moved dramatically, and if the conflict resolves, prices would likely fall, returning these stocks to the "hated" category that Rule finds so attractive.

"This would be, in my own opinion, the calm before the storm. I'm hoping for a resolution of the conflict for a whole bunch of reasons that have nothing to do with my portfolio and everything to do with the future of humankind. But a resolution of the conflict and declining oil prices would put the oil stocks probably back into the hated category, which is where I want to see them because I'd like to buy more."

## Regional Opportunities

When considering where to look for opportunities, Rule highlights several regions.

**Scandinavia** remains geologically attractive. "The geological endowment in Scandinavia is spectacular and there is still at least the vestige of the rule of law." The challenge is political: Scandinavian governments have traditionally been anti-energy despite enjoying the social benefits of resource revenues. Rule notes the Lundin family, who after 50 years of trying scored a billion-barrel field in the Norwegian North Sea and sold it—a telling example of return expectations in the region.

**Australia's Woodside** and the development of the Northwest Shelf has opened a real alternative source of supply for East Asia. So prolific is this production that a ship of LNG recently landed in Atlantic Canada—something Rule finds paradoxical given Canada's own natural gas potential.

"Why the Canadians couldn't figure out the business case for supplying natural gas to Eastern Canada and instead chose to import it from Australia is one of the political absurdities of the world. I collect absurdities."

**The Permian Basin** is a different story entirely. While the rest of the world defers sustaining capital investment, the US is "right back to drill baby drill." This production surge is why Henry Hub natural gas prices have fallen so quickly—the byproduct gas from Permian oil wells is overwhelming infrastructure. The critical difference is that this isn't *sustaining* capital; it's growth spending.

In **Canada**, Rule sees a troubling pattern. "You are not seeing a great increase in sustaining capital expenditure in Canada, which I suspect is a consequence of the political leadership in Canada who, despite their kind words to the contrary, have proven to be continually anti-oil and gas."

Rule confesses he hasn't sold his Canadian oil stocks, hoping the geopolitical circumstances might give Canadian politicians the courage to allow infrastructure development. "I'm probably giving too much credit for Canadian politicians. They're probably incapable of rising to the moment. But I'd like to believe that they're capable of it. I'm very fond of that country and its people, and very very fond of the resource inventory."

## The Underinvestment Problem

Rule is particularly concerned about the arithmetic of deferred capital spending. The world is underinvesting in sustaining capital by approximately a billion dollars *per day*. The consequences are compounding.

"If you aren't recompleting wells, if you aren't drilling makeup wells, if you aren't replacing and repairing infrastructure, your ability to produce declines."

Venezuela's state oil company PDVSA, with the largest reserves in the world, saw production decline by 85% as a consequence of deferred sustaining capital investments. The country now faces a brutal bind: reduced production means reduced free cash flow, while the investment needed to restore output must be made at inflated costs. Two decades of underinvestment have coalesced with two decades of input inflation.

"And the other thing is, as you say, the fact that oil in inflation-adjusted terms is cheap and the fact that sustaining capital deferred today needs to be replaced three years from now in a cost environment where, as a consequence of inflation, the input costs are rising between five and eight percent a year compounded. Which is to say, a billion dollars deferred today will likely cost you $1.2 to $1.3 billion to make up three years from now, after you've already experienced the production declines, when it's more difficult to make that investment."

## Helium: An Intriguing Speculation

Moving to other commodities disrupted by the conflict, Rule turns to helium.

"Helium is interesting. I recently made a helium investment. With Qatar offline, the world was truly awash in helium. But that party came to a screeching halt."

Helium forms from the breakdown of uranium and accumulates in sedimentary horizons adjacent to large historical sources of uranium. This suggests promising targets in Saskatchewan, Canada, or Kazakhstan. However, Rule cautions that finding a pure-play vehicle is difficult.

"Helium's in the middle" between oil and gas and hard rock mining, he explains. "You have to drill out enough of it that you can afford to put in extraction infrastructure. In other words, you have to strip the helium from the other gases present."

If the Straits of Hormuz remain closed, Rule believes within two years the industry will have drilled sufficient wells to identify where global supplies will come from. But for now, this remains "probably the province of investors and speculators who are more sophisticated than the average ones. The probability of making mistakes is high. The impact on the helium market of the resumption of Qatari supply, if it occurs, are high too."

## Fertilizers: The Nitrogen Problem

On the agricultural side, urea—a nitrogenous fertilizer—has nearly doubled in price due to the disruption of Gulf gas infrastructure. The key question is whether potash and phosphates, which aren't directly disrupted, represent opportunities.

"They could," Rule begins, but immediately adds caveats. "I think you need to regard that as speculative because you're looking at macro circumstances that neither you nor I can control."

Gulf gas producers have a durable competitive advantage in nitrogenous fertilizers given abundant feedstock and massive fixed capital. Even when Gulf production comes back online, alternative natural gas sources will struggle to attract investment because developers know their projects will become marginal once again.

Potash tells a different story. "The largest and potentially lowest cost producer of potash is Russia. Far and away. And the Russians have not come anywhere close to making the sustaining capital and new project investments necessary to exploit their potash because they've had other uses for money. In a word, Ukraine. And there are some constraints on the export of Russian nutrients."

This creates an opportunity for **Saskatchewan producers**, who occupy second place globally but are well ahead of everyone except the Russians. "There's lots of scope to expand production. While they will not be as cheap as the Russians, they are much, much, much cheaper than anybody else. And it's a jurisdiction that for the most part has the advantage of the rule of law."

Morocco has become the world's leading phosphate producer, though the US's former dominance in Florida and South Carolina is "pretty well played out."

For long-term investors, fertilizers represent a demographic bet. "There are eight billion people on Earth. This is basically a GDP story. As poor people become less poor, one of the first things that happens is they increase their per capita consumption of calories. One of the great things we've done as a species in the last 40 years is we've moved two billion people away from being desperately poor to being very poor. We have a billion people to go. We'll accomplish that in the next 20 years. More people will eat more, and without the green revolution—without modern diamonium phosphate and those forms of fertilizer—we can't feed eight billion people. It's as simple as that."

Rule has been invested in nutrients for 25 years, weathering substantial cyclical volatility. The slow growth nature of the business means much of his return has come from dividends, which he has reinvested. His compound return on capital employed over that period has been "superb."

## Gold: The Ultimate Safe Haven?

Shifting to precious metals, Rule is characteristically direct about gold's role in a portfolio.

"I don't think that one measures safe haven in weeks," he begins. "I began systematically saving in gold in the [1970s], except for I wasn't. I was more of an investor and a speculator than I was a saver. From the time I developed a lot of surplus capital—which was the beginning of this millennium—I began to systematically save in gold and maintain liquidity in US dollars."

Over 26 years, gold has generated almost a 9% internal rate of return annually in his portfolio. But Rule measures his wealth in gold, not dollars. The results are striking.

"When I, as I do, informally look at my income expressed in gold, my assets expressed in gold, and my costs expressed in gold, I'm surprised at how cheap things are. Real estate measured in gold is cheap. Food measured in gold is pathetically cheap. Health insurance and healthcare measured in gold are cheap. When, by contrast, I measure them in US dollars, all of those things are frightfully expensive. That tells you gold's true role."

Recent gold selling by Turkey illustrates gold's unique liquidity characteristics. "The Turks needed to sell their gold to defend their currency, to keep politically expedient domestic pricing low. And they were able to do that. Had the Turks gone to the global bond market and tried to borrow any currency in the world, but particularly Turkish lira, they would have been singularly unsuccessful. Given the fact that they had 60 tons of gold to sell and sold it, they weathered the storm. And there's a real lesson there. Gold has spectacular liquidity—24/7 in any markets, in any quantity. It's really unique in that regard."

Rule compares gold favorably to real estate, which is notoriously illiquid and subject to wide valuation swings. "Have your home appraised by three different appraisers, and you will find at the same minute of the same day that the price of your home, according to a non-market transaction—which is to say an expert values—varies by as much as 10% per second depending on the valuer."

His conclusion is unequivocal: "I think including gold in one's savings portfolio is simply a matter of sanity. I don't think that there's an excuse for not holding it. Whether or not gold is an investment class or a speculative class depends more on the investor. But as a savings class—a savings or insurance class—I think not holding gold is irresponsible."

## Silver: Still a Speculator's Game

Rule sold 80% of his physical silver in January, when silver had moved from hated to loved following its parabolic price rise. He put 25% of those proceeds into gold, his preferred savings vehicle.

"I believe the next 10 years will unfortunately be good for the gold price," he says with characteristic candor. "I say unfortunately because circumstances that are good for the gold price are usually bad for credit markets, and credit markets are where people live."

Silver remains a speculative asset in his view, and there are other speculative opportunities he prefers. He won't be a buyer unless silver returns to the "rank hatred" he saw four and a half years ago, when the aborted silver squeeze left the metal below $20 per ounce.

"I love circumstances where a commodity for which there's ongoing demand experiences rank hatred. There's nothing in the world I like as a speculator more than hate."

That said, he notes that even during the spectacular 1970-1980 bull market, when silver ran from $1.50 to $50, the prices of both gold and silver fell by half in early 1975 before resuming their climb. So he wouldn't rule out a temporary opportunity if interest rate pressures or other factors derailed precious metals prices.

## Where's the Hatred?

Rule's investing philosophy is built on finding assets that are hated and buying them at depressed prices. Today, finding genuine hatred in commodity markets is difficult.

"Sadly, no. We have run out of hate. The only hate that is expressed in the market right now is geographic. There are places in the world that are still hated."

Russia tops his wish list but is off-limits due to regulatory constraints. Iran would be another opportunity, but also inaccessible to Western investors.

"The truth is that western investors are overly concerned—I think—about the political risks in a lot of places that they can't pronounce or spell, mostly because of ignorance. I'm not suggesting that those places aren't risky. I'm just suggesting to you that they are not as risky relative to the US and Canada as they're priced—which is to say, I think the US and Canada are more risky than people give them credit for being."

For now, Rule continues looking for conventional oil explorers—the segment most investors have abandoned in favor of shale, offshore, and frontier plays. "Unfortunately for me, the shares of those companies, which were really in the dumpster in November and December, are up 30 or 35% as a consequence of a higher oil price. And so right now my bids for them are unrequited love."

## The Junior Miners: A Stock Picker's Paradise

When it comes to precious metals miners, Rule owns "a lot" of gold equities. His discipline involves moving down the quality curve as prices rise: from majors to intermediate producers to single-asset developers.

"My instinct tells me that over 10 years, the beta in the gold market—I would define beta as the difference between the performance of gold equities relative to other equities—will be significant and substantial. Part of me says at my age, when I ought to be retired, that I should confine my activities to the biggest and best companies, the Franco-Nevadas, the Wheaton Precious Metals, the Agnico Eagles, because I think they're going to do spectacularly well with relatively little company risk."

But these premium companies trade at premium valuations. For patient, disciplined investors, single-asset producers offer a different opportunity. "The discount goes away if they get taken over. I mean immediately. The whole circumstance that we're seeing in the gold equities chain is that the larger producers have more liquidity, more trading volume, and higher share prices, and the rest don't. If value arbitrageurs don't come tighten the spreads between the big companies and the small companies, the market will—the big companies will take over the small companies."

The recent G Mining takeover of G2 at a 72% premium, while still accretive to G Mining's shareholders, "will tell you how much room there is in the next three years in the gold mining space."

On juniors, Rule is characteristically blunt. "If you merge every junior gold explorer in the world into one company, Junior Explore Co., that company would lose about $2 billion a year. So what should you pay for that company? 10 times losses, 12 times losses in a bull market, maybe 15 times losses. The point is that the junior mining industry is always overcapitalized because as an industry as a whole, it's worth nothing. It's worth less than nothing. It destroys capital."

This belies the fact that the best 10% of juniors generate enormous value. "So it's a stock picker's game. The overall junior market is hopelessly overcapitalized. It's probably 1,500 companies worldwide and there's probably 200 that are viable."

For those willing to do the work—to learn about underlying asset values, hold through 50% price declines, and commit to five or six year time frames—the junior sector "can be a truly, truly, truly wonderful place."

On developers specifically, Rule distinguishes between those with genuine expertise and "Bay Street cowboys" who are more financial engineers than mine builders. G Mining, for example, has spent 40 years building gold mines in orogenic settings in tropical, high-rainfall remote locations. "The consequence of that is that the stock is up 10-fold in three and a half years and is still, from my point of view, undervalued."

## The Bottom Line

After more than four decades in natural resource investing, Rule's framework remains remarkably consistent: identify structurally underinvested markets, find hated assets trading below intrinsic value, and hold with patience through volatility.

The current geopolitical crisis has created both chaos and opportunity. For oil, the immediate supply disruption is real but may prove temporary; the structural underinvestment problem is permanent and deepening. For gold and silver, rising prices have attracted capital but haven't eliminated the fundamental case for ownership. For juniors, the sector-wide rally has lifted boats, but value opportunities remain for disciplined stock pickers willing to do the work.

As Rule himself might say: the easy money in commodities was made when nobody liked them. The hard work—and the real profits—come from thinking in two to five year time frames while everyone else watches the news cycle.
