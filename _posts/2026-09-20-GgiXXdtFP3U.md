### Why the Fed's 25bps Hike Was a Credibility Play, Not a Policy Shift

On September 16, the Federal Reserve raised the Fed funds rate by 25 basis points in a move that was widely telegraphed yet still triggered a sharp sell-off in risk assets. Stocks, gold, and Bitcoin all dipped in the immediate aftermath of Chair Kevin Warsh's press conference, while the 10-year US Treasury yield climbed back above the 5% mark. Wall Street veteran Dave Weisberger, co-founder of Coin Routes and author of *Million-Dollar Frat Boys*, joins the show to dissect the macroeconomic forces driving this volatility.

## The Bond Market is the "Most Important Asset"

The central thesis of the discussion is that the bond market is currently dictating monetary policy. Weisberger notes that Chair Warsh effectively admitted that the US 10-year bond is the "most important asset on earth." When the yield curve steepened and the 10-year yield breached 5%, it signaled to the Fed that they could no longer remain on hold.

The decision to hike was unanimous, likely to reestablish the Fed's credibility in its fight to push inflation back to the 2% target. However, Weisberger argues that 25 basis points is essentially a "narrative" move rather than a policy shift with real economic impact. The math is stark: a quarter-percent rate increase on a $40 trillion federal debt adds $100 billion to the federal deficit in financing costs alone. This creates a paradox where the Fed's attempt to cool inflation may actually worsen the deficit, which is the primary driver of inflationary pressure.

## Inflation is "Baked in the Cake"

Weisberger, an Austrian school economist, argues that inflation is a monetary phenomenon driven by the expansion of the money supply through massive federal deficits. "As long as we are running massive deficits and the debt continues to expand, inflation is baked in the cake," he states.

While the Fed can manage wage-push inflation, it has no control over supply-side shocks like oil and diesel prices. Diesel refiners are running at 98% utilization, and geopolitical conflicts in the Middle East and Ukraine have disrupted global refining capabilities. Weisberger warns that any attempt to restrict diesel exports would be counterproductive, as diesel is significantly less elastic than gasoline—shipping cannot simply be canceled if the price rises.

## The AI Buildout and Capital Demand

A fascinating insight from Warsh's press conference was his identification of "capital competition from hyperscalers" as a driver of high yields. The massive capital expenditure required for AI data center buildouts is competing for the same capital that might otherwise buy US Treasuries.

Weisberger agrees that this is a major macro factor. He notes that compute is the "oil of the next 20-25 years." This demand for capital, combined with a geopolitical lack of faith in the US due to ongoing wars, keeps the long end of the curve elevated. Even if the wars were to end, the sheer demand for capital for AI infrastructure would likely keep yields high.

## The Clarity Act Failure and Crypto Sentiment

The Clarity Act, a major piece of crypto legislation, failed to pass in Congress, disappointing many in the sector. Weisberger explains that the failure was largely political: Democrats were unwilling to pass a bill that would benefit Trump’s family crypto ventures without significant ethics provisions.

However, Weisberger argues that the Clarity Act was never about Bitcoin. Bitcoin is a commodity, and its value is driven by its unique properties, such as 24/7 liquidity and the ability to hedge risk over weekends—something traditional stocks cannot do. The failure of the Act is a "speed bump" for DeFi developers, who may move offshore, but it does not change the long-term trend toward digital assets.

## The Million-Dollar Frat Boys: Wall Street's Tech Evolution

Weisberger’s upcoming book, *Million-Dollar Frat Boys: How Wall Street's Tech Geeks Took Over and Changed the Game*, covers the evolution of Wall Street from 1985 to 2008. The book argues that technology has improved markets for all participants, debunking the myth that "good old days" were less manipulated.

Weisberger shares anecdotes from his early career, where technologists were treated as "back office" staff. The turning point came with the rise of fintech and firms like Citadel and Two Sigma, which valued technologists as the core of their business model. The book also touches on the 1987 crash, where program trading amplified the sell-off, and the eventual shift from human-driven to algorithmic-driven markets.

## Key Takeaways

*   **The Fed is reacting to the bond market:** The 25bps hike was a credibility play to address the 5% 10-year yield, not a response to immediate inflation data.
*   **Deficits are the inflation driver:** As long as the US runs massive deficits, inflation will persist regardless of Fed policy.
*   **AI is a macro factor:** The capital demand for AI data centers is a key driver of high yields and a major investable theme.
*   **Crypto is not about the Clarity Act:** Bitcoin's value is driven by its utility as a 24/7 liquid asset, not by legislative clarity.
*   **Technology improved markets:** The shift to algorithmic trading has made markets more efficient and less prone to human manipulation.

## Conclusion

The Fed's rate hike is a minor event in the grand scheme of macroeconomic forces. The real drivers are the expanding federal deficit, the AI capital boom, and geopolitical uncertainty. For investors, the focus should be on the long-term trends: the digitalization of assets, the productivity boom from AI, and the evolving role of technology in financial markets. Weisberger’s *Million-Dollar Frat Boys* offers a fascinating look at how Wall Street transformed over the last four decades, and it is a must-read for anyone interested in the intersection of technology and finance.
