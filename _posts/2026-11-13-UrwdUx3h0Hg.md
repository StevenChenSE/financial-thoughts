The current economic landscape presents a complex puzzle for investors. With the 10-year US Treasury yield hovering near 5% and inflation data proving stickier than anticipated, macro strategists are divided on how to navigate the coming months. In a recent panel discussion, David Rosenberg of Rosenberg Research and Jim Bianco of Bianco Research offered contrasting yet complementary views on the sustainability of the AI boom, the role of the Federal Reserve, and the optimal asset allocation for 2025.

## The Bond Market: Value Amidst Volatility

A central theme of the discussion was the state of the bond market. Jim Bianco revealed that he had turned bullish on bonds in late September when yields broke above 5.25%. He argued that at these levels, the risk of further capital loss from rising yields was a low-probability event unless oil prices spiked to $200 a barrel. With oil trending downward, Bianco maintained that bonds represented significant value, offering a secure 5% coupon.

David Rosenberg agreed with the fundamental assessment but approached duration more cautiously. While initially favoring short-duration assets like two-year notes and emerging market local currency bonds, Rosenberg has recently dipped his toes into the long end of the curve. He emphasized that the risk-reward profile is vastly different from 2020, when yields were near zero. Today, with real yields in the 10-year note at approximately 2.8% and over 3% in the long bond, investors have a substantial cushion against potential losses.

## The Fed’s Dilemma: Hikes vs. Lagging Indicators

The debate extended to the Federal Reserve’s next moves. Following a hotter-than-expected CPI report showing a 0.4% monthly rise, Fed Governor Christopher Waller suggested a half-point hike in December might be warranted. Both strategists agreed that while a 50-basis-point hike would be debated, a standard 25-basis-point increase was the likely outcome.

However, they diverged on the interpretation of inflation. Bianco argued that the public’s recent election results signaled a demand for action against unanchored inflation and high cost of living. He believed the Fed must raise rates in December and subsequent meetings to address this political and economic pressure.

Rosenberg offered a counter-narrative, suggesting the market is "hyperventilating." He pointed out that core inflation has fallen from a peak of over 6% to below 3%. Rosenberg attributed the persistent inflation to a series of global supply shocks—including the pandemic, the Russia-Ukraine war, tariffs, and conflicts in the Middle East—rather than systemic domestic issues. He criticized the Fed for chasing lagging indicators like CPI and unemployment while ignoring leading commodity markets where diesel and grain prices have fallen significantly from their peaks. Rosenberg warned that the Fed’s tightening cycle has already had a cumulative effect equivalent to multiple rate hikes, potentially pushing aggregate demand toward zero next year.

## The AI Boom: Bubble or Early Stage?

The most contentious debate centered on the artificial intelligence sector. Jim Bianco acknowledged that "every technology ends in a bubble" but argued that we are currently closer to 1997 than 2000. He believes there is still room for growth before the market reaches an overbuilt stage, citing constraints on compute capacity and the historical pattern of financial innovation accompanying new technologies. Bianco compared the current moment to the invention of the secondary bond market in Venice or the public equity market by the Dutch East India Company, suggesting Wall Street will need to mutate further to finance AI infrastructure through products like SPVs or compute futures.

David Rosenberg presented a starker warning. He argued that the current AI cycle is already four years old (dating back to ChatGPT’s launch in late 2022) and exhibits far more acute stresses than the internet boom of the late 1990s. Key indicators he cited include:
*   **Credit Stress:** Credit default swaps for major tech companies like Microsoft have tripled, a phenomenon not seen in 1997.
*   **Capex Overrun:** Projected capex of $10 trillion from 2026 to 2032 is unprecedented as a share of GDP.
*   **Revenue Gap:** Research suggests AI may need to generate $8.5 trillion in accumulated revenues to justify current capital commitments, yet realistic estimates suggest only $4 trillion is achievable.

Rosenberg drew parallels to the dot-com bust, noting that 30% of telecom capex was written down by 2002. He fears a similar correction could see 40% of AI capex written off, potentially leading to bankruptcies among smaller players while giants like Amazon, Apple, and Google survive.

## Economic Fragility and the K-Shaped Reality

Both strategists highlighted the fragility of the current economic expansion. The US GDP growth rate of roughly 2.1% is heavily supported by three factors: AI data center construction, the equity wealth effect, and fiscal stimulus from recent legislation. Rosenberg noted that if you remove these supports, the underlying economy is much weaker. He pointed to a "K-shaped" business sector where AI spending surged by 90%, while other capital expenditures remained flat.

Bianco added that demand for AI is currently throttled by corporate IT departments concerned about security and agentic capabilities, similar to early internet fears about putting credit cards online. However, he believes these barriers will resolve, driving further demand. Conversely, Rosenberg warned that without the "one big beautiful bill" fiscal stimulus and with potential tax increases under a future Democratic administration, aggregate demand could collapse, leading to disinflation or even deflationary pressures.

## Asset Allocation: The 4-5-6 Strategy

In concluding the discussion, the strategists offered specific asset allocation advice for the coming year.

**Jim Bianco’s View:**
Bianco advocates for a conservative approach he terms "4-5-6":
*   **Cash:** ~10% (yielding around 4%)
*   **Bonds:** 40-50% (Core holding at 5% yield)
*   **Stocks:** 30-40% (Expecting 6-7% returns, acknowledging higher risk)

He cautions against expecting 17-20% annual returns, a mindset fostered by recent bull markets. He views bonds as the anchor, with equities offering higher expected returns but significant volatility potential.

**David Rosenberg’s View:**
Rosenberg’s portfolio mix is:
*   **40% Bonds**
*   **40% Stocks**
*   **10% Hard Assets** (Gold/Silver)
*   **10% Cash**

He argues that bonds are cheap relative to equities on a yield-for-yield basis. With the Shiller PE ratio running at 40, implying a 2.5% real earnings yield compared to higher real bond yields, he sees superior value in fixed income. He remains skeptical of equities' ability to outperform significantly given the high valuations and economic headwinds.

## Summary

The debate between Rosenberg and Bianco highlights the uncertainty facing markets today. While both agree on the current attractiveness of bonds at 5% yields, they differ sharply on the trajectory of the AI sector and inflation. Bianco sees a technology cycle still in its growth phase requiring financial innovation, while Rosenberg warns of an imminent bubble correction driven by excessive capex and credit stress. For investors, the consensus leans toward a balanced portfolio with significant exposure to bonds and cash, tempered by selective equity positions that acknowledge the high risks inherent in the current market environment.
