The post-World War II era saw the Allies establish an institutionalized system that defined global economic and political relations for decades. According to financial analyst Brett Scott, that system worked—until it no longer favored the hegemon: the United States. And when it stopped working, Scott argues, the U.S. began to dismantle it.

“They are now tearing it down,” Scott states. “And they're not going to tear it down in a way that benefits somebody else and doesn't benefit them.”

This dismantling is not a passive decline but an active, strategic process. Scott points to a surprising potential tool in this transition: **U.S. dollar stablecoins**. Rather than signaling the end of dollar dominance, these digital assets could extend its reach.

“Before everybody says the hegemon has to fall, I would encourage everybody to take a very close look at US dollar stablecoins because this has the potential to re-dollarize the world rather than de-dollarize the world,” he explains.

Looking 15 to 20 years ahead, Scott envisions a fragmented global economy. “We're going to have a fragmented world and there's going to be this block that trades with this currency and there's going to be that block that trades with a different currency.” In this multipolar landscape, he doesn’t believe the U.S. dollar will necessarily remain the single global reserve currency. However, the transition will be turbulent.

“Any transition from this system to that system is going to be incredibly chaotic. And it's not just going to hurt the United States if that happens. It will hurt everybody.”

The pace of this change, Scott suggests, has accelerated dramatically under specific political leadership. He makes a striking comparison: **“Trump has done more in 16 months to change the monetary system than the BRICS have done in 16 years. And it's not even close.”**

This aggressive shift, he concludes, is a key driver behind the current surge in gold prices. “And that is part of the reason why gold is where it’s at—because he is fundamentally tearing down what's called the rules-based system.”

The implication is clear: the foundations of the global monetary order are being actively disrupted, not by emerging challengers alone, but by the very power that built it. The coming fragmentation promises not a smooth handover, but a period of significant instability with worldwide repercussions.
