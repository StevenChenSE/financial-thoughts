## Ex-CIA Analyst Larry Johnson Reveals What's Really Driving the Rush to Geneva

The Trump administration's desperate push to sign an Iran Memorandum of Understanding on Friday masks a troubling reality: the United States is running on borrowed time. Former CIA analyst Larry Johnson, appearing on Capital Cause, painted a stark picture of an administration facing mounting economic and military pressures that make a deal with Iran almost inevitable—whether it holds or not.

"This MOU is not as enforceable as a treaty," Johnson cautioned. "But we'll take what we can get." That resignation underscores just how constrained the administration's options have become.

## The Deal Nobody Can Agree On

Even as officials prepare for a Friday signing in Geneva, the fundamental question remains: what exactly is being signed? According to Johnson, Iran has leaked its own 14-point version to the press, while Bloomberg obtained what it claims is the American version. The two don't match.

Johnson outlined two possible explanations. First, negotiations continue with the intent to finalize terms by Friday. Alternatively, "Trump has basically met Iran's demands and is not putting out the official version for fear that the political blowback might prevent him from getting a signature."

Adding to the theater, CNBC reported that Trump himself admitted he likes "the idea of blaming Vance if Iran deal doesn't work out." Johnson sees this as calculated positioning: "When it falls apart—and I think it will fall apart because of Israel—Vance gets the blame."

The pro-Zionist elements are already on television calling it "the hillbilly MOU," Johnson noted, attempting to use the agreement "to dirty up Vance."

## The Economic Time Bomb

Johnson identified the real driver behind Trump's urgency: "The worsening economic conditions. The unpopularity of this war coupled with what I'll call the economic tsunami that's coming down the pike."

The numbers are alarming. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve has plummeted to approximately 349 million barrels as of June 5th—roughly a 17-18 day supply if the nation had no other oil production. The Trump administration has been tapping reserves to suppress gasoline prices, but Johnson warned this "doesn't solve the shortage problem."

Even if Friday's signing happens, relief is weeks away. Oil from the Persian Gulf takes 4-12 weeks to reach final destinations depending on location. Then it must be refined. The Strait of Hormuz cannot simply flip a switch and reopen: Department of Defense testimony before Congress in April estimated clearing the mines currently littering the Persian Gulf would take up to six months.

"Maritime insurance industry—are they going to insure ships sailing through an area they're not sure is free of mines?" Johnson asked. "I don't think so. Or the premiums are going to be sky high."

## The Collateral Damage Chain

The disruptions extend far beyond oil. Qatar's Ras Laffan LNG facility was "severely damaged," with estimates of six months to a year before repairs complete. That facility produced not only LNG but also extracted helium—accounting for 44% of the world's supply.

The fertilizer industry faces its own crisis. Johnson, who lives south of Tampa, cited Mosaic—one of the nation's largest fertilizer producers, heavily involved in phosphate mining—as cutting operations in half because it couldn't obtain the sulfur needed for processing.

Farmers now face soaring diesel costs—they're paying more while their tractors run primarily on diesel. Meanwhile, the United States is not a net exporter of diesel; it must import from Canada, Mexico, and increasingly Venezuela.

"Trump's been going out of his way to piss them off," Johnson said of Canada. "And they're looking at China saying, 'We'd rather deal with China now.'"

The compounding effect is inevitable: higher production costs plus expensive fertilizer means food prices will rise. "Some farmers are saying, 'Hey, I can't afford to stay in business.' They're going out of business."

Johnson's verdict: "We have not yet begun to experience the full dimension of it, and I think it is very serious."

## The Israel Problem

Here's the MOU's fatal flaw: both American and Iranian versions agree on an immediate and permanent ceasefire in Lebanon, including "no further hostile actions." Iran extracted this as a concession. The question is whether Trump will actually enforce it.

"The problem is: is Donald Trump willing to hold Israel's feet to the fire?" Johnson asked.

Israel presents a united front against any accommodation with Iran. It's not just Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. Opposition leader Naftali Bennett—whom Johnson compares to "Barack Obama to Trump's Donald Trump"—is already "accusing Netanyahu of being too soft on Iran" and vowing to be "much tougher."

"The majority of Israeli public opinion is still intent on fighting Iran," Johnson observed. "And they're not going to pull out of southern Lebanon if they can avoid it."

Iran has made its position clear. For the first time in 47 years, Tehran has publicly linked its own security to what happens in Lebanon, warning that any further bombings in Beirut's Dahiya suburb would trigger retaliation. When Netanyahu violated this warning, "boom, Iran hit him back."

Most recently, Israel allegedly sabotaged the ceasefire process deliberately. "Iran was gearing up. They were all ready to start striking back, and the Trump administration was pleading with them not to do it," Johnson recounted. The administration made "additional concessions, both financial, as well as the lifting the blockade," and Iran agreed to stand down—though China was also pressuring Tehran to give the US a chance to extricate itself.

## Military Losses Mount

Johnson offered what he called "legitimate" rather than theatrical conflict between the US and Israel. The evidence: American military setbacks over the past three weeks of fighting.

The US attempted freedom of navigation operations in the Strait of Hormuz, probing Iranian positions and attacking communications systems. "Every time we did that, within an hour or two, Iran would immediately respond," Johnson said.

Iranian retaliation struck multiple American assets: two US bases in Kuwait, a CIA base in Erbil (northern Iraq) attempting to arm Kurdish forces against Tehran, and Bahrain—the former Fifth Fleet headquarters—where "billion-dollar-plus price tags" of sensitive equipment were destroyed.

Attacks also hit Prince Sultan Airbase and the Muwaffaq al-Salti airbase in Jordan, destroying and damaging up to 12 F-35 aircraft. "That's a very expensive aircraft, and we don't have a lot of replacements for it," Johnson noted.

## The Bottom Line

Looking at the political calculus, Johnson sees a president trapped: "Logan said, 'If you don't get this stopped and turned around soon, you're dead come the midterm elections in November.' If the Democrats take over the Senate and the House, you're going to face impeachment, and there are enough Republican senators who will side with the Democrats that you could actually be convicted."

Trump's recent criticism of Israel at the G7—"they were unnecessarily killing people, trying to destroy an entire apartment building to get one person"—represents "an interesting change of tone." But whether Trump can sustain pressure on Israel remains uncertain.

Johnson's final assessment is sobering: "I think we've already driven off the cliff. We just haven't landed yet."

The Friday signing may happen. But with Israeli defiance built into the agreement's architecture, economic recovery measured in months rather than weeks, and the fertilizer-to-food supply chain already fracturing, the MOU looks less like salvation and more like a band-aid on a sucking chest wound.
