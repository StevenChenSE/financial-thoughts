*March 13, 2026* – In a world fixated on geopolitical conflict, conventional wisdom suggests that war—especially in the oil-rich Middle East—should send gold and silver prices soaring. But according to analyst Eric Jung, the reality is far more complex, and metals may not get the bullish surge many investors expect.

In a recent interview on *Capital*, Jung laid out a multi-phase framework explaining why the ongoing war between the U.S., Israel, and Iran could initially suppress precious metals, with surprising ripple effects across industrial commodities.

## Phase One: The Dollar Dash for Oil

The conflict has not completely halted oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz, contrary to many expert assumptions.

> “Iran is actually shipping a lot of oil to China and apparently they are now shipping stuff to Russia and also to India,” Jung noted. “If that’s the case… I just don’t think the oil coming out from the strait is going to be zero. It might be 25%, 30%, 50% of whatever it was pre-war.”

With some oil still moving, Jung argues that major oil-importing nations are now in a scramble for U.S. dollars to buy whatever marginal barrels are available.

> “The bigger participants… might try to sell a lot of their assets for US dollars. Get US dollars and buy whatever marginal oil that is still out there… that is denominated in US dollars.”

This rush to liquidity means selling dollar-denominated assets—including **gold, silver, and bonds**—which explains why the U.S. Dollar Index (DXY) recently hit 100 and Treasury yields spiked, even amid war.

## Phase Two: The Petro-Dollar Trust Crisis & a Gold Pivot

If the war grinds into a stalemate, Jung sees a critical shift. Gulf states, whose security was guaranteed by the U.S. in exchange for using the petrodollar, may lose faith.

> “The petro dollar deal is that… when the poo poo hits the fan, you got to protect us. Uncle Sam has to protect us… Apparently that's maybe the US tried but like it's not really successful.”

In this phase, oil proceeds still earned in dollars could be diversified into **physical gold**, driving its price up. Simultaneously, a hidden crisis emerges in the industrial metals complex.

## The Sulfuric Acid Squeeze: A Chain Reaction to Silver

Here’s where Jung’s analysis introduces a crucial, overlooked variable: **sulfuric acid**. Most of the world’s sulfuric acid is a byproduct of refining sour crude oil from the Middle East. Reduced oil flow means less sulfuric acid, which is essential for extracting base metals like copper and zinc from ore.

> “If the situation gets worse… you have a severe supply crunch of the sulfuric acid and then your base metals refinement is in serious trouble.”

Silver, primarily mined as a byproduct of copper and zinc, could see its supply constrained. This could benefit primary silver producers in the medium term. However, Jung warns of a dangerous third-order effect.

## Phase Three: When Industrial Demand Collapses

If the conflict worsens and oil flows drop below a critical threshold (e.g., 10-20%), the sulfuric acid shortage could become severe. Global above-ground copper stockpiles might buffer the impact for a few months, but once depleted, industrial production could crash.

> “Once you run out of that… then it might crash the price of silver because if you can't make solar panels or EVs because you don't have the copper, then you don't need the silver even if the silver is available.”

This scenario reveals a paradox: an initial supply-driven silver shortage could be followed by a demand collapse if key industrial inputs vanish.

## Phase Four: A New Equilibrium

Eventually, Jung believes supply chains will reconfigure—bypassing the Strait via rail, or other producers like Russia increasing output. In this new equilibrium, **gold and silver would likely rise sustainably** as global trust in U.S. Treasuries erodes and dollars are quickly converted into tangible assets.

> “US dollars may simply become a trade token like a hot potato. Once they get the US dollar, they convert it into physical gold, physical silver, and other precious commodities.”

## The Investor Takeaway: Hold, Don’t Trade

Given the unpredictable, news-driven market, Jung’s personal strategy is straightforward: hold physical metals and avoid trying to time the chaos.

> “I'm holding on to all my gold and silver… I think it's extremely difficult to trade this stuff. Every day the news coming out is completely different… It's pure gambling at this point.”

For those under-allocated, he suggests dollar-cost averaging, as calling a bottom is impossible. His base case remains the multi-phase grind, not a quick resolution.

> “If the US walks away, we might get some sideways action… but I don't think that's likely based on my observation so far.”

**Bottom Line:** War doesn’t automatically mean higher gold and silver. In Jung’s view, investors must look beyond first-order effects and consider the intricate web of oil, currency flows, and industrial chemistry—where the path to higher metals prices is neither straight nor guaranteed.
