## Introduction

In a recent interview on the Capital Gains podcast, renowned macro analyst Alasdair Macleod provided a detailed analysis of the current state of precious metals, the global oil market, and the looming risks within the fiat currency system. Macleod, author of *The Crisis of Modern Economics*, argued that the world is currently witnessing a fundamental shift in how investors perceive risk. Rather than viewing gold as a traditional commodity, the market is beginning to recognize it as the primary monetary marker and a safe haven against the accelerating debasement of fiat currencies.

## The Gold Breakout and the Oil Paradox

The conversation began with the recent breakout in gold prices. Macleod confirmed that this movement is not a "fake-out" or a trap, but a fundamental realignment. He noted that while gold and oil had been inversely correlated for several months, they have recently begun to move in tandem. This shift is significant because it signals that the market is recognizing the risk in fiat currencies (specifically the US dollar) rather than in physical commodities.

Macleod explained that the US has effectively depleted its strategic petroleum reserve, which had previously been used to suppress oil prices and prevent a crisis in energy-dependent nations like Japan. With these reserves running out and key shipping lanes like the Strait of Hormuz remaining unstable, Macleod predicts that oil prices will rise substantially, potentially breaking through the $100 mark. He emphasized that the current low inflation figures are misleading, as they are a byproduct of artificially suppressed energy costs.

## Japan: A Microcosm of Global Debt Trouble

A major portion of the interview focused on Japan, which Macleod described as being in a "debt trap." He argued that the recent volatility in the Japanese yen and bond markets is not merely a technical adjustment but a sign of a structural funding crisis. Japan imports 90% of its oil and LNG from the Persian Gulf. As global oil prices rise, the cost to service Japan's massive government debt (currently around 240% of GDP) becomes untenable.

Macleod dismissed the common narrative that the US Treasury or the Bank of Japan were engaging in "yield curve control" to stabilize the market. Instead, he suggested that the US intervention was a desperate attempt to support the yen to prevent a total collapse that would ripple through the G7. He pointed out that the "carry trade"—borrowing in low-interest yen to buy high-yield assets—has inflated global assets to the tune of $20 trillion. The unwinding of this trade, he warned, could trigger a crash that dwarfs the 1929 stock market collapse.

## The Myth of Equities as Inflation Hedges

Macleod challenged the popular belief that equities are a good hedge against inflation. He argued that stocks are a form of credit, not a store of value. He highlighted that the US stock market is currently in a bubble, with approximately $9 trillion in leverage. Because stocks represent a claim on future income, they are subject to the same risks as bonds. Furthermore, Macleod noted that in a major financial collapse, the legal ownership of equities held in centralized registers could be compromised, leaving investors with no final settlement.

He specifically addressed mining stocks, noting that while they benefit from rising metal prices, they are exposed to rising input costs, particularly diesel. As diesel prices rise due to supply constraints, the margins for mining companies could be squeezed, making the physical metal a safer investment than the equity of the company that mines it.

## The Fate of Fiat Currencies

The core of Macleod's thesis is that the current economic system is unsustainable. He drew parallels to the German hyperinflation of the 1920s, where the government prioritized the stability of the stock market over the value of the currency. In that era, the stock market crash was only 25% in terms of Reichsmarks but over 90% when measured in gold. Macleod predicts a similar dynamic today: central banks will prioritize the solvency of the financial system and the stability of the stock market by ramping up Quantitative Easing (QE) and suppressing interest rates, regardless of the impact on inflation.

He warned that this policy will lead to the "crack-up boom" described by Austrian economists, where the value of savings is wiped out not by a direct seizure, but by the rapid loss of purchasing power of the currency.

## Conclusion

Alasdair Macleod’s analysis paints a stark picture of the current financial landscape. He advises investors to watch bond yields, particularly in Japan, and to monitor the price of oil as a leading indicator of global economic stress. His recommendation is clear: move out of credit (bonds, equities, and bank deposits) and into physical money (gold and silver bullion or coin) to protect against the inevitable debasement of fiat currencies. For Macleod, the risk is no longer in the commodity; the risk is in the currency itself.
