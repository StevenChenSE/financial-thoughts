## The New Inflation Reality Is Already Here

The era of cheap money may be over—but so is the promise of deflation. According to Clem Chambers, veteran market analyst and founder of ADVFN, Western economies face a structural shift toward sustained inflation of 7%, 8%, even 9% that could persist for the next five to ten years. This isn't a temporary spike like COVID-era price surges; it's a permanent recalibration driven by two forces: a massive industrial buildout and the trillion-dollar AI revolution.

"During COVID and early on in your show before inflation showed up, I said inflation's coming and then it arrived," Chambers recalls. "And they got it down now. It's coming again now. It's going to be at least as bad as the COVID inflation, and it's going to go for a lot longer because it's not going to be inflation created to pave over something that lasted six months."

The core problem, according to Chambers, is that governments face an impossible choice: either print money to fund massive infrastructure and AI development, or watch their economies stagnate. History suggests which path they'll choose.

## Why the Bond Market Faces a Crisis—And How Governments Will Respond

### Jamie Dimon's Warning and What It Means

JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon recently warned that rising government debt levels could trigger a bond market crisis, urging policymakers to act before markets force their hand. Chambers views this warning as "very accurate" but notes the inevitability of the outcome.

"Of course I can deal with it. Of course it has consequences. And they will deal with it, and it will have consequences."

The real danger lies not in the debt itself but in how governments handle it. When bond yields rise sharply, investors rush to sell, central banks must step in as lenders of last resort, and the resulting volatility can cascade through pension funds and financial institutions.

### Lessons from the 2022 UK Gilt Crisis

The 2022 crisis in UK government bonds—known as the "gilt crisis"—offers a cautionary tale. Financial institutions had heavily leveraged their positions in government securities, borrowing cheaply to buy more bonds in a self-reinforcing cycle that worked beautifully while interest rates fell.

"All the pension funds, a lot of them, had levered up government securities to the moon on borrowed money," Chambers explains. "When interest rates are going down, you appear to be a genius, but the bending in kills you because you've levered up. And then when the thing goes up a little bit after that trend finishes, you're levered to the ears, and you go bust."

The trigger was a new UK government announcement that spooked markets. Interest rates rose sharply, and institutions that had bet on continued low rates suddenly faced margin calls they couldn't meet. The Bank of England was forced to intervene to prevent systemic collapse.

### The Monetization Loophole: How Governments Really Handle Debt

Here's what most investors miss: when governments face a bond crisis, they have a simple solution that's technically illegal but universally practiced—monetization.

"The government can always buy its bonds back and has been doing so, even though technically it's illegal," Chambers reveals. "There's ways around that illegality. They can publish, print their own bonds, and then turn it into cash, and then pay themselves."

The process works like this: the government needs money for a deficit. It creates bonds and sells them to the central bank. The central bank creates cash out of nothing and pays for the bonds. The government spends that cash, creates more bonds, and repeats. It's monetization—effectively printing money—and it's how developing nations typically handle fiscal shortfalls.

"That's the monetization of government debt—is really the shortcut to inflation. That's how you get hyperinflation. That's what all the South Americans do. They don't bother taxing people. They just print the money and pay their people with it."

## The 60/40 Portfolio May Need a Rethink

With yields on US 10-year Treasury bonds having peaked after decades of decline, the question arises: what replaces government bonds in a traditional 60/40 portfolio if the structural bull market in bonds is truly over?

Chambers isn't convinced this is the crisis it's made out to be. "Interest rates on government bonds are completely and utterly fixable by the government. They just buy them back and push that price down. Simple as that. That's what QE is."

Japan demonstrates this principle: the Bank of Japan has pushed interest rates negative and continues to buy government bonds aggressively. The same playbook works anywhere.

"But when you do that, if you don't do it right, or you're not extremely clever, it turns immediately into inflation. Because all you're doing is flushing cash into the system."

The trade-off is clear: suppress interest rates through bond-buying, or face high rates and potential recession. Neither option preserves the value of money.

### The Balance Sheet Perspective: Americans Are Rich, Government Is Poor

Chambers offers a different framework for thinking about national debt. If the government owes money to its own citizens—which it mostly does—that debt represents both a liability for the government and an asset for the people. The two sides cancel out.

"Americans rich, government poor. Money coming off your bonds yield is like a reverse taxation. That's not so bad."

The danger emerges when debt is owed to foreigners who won't accept domestic currency in payment. "The trouble comes when you're Argentina and you owe that money to people that aren't in your country. So it's all liability and no asset."

America's position is considerably stronger: only about 30% of US national debt is held by foreign creditors. The rest is domestic debt—essentially Americans borrowing from Americans.

## Understanding Inflation: Not All Price Rises Are Created Equal

### Three Types of Inflation

Chambers distinguishes between three distinct inflation regimes:

1. **Hyperinflation/Stagflation**: The worst scenario—prices rising while the economy contracts, erasing purchasing power and savings
2. **Erosion Inflation**: Persistent price increases that gradually devalue money without corresponding economic growth
3. **Productive Inflation**: The "least worst" form—money printing that flows into actual economic activity, allowing wages and asset values to rise faster than prices

"The inflation that creates economic activity, you can put up with because your money goes up 10%, even though the actual value of it goes down, but overall you're better off."

After COVID, American wages initially outran inflation—a rare example of productive inflation working as intended. Chambers believes the coming wave will be similarly constructive, though not painless.

"The capital that you have to pump in, and then the capital of the AI conflict—it's impossible for it not to be inflationary at a higher level than we're used to."

## UAE Departure from OPEC: What It Means for Oil Markets

While markets focus on bonds and inflation, a quieter seismic shift is occurring in energy markets: the UAE has announced its departure from OPEC, representing approximately 12% of the cartel's total output.

"Undoubtedly they've been angered by Iran's bombing and missile strikes on UAE infrastructure in Dubai," Chambers notes. "Other UAE members are not cooperating in terms of what they want for output. Saudi Arabia wants one thing, and the UAE wants another."

The implications for oil prices are significant:

- **Supply disruption risk**: Iran faces potential military confrontation with the US, which would eliminate Iranian oil from markets
- **Fragmentation premium**: OPEC's breakup removes the cartel's ability to manage supply, likely pushing prices higher
- **Budget pressures**: Major producers have accumulated deficits and will need high oil prices to balance their books

"If OPEC starts breaking up, one by one, what's going to happen to the price of oil? It's going to get elevated. Iran's infrastructure is going to get broken at this rate. And therefore, the whole output of Iran is going to go through a very large hiccup."

Chambers has previously written about oil potentially reaching $300 per barrel—a prediction that seemed fanciful but may be gaining plausibility as Middle Eastern tensions escalate.

## Gold Is for War: Understanding Precious Metals' Next Move

### The Taiwan Connection

Gold and silver have been hovering in recent months, waiting for a catalyst. According to Chambers, that catalyst was always likely to be geopolitical: specifically, the potential conflict between China and Taiwan that had markets on edge through late 2024 and early 2025.

"What is gold waiting for? It's waiting to see what happens to President Xi and the PLA."

The timeline is telling: when tensions around a potential Taiwan invasion peaked, gold spiked. When reports emerged of internal conflict within the Chinese military leadership—specifically, that Xi had sacked generals and the PLA balked at the prospect of amphibious assault against Taiwan's defenses—gold retreated sharply.

"Gold went down like a lump. Now, it doesn't mean it's actually delayed for good, but it is put off."

The Taiwan stock exchange tells the same story. As the invasion threat diminished, Taiwanese equities rallied dramatically—the market's way of celebrating the removal of an existential risk.

### The "Gold Is for War" Framework

Chambers' investment thesis is elegantly simple: gold is the currency of international conflict.

**Before war**: "We might have a conflict with the US. I think we better buy gold. Keep buying it. Keep buying it. Because if we do have a war, we're going to have to start selling it to buy stuff. No one's going to take your paper. No one's going to take your IOU. They're only going to take gold."

**During war**: "When you're fighting one, you sell it. Russia's been selling gold recently. Iran probably needs the money. And nobody's going to take your paper during war, are they?"

**After war**: "During the war, there's been so much inflation. Everybody wants to get their hands on a hard asset. And bang, up goes gold."

This explains why gold didn't surge when the Middle East conflict heated up—it's performing its expected function. The move comes before the shooting starts, not after.

### Investment Strategy for Gold

For investors building positions, Chambers recommends dollar-cost averaging:

"If you don't have exposure to gold, it's a good time to dollar-cost average. Have a bit next month. Have a bit the month after that. Have a bit another month. And you'll build up a position over a couple of years, and you'll get a good average price."

The current consolidation phase—in which volatility has compressed—typically precedes the next directional move. When normal volatility returns, the trend will become clear.

## Where to Put Your Money: Nuclear Power, Cables, and Copper

### Nuclear Renaissance: The Unlikely Winners

Chambers' most surprising call: Nokia, the once-dominant mobile phone company that became a byword for corporate failure. But the new Nokia isn't about phones—it's about infrastructure.

"Nokia's the biggest joke in town, apart from the fact that Nvidia just put a billion dollars into them to build out 6G because the Chinese infrastructure people aren't trusted anymore. And they're one of the few games in town that can build the infrastructure in mobile land and 6G."

Alongside tech infrastructure, Chambers sees nuclear power as perhaps the most compelling opportunity:

- **Limited competition**: Only a handful of companies can build modern nuclear facilities
- **Enormous demand**: AI data centers alone will require massive power generation
- **Premium pricing**: These specialized firms can charge accordingly

"Fluor Corp, because they build clean nuclear stuff. And there ain't that many people out there that do that anymore. And boy, are they going to be building out nuclear like there's no tomorrow."

### The Energy Bottleneck

AI's infrastructure demands are staggering. The math is simple: every AI query requires computation, computation requires electricity, and electricity requires generation capacity, transmission infrastructure, and cooling systems.

"Energy is the choke point. Energy distribution, energy making it—all of that stuff is the big choke point. And there ain't going to be enough energy, full stop, period, until further notice."

Specific beneficiaries include:

- Companies manufacturing server center cabling
- Copper producers
- Power generation equipment makers
- Utilities with nuclear assets

"AI changes everything on many different levels. And if you're economically active, you're going to make a ton of money. If you're economically passive, it ain't going to work out well for you."

## The Tech Layoff Paradox: Why Companies Are "Destocking"

### Silicon Valley's Dirty Secret

Tech companies have been cutting headcount aggressively—Meta laying off 10% of workers, Microsoft offering voluntary retirement to 7% of staff, Nike reducing workforce by 1,500. But Chambers offers a different interpretation of what's actually happening.

"They were hiring people for non-existent jobs. And they were doing it for decades."

The revelation that changes everything: in Silicon Valley HR departments, employees are internally referred to as "stock."

"They call employees stock. Let that sink in. So they're destocking. Those people are stock. They're not employees. They're stock."

Companies were essentially hoarding talent—not because they needed the work done, but to prevent competitors from acquiring those employees. The hiring wasn't about productivity; it was about competitive positioning.

### The AI Productivity Equation

Now the calculus has changed. Building AI infrastructure costs real money—data centers, GPUs, power, cooling. Money that was previously spent on headcount.

"You've got to turn that stock into cash. And you do that by getting rid of them."

But here's the nuance: AI isn't replacing workers wholesale. It's amplifying the output of remaining workers.

"Programmers are doing coding for 20 people now. They spend a quarter of a million pounds on AI doing it. But that's like 250,000 and two and a half million pounds for the programmers. And they're doing it single-handedly because of AI."

The new reality: employees who can effectively use AI tools become dramatically more productive. Those who can't are the ones being destocked.

## The Federal Reserve's Dilemma: Ultra-Dovish While Sounding Hawkish

### What to Watch at the Next FOMC Meeting

Chambers expects the Federal Reserve to embark on aggressive rate cuts and money printing—regardless of what officials say publicly.

"All I know is the Fed is going to turn ultra-dovish while claiming to be ultra-hawkish. They're going to go on a complete printing bender."

The political dimension is impossible to ignore. When Donald Trump wanted lower interest rates, Jerome Powell faced investigations and pressure. The new Fed chair will presumably have different priorities.

"He's already said, 'Oh, we want interest rates coming down because there's going to be deflation.' So anyway, you can imagine that somehow this is a super-hawk that's not going to do what Donald Trump said."

The inflation reality won't disappear by pronouncing the word "deflation." But for the economically active—those actively participating in markets and businesses—the coming money printing represents opportunity.

## The Real Divide: Active vs. Passive

### Winners and Losers in the New Economy

Chambers draws a stark distinction between those who will thrive and those who will struggle in the coming decade.

"There's a lot of people going to be absolutely in the gravy here. And there's no replacing you and me. There's absolutely people care about people. People want to know about people. They watch television to see people."

The AI revolution, like previous technological shifts, will eliminate certain jobs while creating others. The key variable isn't AI itself—it's human adaptability.

"Farmers thought that was the end of the world when they invented tractors. It wasn't, was it? It just changed."

### A Message to Passive Investors

For those sitting on the sidelines, the message is sobering:

"Why are 350 million Americans watching shows like this? Because they're passive. And they're going to perish. Not people like who are watching your show, who care about the future, who want to know what's going to happen. They'll be absolutely fine."

The opportunity is there for those willing to engage. The costs of passivity—in an inflationary environment where cash constantly erodes in value—will be severe.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Structural inflation of 7-9% is coming and will persist for years**, driven by massive spending on AI infrastructure and industrial buildout. Governments will choose to print money rather than let their economies stagnate.

2. **The bond market will face periodic crises, but governments have tools to suppress yields** through bond-buying programs. This "monetization" creates inflation but prevents catastrophic defaults—a trade-off that favors hard assets over bonds.

3. **Gold follows geopolitical tension, not just inflation**. The metal performs before wars begin and after they end. Current consolidation reflects the postponement of the Taiwan conflict, not a structural breakdown in the bull case.

4. **Energy infrastructure is the critical bottleneck of the AI era**. Nuclear power companies, cable manufacturers, and copper producers are positioned to benefit from demand that will require 10x current electricity generation.

5. **Economic activity is the path to prosperity**. Money printing, while inflationary, flows to those actively participating in markets. Passivity—in a world of eroding cash value—is the greatest risk of all.
