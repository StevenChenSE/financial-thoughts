## David Nikoski on Market Rotation, the Yen Carry Trade, and the Best Sectors to Own

### Introduction
In a rapidly evolving market landscape, investor sentiment is shifting away from the concentrated gains of the technology sector toward a more diversified approach. On a recent episode of *The David Lin Show*, guest David Nikoski, CIO of Vermillion Research, provided a technical and fundamental breakdown of the current economic environment. Nikoski highlighted the dangers of extreme concentration in tech stocks, analyzed the implications of the US government’s unprecedented intervention in the Japanese yen market, and outlined specific sectors poised to benefit as capital rotates out of artificial intelligence (AI) and semiconductors.

### Korea’s Crash and the Warning for U.S. Stocks
The discussion opened with a stark example of concentration risk: the collapse of the Korean KOSPI index. Up until July, Korea had been the world’s top-performing market; however, it subsequently plummeted 33% in a single month—the worst drop in the country’s history, surpassing the crashes of 1998 and 2008. Nikoski attributed this volatility to a lack of market breadth, where the index was propped up by only a few stocks while the majority of the market deteriorated.

Nikoski drew parallels between this event and the current US market, where the technology sector accounts for approximately 42% of the S&P 500 weighting. He noted that while the US economy is more diversified, the "greed" driving Wall Street into tech mirrors the conditions seen in emerging markets like Korea and Taiwan. Nikoski emphasized that while he is not calling a definitive market top, the parabolic extension of tech stocks is unsustainable. He pointed out that the semiconductors industry, which was 9% of the market during the dot-com bubble, now represents 22%, excluding major tech firms developing their own chips.

### Semiconductor and AI Stocks at Risk
Focusing on technical indicators, Nikoski analyzed the VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SMH), which has declined over 20% from its June highs. He suggested that support may be found at the 200-day moving average, a level that has historically provided a floor for corrections. Nikoski warned that the current environment resembles the dot-com era in terms of extension above this moving average.

The AI trade is also showing signs of bifurcation. Nikoski observed divergences in individual stocks, such as engineering and construction firms tied to AI infrastructure. He noted that correlation across the broader market is low, creating a "stock picker's market" where single-stock skew is high. Investors are rotating out of "safe" premium valuations like Apple and Costco back into other names, signaling a shift in risk appetite.

### Why the U.S. Intervened in Japan’s Yen Crisis
A major focus of the interview was the US Treasury’s decision to buy Japanese yen over the weekend, the first intervention of this scale since the 1998 Asian financial crisis. Nikoski explained that the US acted to prevent a "Asian contagion" scenario caused by the yen’s 40-year lows.

The logic, according to Nikoski, involves the "yen carry trade." Investors have been borrowing cheap yen to buy higher-yielding US assets. If the yen had collapsed further, the Bank of Japan would have been forced to raise interest rates aggressively, potentially triggering a massive unwinding of this trade and a subsequent sell-off of US equities. By intervening to strengthen the yen and pushing Japanese yields higher, the US is attempting to force a controlled unwind of the carry trade ahead of the midterms. Nikoski expressed surprise that US equity markets had not reacted more violently to this shift, suggesting that many firms may have been positioned correctly or that unforeseen catalysts are at play.

### Dollar Weakness and the Threat to U.S. Markets
Nikoski also addressed the strength of the US dollar, noting that it had recently broken a long-term uptrend. He referenced a historical precedent from April 2002, when a similar break in the dollar’s trend preceded a 25% decline in the S&P 500. The rate of change in currency trends is often more consequential than the trend itself.

Regarding the impact on the average investor, Nikoski explained that the intervention benefits Japan, which relies heavily on imports like oil. A stronger yen allows them to purchase commodities without their economy grinding to a halt. For the US, the risk lies in the potential for rapid currency devaluation, which could lead to a repatriation of capital and significant market volatility.

### Oil, Energy Stocks, and Inflation
In the commodities space, Nikoski highlighted the volatility of oil prices, driven largely by geopolitical factors and the release of oil from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR). Despite recent drops, he maintained that prices would not fall to $40 a barrel. This stability implies that inflation expectations will remain elevated, keeping bond yields higher for longer.

Nikoski pointed out that energy stocks (XLE) have actually been outperforming tech stocks (XLK) since the COVID lows. With energy representing only about 3% of the market, even a small influx of fund flows could result in significant gains. He argued that ignoring energy due to price uncertainty is a mistake, as many companies are cash-flow positive and sitting above 20-year bases.

### Consumer Weakness and the Stock Picker’s Market
The interview also touched on the state of the consumer. While some argue that spending is robust—citing full stadiums and record-breaking box office openings for films like *Spider-Man*—Nikoski pointed to relative strength data showing weakness in major consumer discretionary groups. Retailers like Home Depot, Lowe’s, and Lululemon are trading at multi-year relative strength lows.

Nikoski suggested that consumers are trading down, favoring affordable experiences like movies and casual dining (e.g., BJ’s Restaurants) over big-ticket items like RVs or boats. This divergence reinforces his view that this is a stock picker's market where group correlation is low, and selecting the right individual names is critical.

*   **Financials and Insurance:** Banks, particularly European institutions like UBS and Deutsche Bank, have outperformed for years as interest rates have turned positive. Insurance names are also attractive, provided they have limited exposure to risky AI-related private debt.
*   **Healthcare:** Companies such as Medtronic, Abbott, and Baxter are showing relative strength as funds diversify away from tech.
*   **Energy:** Despite volatility, the long-term trend remains positive, and the sector is under-owned by the broader market.
