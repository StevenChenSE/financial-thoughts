## The Perfect Storm: Why This Crisis Is Different

The World Uncertainty Index — which tracks how frequently the word "uncertainty" appears in economic reports — has just recorded its **highest reading in over 30 years**. It spiked during COVID. It spiked during the 2008 financial crisis. It spiked after 9/11 and during the Iraq War. But according to Preen, "what we're seeing right now blows past all of this."

What makes this crisis fundamentally different from previous ones is that **it isn't caused by a single event**. Instead, it's the simultaneous convergence of multiple destabilizing forces:

- **Tariff chaos:** Active tariff policies are creating trade uncertainty where businesses literally cannot plan because rules keep changing week to week
- **Oil price explosion:** Brent crude surged from normal levels to approximately $126 per barrel due to Iran disruptions, with ripple effects cascading through the entire economy
- **Dollar weakness:** The greenback has weakened to levels generating headlines even in mainstream media
- **Gold's historic run:** The ultimate fear trade has blown past $5,000 per ounce for the first time ever
- **Stickier inflation:** The OECD projects US inflation hitting 4.2% — far above expectations
- **The Fed's impossible position:** Cannot cut rates aggressively (inflation too high) and cannot raise rates (economy slowing)
- **Diminished growth cushion:** Global growth sits well below pre-COVID levels, leaving less room for policy error

Yet despite all of this, the S&P 500 remains near record highs — a divergence that Preen insists "doesn't last by historic standards." Some things have got to give.

## The Three Catastrophic Mistakes Retail Investors Keep Making

When uncertainty spikes like this, retail investors consistently make the same three errors. Every single time. Preen has seen this "playbook" before — it's just "happening on speed."

### Mistake #1: The Cash Panic

The first instinct is to sell everything and move into cash. It *feels* safe — your brain is screaming to protect what you've worked hard for.

But here's the trap: that cash is guaranteed to lose 4% or more per year to inflation. Meanwhile, the stock market has historically been higher one year after every major geopolitical event. The people who panic-sold basically locked in losses, missed the recovery, and "basically made the most expensive way of playing it safe."

### Mistake #2: The Freeze

The second mistake is doing absolutely nothing — closing your eyes and hoping it goes away.

This is fundamentally different from having a strategy and choosing to hold. This is paralysis. The problem with freezing is that you're not managing your risk — you're ignoring it. "Your portfolio was built for an environment," Preen explains. "That environment has now changed. If you don't at least evaluate your positions, you are driving basically with your eyes closed."

### Mistake #3: Chasing the Spike

The third and "most dangerous" mistake is buying whatever just spiked 60% — gold at the top, energy stocks after an oil surge, defense stocks at their peak.

What history consistently shows is that the initial spike in any crisis is "pretty much always the worst time to buy." In the first phase of any shock, gold, energy, and defense stocks spike — but those moves are driven by fear and algorithm trading, not fundamentals. They usually pull back hard when the dust settles. "Retail investors buy at the peak," Preen states flatly. "Institutions are well-positioned, which is why it went up to start with."

## What Institutions Do Differently

The critical difference between those who lose fortunes and those who make them comes down to one key insight: **Institutions don't react to chaos. They prepare for it.**

While retail investors are panicking, freezing, or chasing spikes, institutional players are studying patterns, understanding sector rotation, and repositioning their money based on where capital is flowing *next* — not where it just went.

"Retail investors see a conflict and they do one of three things we just covered," Preen explains. "But the big boys, the ones with the deep pockets, they're repositioning based on patterns they've understood for the last 50 years."

This repositioning isn't random. There are predictable frameworks — confirmed patterns — that govern how money flows from one sector to another during times of uncertainty. Understanding these patterns is the difference between being a sitting duck and being positioned to profit from the madness.

## The Four-Phase Framework Institutions Use

The same pattern repeats regardless of whether you're dealing with a war, a trade war, an energy crisis, or a pandemic. Money moves in relatively simple ways once you truly understand it. Here's how it breaks down:

### Phase One: The Shock

The first days to weeks after a major event, when fear and algorithms dominate:

- The S&P typically drops **5 to 7%** in the first 10 days
- The fear index spikes to 20s, 50s, sometimes 80s
- Gold shoots up **8 to 12%**
- Defense stocks perform well
- This is where retail investors panic and make the catastrophic mistakes outlined above

### Phase Two: Repricing

This is where "smart money" starts asking better questions:

- *How long is this going to last?*
- *What's the structural impact?*
- *What does it mean for inflation?*
- *What will the Fed do next?*
- *How are supply chains affected?*

During this phase, institutions quietly reposition. Markets typically bottom approximately **three weeks** after a conflict breaks out and start recovering within **one to two months**.

### Phase Three: The Rotation

This is "where the informed people make money" — and it can last for quarters or even years.

Retail investors who sold are on the sidelines. Those who froze are still paralyzed. Those who chased spikes are nursing losses. Meanwhile, informed investors are rotating into companies that genuinely benefit from the new normal.

"While the market tends to recover over time," Preen notes, "there are certain sectors, certain industries that lead the recovery every single time. Where does the capital actually go? Where does the money actually flow? Those patterns are not random."

### The Critical Insight

When uncertainty hits, **money doesn't leave the market — it moves within the market**. From biotech, software, and AI into defense, energy, utilities, and oil services. Preen uses a simple analogy: "I think of it like a swimming pool. You push down on one side, the water rises on the other."

## Sector-by-Sector: Where the Money Flows During Disruptions

Understanding the typical money flow patterns during major disruptions can help you position before the move, not after it.

### Energy: The First Mover

Energy is the first mover during conflicts involving oil or shipping — both clearly affected in the current environment. However, Preen emphasizes that "the initial spike in oil companies is usually quite a short-term trade."

The longer-term winners are **energy infrastructure**: pipelines, storage terminals, and refineries. Preen reveals his own positioning: "We've been investing in these oil service companies for months actually. Before this war started, we were already in these companies. Why? Because the money had been starting to flow in there."

### Defense: The 40% Opportunity

Defense is an obvious first mover, historically climbing approximately 40% during conflicts. But institutions don't chase the big names everyone knows. Instead, they look for specialized plays:

- Companies focusing on components
- AI-driven defense systems
- Drone technology

Critically, defense spending tends to stay elevated long after a war ends — it's not a temporary bump. Preen points out that the US defense budget may see a **50% increase** — a structural shift that extends far beyond initial conflict pricing.

### Gold: Beyond the Commodity

Gold is being purchased by central banks, and dollar weakness adds further fuel to the rally. But Preen urges investors to think beyond the commodity itself:

- Gold miners
- Gold streaming companies
- Gold ETFs

"There's a whole ecosystem there behind that," he notes. "Don't think about gold the commodity. Think about the entire supply chain."

### Consumer Staples: The Reliable Boring

Food, beverages, household products — boring companies that sell things people need no matter what's happening in the world. These companies have pricing power that allows them to maintain margins even when inflation bites.

"Not exciting," Preen admits, "but again, it's incredibly effective if you want to protect yourself. It might be a heck of a lot better than sitting in cash. It might be a heck of a lot better than just freezing."

### What Gets Hit

Perhaps counterintuitively, utilities and real estate — often perceived as "safe" — actually get hammered during periods of uncertainty and high interest rates. The assumption that these defensive sectors will protect you is precisely the kind of conventional thinking that costs retail investors money.
