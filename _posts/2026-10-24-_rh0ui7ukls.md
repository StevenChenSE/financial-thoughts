## The Hidden Crisis: Liquidity Is Draining from Financial Markets

Michael Howell, founder of GL Indexes and author of *Capital Wars*, delivers a stark warning: the global financial system is facing a severe liquidity drought driven by massive AI capital expenditures and an impending debt maturity wall. While headlines focus on inflation and Fed rate decisions, Howell argues that the real story is the diversion of capital from financial markets into the real economy, setting the stage for significant market pain leading into 2027.

## The Scarcity of Capital in the AI Era

A recent admission by Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang highlights a critical paradox in the tech sector. While demand for AI infrastructure is "off the charts," there is a profound scarcity of working capital to fuel this growth. Howell confirms that Huang is "completely right." 

The logic is straightforward: all money must be somewhere. Currently, vast sums are flowing into **AI CapEx** and real-economy projects, crowding out traditional asset markets like stocks and bonds. This diversion is not merely a sector-specific issue but a macroeconomic shift where a "red hot" economy—characterized by US nominal GDP growth exceeding 6%, levels last seen in the mid-1980s—is absorbing liquidity that previously supported financial asset prices.

## The $2 Trillion Debt Wave and the Maturity Wall

Compounding this liquidity drain is an eye-watering wave of new debt issuance required to fund AI build-outs, estimated at least **$2 trillion** next year. This new supply collides with the "debt maturity wall," a surge of refinancing demand stemming from low-interest-rate loans taken out during the COVID-19 era (2020–2024) that are now coming due.

Howell describes capital markets today primarily as **debt refinancing vehicles**. The convergence of new AI-related debt and the rolling over of legacy corporate and government debt creates a massive suction for liquidity. As credit spreads widen, it becomes increasingly difficult to finance deals, signaling stress in the funding environment.

## Misreading Bond Yields: Growth vs. Fiscal Fear

Howell challenges the prevailing narrative that rising bond yields are solely due to fiscal irresponsibility or inflation fears. Instead, he argues that yields are rising because **economic growth is accelerating**. 

Data shows a tight correlation between nominal GDP growth and risk-adjusted Treasury yields. In Japan, for instance, nominal GDP growth has surged toward 5%, pushing rates higher after decades of deflation. Similarly, in the US, bond markets are pricing in strong economic momentum rather than just fiscal deterioration. However, there are exceptions: France’s term premium is rising due to genuine concerns over debt sustainability and social unrest, causing French 10-year yields to exceed US levels for the first time in over a decade.

## Central Banks Caught Between Tightening and Catastrophe

The Federal Reserve finds itself in a delicate position. It must manage money markets to ensure enough liquidity exists for debt rollovers without triggering another 2008-style crisis. 

- **Repo Market Tensions:** Recent spikes in repo rates revealed air pockets in funding when the Fed attempted balance sheet reduction (QT). In response, the Fed introduced tools like **Reserve Management Purchases**, effectively acting as a form of quantitative easing to stabilize liquidity.
- **Volatility Control:** Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent’s bond buyback strategy is designed not to influence yields directly on a $40 trillion market, but to retire illiquid, off-the-run Treasuries and keep the **MOVE index** (bond market volatility) low. Low volatility is crucial because it determines collateral haircuts; high volatility reduces leverage capacity, further strangling liquidity.

Howell asserts that while Fed funds rate decisions dominate headlines, they are "the least relevant lever" compared to these underlying money market operations. The Fed is cautiously managing liquidity to avoid a major catastrophe, even as the broader financial cycle turns downward.

## Market Implications: Equities, Bonds, and Hard Assets

### Equities
Financial markets are leading indicators. As liquidity declines, P/E multiples contract. While earnings have remained robust due to tech success, Howell suspects this earnings boost is cyclical rather than structural. If it fades next year, stock markets—particularly small-cap indices like the Russell 2000, which already appear bearish—will face significant pressure.

### Bonds
Contrary to media pessimism, bonds may now be attractive. With yields at levels last seen before the post-2020 era of "insanity" (near-zero rates), buying Treasuries around 5–6% offers reasonable income. TIPS offering 3% real yields provide a government-guaranteed return that is competitive in a high-monetary-inflation environment.

### Gold and Bitcoin
Howell advocates for holding gold and Bitcoin as hedges against inevitable central bank liquidity injections. 
- **The Causal Link:** Central banks *must* print money to refinance the massive global debt burden. This leads to monetary inflation (debt growing at ~8.5% annually since 1971).
- **Performance:** Gold has historically matched this rate of monetization (~8.6% annual growth in price). Bitcoin, being highly sensitive to liquidity, is expected to rise as central banks resume money printing. While short-term volatility remains (Bitcoin faces support testing at $70,000), the long-term trend favors these hard assets as protection against currency debasement.

## The Timeline: Pain Extends into 2027

The cyclical downturn in liquidity has further to go. Because financial markets lead the real economy, turmoil in asset prices will eventually bleed into Main Street. Howell predicts that the real economic pain—manifesting as job losses or reduced consumption due to tightened credit—may not fully materialize until the **second half of 2027**.

Investors are advised to prepare for worsening market conditions over the next few months, characterized by widening credit spreads and increased volatility in risk assets. The resolution will ultimately require central banks to step back into liquidity provision, reinforcing the case for holding hard money assets like gold and Bitcoin alongside high-yielding bonds.
