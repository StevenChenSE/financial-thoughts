**An analysis with Chris MacIntosh**

In a world increasingly defined by geopolitical fractures and supply chain breakdowns, the recent closure of the Strait of Hormuz is not merely a temporary disruption—it is the catalyst for a prolonged period of elevated energy costs and severe food insecurity. According to Chris MacIntosh, publisher of *Capitalist Exploits Insider*, the mechanisms that have kept global trade flowing are breaking down, with consequences that will ripple through every facet of the economy for years to come.

### The Oil Equation: A Long-Term Problem

At the time of the interview, oil was trading at $96. MacIntosh argues this elevated price is not a spike, but a new baseline. The common assumption is that once the Strait reopens, oil flows will normalize. He dismantles this notion.

> "The issue... is that in order for the oil to flow, we need to have all of the ships in place. We need to have the core infrastructure operational. And those two main elements... are not at all what they were prior to this war."

He explains that ships are scattered across alternative routes, and critical infrastructure—refineries, storage tanks—has been damaged. Repairing this will take "many months to years." Furthermore, rerouting the global shipping fleet alone would take an estimated two months *if* the Strait opened tomorrow—a scenario he finds unlikely.

> "Iran essentially controls that strait at the moment and it's not likely to be opened anytime soon. There's a cost asymmetry... For every dollar that Iran spends attacking the Gulf, states spend about $15 to $35 defending it."

This asymmetry makes reopening the Strait to Western-allied shipping a monumental, costly challenge. The result is a sustained physical supply constraint that paper markets cannot wish away, leading to a market condition known as backwardation, where spot prices outpace futures.

### From Fertilizer to Famine: The Coming Food Crisis

The energy crunch directly triggers a far more dire threat: global food shortages. The linchpin is fertilizer.

> "Moving into the planting season and if that fertilizer doesn't move, which it doesn't look like it's going to... we'll have a famine. We will have a global food shortage."

MacIntosh breaks down the food supply chain: producing staples like wheat or cattle requires fertilizer and diesel (made from oil). With both inputs under severe pressure, productive capacity plummets. The crisis will not be felt equally, however. He identifies regional self-sufficiency as the key differentiator.

*   **The Western Hemisphere:** North and South America are in a relatively strong position. "They got the feedstock... You've got natural gas, plenty of it. You've got oil... an ability... to produce food."
*   **High-Risk Regions:** Southeast Asia, South Asia (Bangladesh, Pakistan, India), and the Middle East are exceptionally vulnerable. They rely heavily on feedstock and fertilizer imports from the Gulf. He cites an AI analysis noting India buys 40% of its urea/phosphates from the region and faces "large-scale malnutrition risk for the poor."
*   **The Domino Effect:** The shortage of sulfur—a byproduct of processing sour crude—throttles the production of sulfuric acid. This acid is critical for fertilizer production, wastewater treatment, and **mining metals like copper and cobalt**. MacIntosh highlights the Democratic Republic of Congo, which imports 500,000 tons of sulfur annually and is now declaring *force majeure* on its copper belt. "You can't get your electric vehicle battery without your copper and your cobalt," he notes.

### Cascading Failures: Chips, Grids, and Corporate Margins

The dependency on this strained energy and chemical base is total. MacIntosh points to a critical, overlooked vulnerability: Taiwan.

> "Taiwan imports 98% of its energy. Qatar supplies 30% of its LNG. They got storage of 11 days. So now their power grid is going to start failing. This is where TSMC [the world's leading chipmaker] is."

This exposes the fragility of the global semiconductor supply chain, which depends on a precarious energy supply.

The ultimate economic impact, he argues, will be violent margin compression across all industries. Soaring input costs and impossible-to-price supply delays will crush corporate profits.

> "You can't reprice your goods higher to account for the higher cost of production... So now you have a real supply constraint... This translates into massive cost explosion and margin compression."

He predicts this will spill over into credit markets, lead to disastrous earnings seasons, and finally hit equity markets—particularly the hyper-concentrated S&P 500 and NASDAQ, which are set to add money-losing companies like SpaceX and OpenAI to their top holdings.

### The Investment Landscape: Navigating the Chaos

MacIntosh concludes that we are rapidly moving into an environment where the return *of* capital will trump the return *on* capital. His firm has been positioned accordingly for years, with heavy investments in energy and a strategic short position on the NASDAQ as a hedge.

> "It's a shitty situation but you got to make the best of it... I'd rather be making money in the chaos than not making money in the chaos."

The takeaway is stark: the interconnected system built on just-in-time logistics and concentrated chokepoints is fracturing. The effects—persistently high oil prices, food shortages, and broad economic contraction—are not transient headlines, but the defining features of the coming era.
