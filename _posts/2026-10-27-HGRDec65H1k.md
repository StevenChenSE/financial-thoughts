**David Busch, CIO of Trajan Wealth, discusses the shifting landscape of fixed income, the hidden risks in the AI infrastructure boom, and the diverging realities of today’s consumers.**

The financial markets are currently navigating a complex intersection of high bond yields, volatile tech earnings, and a bifurcated consumer economy. David Busch, Chief Investment Officer at Trajan Wealth, provides a detailed outlook on how investors should position their portfolios in this environment. While the AI narrative continues to drive equity valuations, Busch highlights that the debt markets are revealing critical weaknesses, particularly among companies financing massive capital expenditures without immediate returns.

## Fixed Income: A Return to Relevance

For nearly five years, the traditional 60/40 portfolio was widely considered dead due to a bear market in bonds. However, with Treasury yields reaching multi-decade highs, Busch argues that fixed income has regained its utility as a diversifier and ballast for equity portfolios.

Historically, rising rates correlated with lower valuations, a bearish signal. However, strong corporate earnings have supported stock prices despite higher borrowing costs. For investors looking to allocate capital back into fixed income, Busch identifies the **3-to-5-year part of the yield curve** as the sweet spot.

### Managing Duration Risk
Longer-duration bonds (10-to-30-year Treasuries) carry significant price risk. A bond with a duration of 20 to 25 means that for every 1% change in interest rates, the bond’s value fluctuates by 20–25%. To mitigate this volatility while locking in attractive yields, Busch recommends focusing on:
*   **Investment-Grade (IG) Corporates**
*   **Treasuries and Agencies**
*   Avoiding riskier sectors of the bond market

## The AI Trade: From Tech to Infrastructure and Adoption

The current market narrative around Artificial Intelligence is evolving through distinct phases. Busch outlines how this transition impacts different sectors:

1.  **Phase One:** Technology companies and chip manufacturers saw the initial run-up in equity prices.
2.  **Phase Two:** The AI infrastructure build-out is currently underway, providing tailwinds for historically "value" sectors like utilities, materials, and industrials. These companies are behaving more like growth stocks due to this new demand.
3.  **Phase Three:** The next leg of the trade involves the *adopters* of AI. Companies that integrate AI to improve efficiency and profitability—such as financials and software firms—are poised for potential outperformance.

### The Monetization Challenge
Investors are now demanding a return on invested capital (ROIC). Hyperscalers are making massive capital expenditures (CapEx) in the face of higher interest rates, leading to increased debt loads and earnings compression. Companies that miss earnings estimates or lower forward guidance are being punished swiftly by the market.

## CoreWeave: The Bond Market’s Warning Signal

One of the most critical insights from Busch’s analysis concerns **CoreWeave**, a company heavily involved in AI infrastructure. The bond market is often an early indicator of financial distress. CoreWeave’s bonds have seen yields spike above 15–16%, while its return on invested capital remains below 10%.

Busch notes that CoreWeave’s yields are trading nearly **500 basis points above the triple-C curve**, a rating category associated with high default risk. This suggests the market is pricing in a significant probability of default, or as Busch phrases it, the company is **"marching towards default."**

While Busch clarifies that this does not guarantee an imminent bankruptcy, it highlights the severe feedback loop facing leveraged tech firms:
*   Lower creditworthiness leads to higher yields.
*   Higher yields increase financing costs.
*   This further strains cash flow and balance sheets.

Recovery may require funding from larger hyperscalers or private markets, as public markets are pricing the risk too high for traditional issuance.

## The Two-Speed Economy and Consumer Stress

Beyond the tech sector, the broader economy exhibits a "K-shaped" or two-speed recovery. Busch observes a stark divide between the ultra-wealthy and the rest of the consumer base.

### Luxury vs. Staples
In regions like Scottsdale, Arizona, luxury spending on cars, homes, and high-end goods remains robust. However, this does not reflect the broader economic reality for the middle class. As inflation persists—driven largely by energy scarcity tied to geopolitical conflicts like the war in Iran—middle- and upper-middle-class consumers are trading down.

This shift benefits **consumer staples** and lower-cost retailers, which are seeing increased demand as households switch from discretionary luxury goods to essential, affordable items. Busch predicts that if higher interest rates lead to an economic slowdown or higher unemployment, this trend will accelerate, further favoring staple stocks over consumer discretionary names.

### Inflation Outlook
Current inflation is not demand-driven but rather supply-side and geopolitical in nature. The Federal Reserve faces a dilemma: raising rates may suppress demand but cannot fix energy supply issues caused by global conflicts. Non-farm payrolls have remained resilient, but average hourly earnings are not keeping pace with inflation, eroding purchasing power. Busch anticipates slower economic growth over the next several quarters as the economy adjusts to these persistent cost pressures.

## Conclusion

Investors must look beyond headline equity valuations to understand underlying risks. The bond market is signaling distress in highly leveraged AI companies like CoreWeave, while the consumer landscape reveals a fragile middle class trading down for essentials. In this environment, fixed income offers valuable portfolio ballast, particularly in intermediate durations, and equity selection requires distinguishing between genuine growth opportunities and value traps where earnings fail to support valuations.
