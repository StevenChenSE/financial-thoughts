In the spring of 2026, the Federal Reserve faces what economists call the "classic central banker's nightmare" — a negative supply shock that simultaneously produces higher inflation and a weaker economy. It's a scenario where the conventional playbook falls apart: a weakening labor market calls for lower interest rates, but persistent energy price shocks demand higher rates to combat inflation.

Barry Eichengreen, distinguished professor of economics and political science at UC Berkeley and author of the new book *Money Beyond Borders: Global Currencies from Crisis to Crypto*, offers a sobering assessment of where monetary policy stands and what it means for the dollar's future as the world's dominant reserve currency.

"The more Trump and Bessant complain about the Fed," Eichengreen observes, "the more likely it is that the Fed will have to keep interest rates high to signal to the markets that they mean what they say about inflation and they won't be deterred."

## The Federal Reserve's Stagflation Dilemma

The situation facing incoming Fed Chair Kevin Walsh (the transcript references this individual, though the name appears partially garbled) represents the most challenging environment for monetary policy in decades. The Fed confronts what Eichengreen describes as a "nightmare scenario" — contradictory signals that force policymakers to choose between competing mandates.

### Why This Time Is Different

Eichengreen distinguishes between two scenarios that inform his thinking:

- **Before the Iran conflict**: Inflation was "very gradually declining from slightly below 3%" — a trajectory that would have supported keeping interest rates on hold while monitoring employment growth
- **After the energy shock**: With Middle East tensions disrupting oil and gas exports through the Strait of Hormuz and Qatari refining capacity requiring years to repair, "higher energy prices are going to persist and the Fed is going to have to address them"

### The Energy Shock That Won't Go Away

Energy Secretary projections suggest the energy shock will persist through the end of 2026 at minimum. This matters enormously for monetary policy because:

- Temporary supply shocks can be "looked through" — the standard response is to maintain the previous interest rate trajectory
- Persistent shocks require active response — the Fed cannot ignore sustained price pressures without losing credibility

"The more Trump and Bessant complain about the Fed," Eichengreen explains, "the more likely it is that the Fed will have to keep interest rates high to signal to the markets that they mean what they say about inflation and they won't be deterred."

## Political Pressure and Central Bank Independence

The relationship between the White House and the Federal Reserve has become increasingly fraught. Eichengreen details a range of potential actions the administration might pursue:

### Options Under Consideration

- Firing sitting Federal Reserve governors (such as attempts to oust Lisa Cook)
- Removing Jay Powell from his board position even after his chair term ends
- Reconstituting the regional Reserve Bank presidents who serve as independent voices on the FOMC
- Doubling down on various measures that, in Eichengreen's assessment, "none of which would be good for the conduct of monetary policy"

### The Irony of War and Independence

Eichengreen penned a column in early February titled "What Are Kevin Walsh's Priorities?" Even before the Iran conflict began on February 28th, he noted the irony that a president "who values filthy above all else" might be remembered as "a key protector of central bank independence."

That observation has only grown more relevant as inflation has accelerated. "In increasingly inflation control will have to be a priority," Eichengreen states, "and that will mean reasserting the Fed's independence from the Treasury and the White House. In that sense, Mr. Walsh may have to sacrifice his good fellowship with the president in order to protect the independence of the Fed."

### The Correct Policy Path Forward

When asked what both fiscal and monetary policy should look like going forward, Eichengreen is direct:

1. **Contain inflation first** — the Fed must signal commitment to its inflation target
2. **Only then loosen** — once credibility is restored, rates can be lowered
3. **Address fiscal deficits** — the debt held by the public now exceeds 100% of GDP and rises by approximately 6% annually
4. **Create a medium-term plan** — "I'm not saying that we need to raise taxes dramatically or cut government spending now, but we need to put a medium-term plan in place"

The fiscal problem, Eichengreen emphasizes, "is of longstanding and it's on the verge of spiraling out of control." Yet "neither party shows a willingness to address those fiscal problems."

## Banking Supervision: A Return to 2008?

The discussion around Kevin Walsh's potential priorities raised concerns about financial regulation. In a February 9th column, Eichengreen noted that the proposed approach "would appear to come perilously close to obscuring the Fed's responsibility for banking supervision and financial stability, which could encourage additional loosening of the Fed's regulatory reigns."

### What's Already Happened

Eichengreen notes that the Fed has already begun loosening oversight:

- Dodd-Frank reforms from 2010 are being reversed
- Stress test severity has diminished
- Capital requirements for systemically important banks have been relaxed

### The Dual Mandate Question

Walsh has argued the Fed should "focus on its knitting" — concentrating on price stability and maximum employment while avoiding "mission creep" into climate change and gender equity issues.

Eichengreen agrees that central banks lack appropriate instruments for addressing climate or gender issues through standard interest rate policy. However, he draws a sharp distinction:

> "The central bank's regulatory instruments and its interest rate policy are directly relevant to financial stability. So I would hope that Walsh will make clear that he still takes the Fed's responsibility for financial stability seriously while at the same time emphasizing that it should focus on its core mandate."

On the question of whether the Fed should preserve its dual mandate structure (unlike the European Central Bank, which has only price stability), Eichengreen is clear: "The dual mandate has served the Fed well, and on those occasions where it's also paid close attention to its responsibility for financial stability, the dual mandate more broadly defined has served the Fed well."

## The Dollar's Fragile Safe Haven Status

Perhaps no topic in the conversation reveals more about the changing landscape of global finance than the dollar's role as the world's premier safe haven asset. Eichengreen walks through two revealing episodes:

### Episode One: The Iran War (February-March 2026)

On the first two trading days after conflict erupted in the Middle East — March 2nd and 3rd — the dollar strengthened by approximately 1.5%. This represents the traditional safe haven response: more volatility, more uncertainty, and funds move into the dollar rather than out.

### Episode Two: Tariff Announcements (April 2025 "Liberation Day")

When the US administration announced and then delayed reciprocal tariffs, volatility spiked — but the dollar *weakened*. This inverted the traditional relationship.

"The traditional safe haven relationship weekly reasserted itself," Eichengreen notes, "but after six months where the dollar kind of went haywire, there was growing uncertainty about the durability of that safe haven character of the dollar."

### The Fragility Revealed

Eichengreen's assessment is sobering: "We've learned that the dollar safe haven status is more fragile than we thought previously."

Markets have historically taken for granted that when volatility spikes, the dollar strengthens because:

- The market in US Treasuries is the single largest, most liquid market in the world
- The US government reliably stands behind its debt obligations

After April 2nd, 2025, however, "volatility did spike, but the dollar weakened rather than strengthening because of uncertainty about the stance, direction, competence of US economic policy."

The Iran war episode showed the safe haven relationship recovering — but with a caveat: "One and a half percent appreciation of the dollar against a basket of major currencies was a relatively small move, raising the possibility that at least some investors who would have traditionally moved into dollars in response to this spike in volatility and uncertainty were having second thoughts."

## No Viable Alternatives to the Dollar

Given the dollar's fragile status, one might expect competitors to emerge. Eichengreen sees no realistic challengers in the near term:

### Why Alternatives Fall Short

- **The Euro**: "The Europeans haven't been able to create a sufficient stock of safe euro-denominated assets to play the role that US Treasuries have played historically"
- **The Chinese Renminbi**: "Starting out leagues behind the US and the dollar"
- **Non-traditional reserve currencies**: Even collectively, the currencies of "small open well-managed economies like Australia, New Zealand, Singapore, South Korea, the Scandinavians" are "way too small to provide the global liquidity provided by the US and the dollar"

### The "There Is No Alternative" Problem

Many assume the dollar will continue to reign supreme simply because no credible alternative exists. Eichengreen warns against complacency: "I think there can still be a crisis of confidence in the dollar if people begin to wonder about..."

- The competence of US economic policy
- The sustainability of US public debts
- The separation of powers and rule of law in the United States
- The independence of the Federal Reserve

Such concerns could trigger "flight away from the dollar toward gold and other less liquid assets" — producing "a shortage of liquidity, the liquidity that greases the wheels of cross-border trade and finance."

This would create, in Eichengreen's words, "a 1930s-style scenario where globalization is at risk partly because of the lack of sufficient global liquidity."

## Historical Patterns: The Rise and Fall of Global Currencies

In *Money Beyond Borders*, Eichengreen examines the entire history of cross-border currencies and payments from ancient Greece and Rome through the modern digital era. What patterns emerge?

### What Makes a Currency Rise to International Dominance

The rise of international currencies occurs on the back of:

**Economic Strength**
- Successful economic growth
- Becoming a trading power
- Becoming a financial power

**Military Protection**
- Ability to protect ocean shipping lanes
- Security for imports, exports, and foreign investments

**Political Preconditions (Domestic)**
- Separation of powers
- A Senate, parliament, or legislature where investors are represented
- Institutions that can check arbitrary action by executives

**Political Preconditions (International)**
- Durable alliances with foreign partners
- Credibility as a "reliable steward" of others' holdings

### What Causes Currencies to Fall

The decline follows when these components reverse:

- Economic stagnation
- Being overtaken by other trading powers
- Financial problems
- "Military adventures that overtax the fisc"
- Political problems at home — "like those I see the United States experiencing at the moment"
- Fraying geopolitical alliances and "questions about the future of NATO and whether the US is regarded in Europe and East Asia as a reliable alliance partner"

## Gold's Enduring Relevance

In an era of digital currencies and blockchain technology, why does gold still matter? Eichengreen offers a characteristically nuanced answer.

### Why Gold Has Survived

**John Maynard Keynes called gold "a barbarous relic" in 1925** — and he's right that more efficient alternatives exist for storing wealth and organizing payments. Yet:

- "History tradition casts a long shadow"
- Any well-diversified investor wants a commodity component in their portfolio
- Central banks especially need a mix of safety, liquidity, and return in their reserves
- Gold is the commodity they have traditionally held — and tradition persists
- The London Metal Exchange provides liquidity that many other commodity markets cannot match — you can "buy, sell, lend, post as collateral" gold vaulted at the exchange or the Bank of England

### Gold's Two Functions

Eichengreen summarizes gold's appeal as "a mix of history tradition on the one hand and diversification and liquidity benefits on the other."

- **History and tradition**: Explain why gold has maintained a psychological and cultural hold
- **Diversification**: Protection "in the face of uncertainties about whether US Treasury bills will hold their value or not"

This explains why central banks — despite having access to sophisticated financial instruments — continue to hold gold as a core reserve asset.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The Fed faces a stagflation nightmare** — the combination of energy-driven inflation and economic weakness forces a painful choice between raising rates (to fight inflation) or cutting rates (to support a weakening economy)

2. **Political pressure threatens central bank independence** — the more the White House criticizes the Fed, the more the Fed must signal resolve on inflation to maintain credibility with markets

3. **The dollar's safe haven status is more fragile than assumed** — two recent episodes (tariffs and the Iran war) revealed uncertainty about whether the dollar will strengthen when volatility rises

4. **No credible alternative exists, but that doesn't guarantee permanence** — the euro, renminbi, and smaller currencies all fall short of providing the liquidity the global financial system requires, yet a crisis of confidence in US institutions could still trigger capital flight

5. **Gold's enduring relevance combines history with practical diversification** — while not the most efficient payment system, gold provides the liquidity, tradition, and hedge against policy uncertainty that central banks and investors continue to value
