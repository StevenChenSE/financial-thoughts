At Consensus Miami, John Woo—President of Ava Labs and former Wall Street tech investor—sat down with David Lin to discuss one of the most pressing questions facing investors today: where does value actually hide in the AI era?

His answer may surprise you. While most investors chase the hottest AI names, Woo argues that the real opportunity lies in the often-overlooked semiconductor infrastructure powering the AI revolution—and that TSMC remains significantly undervalued relative to its peers.

"I know not only professional hedge fund investors, venture people, as well as operators in all of these companies," Woo explained. "TSMC is historically a low-vol, very steady, high-quality company. But relative to other AI stocks, it's still very cheap, especially considering the growth rates."

## The TSMC Investment Thesis: Why Asia's Chip Giant Remains Underappreciated

TSMC dominates the semiconductor fabrication industry, producing chips for Apple, Nvidia, AMD, and virtually every major technology company. Yet despite this critical position in the AI supply chain, Woo sees a persistent valuation discount—driven by two factors:

**Geographic proximity to China**

While TSMC's technical prowess is unmatched, investors apply a "China risk" premium to the stock. This geopolitical discount, Woo suggests, creates a buying opportunity for those who understand the company's irreplaceable role in global technology supply chains.

**Limited investor awareness**

"US companies just get naturally more publicity," Woo noted. "If I have the same amount of money in two equal companies, would you go for the US market?" He acknowledges the inherent advantage US-listed companies enjoy in capturing investor attention—but argues this dynamic creates mispricings that savvy investors can exploit.

### The Cultural Advantage: Why Taiwan's Engineering Excellence Matters

When asked about TSMC's ability to sustain its technological edge—the core thesis for semiconductor investors—Woo pointed to a deeper competitive advantage: culture.

"We were 10 people in the room, all Cornell engineers, and some were Asian—hard work ethic. But it didn't matter. We're all hardworking. We were very entrepreneurial at the same time. If you can blend those two things together, that's the ideal scenario."

In Taiwan, Woo observed, the engineering culture runs deep:

- Engineers work three-shift schedules
- During natural disasters or emergencies, staff return immediately to facilities
- The "work until you drop" mentality—while extreme—creates operational reliability that competitors struggle to match

"Unfortunately, the reality of the world we live in in technology today in 2026 is that consistent and constant innovation is an edge," Woo said. "The only edge—even in quant trading firms—is constant innovation. This is why some of these firms have a gazillion PhDs, because new models have to come out."

### Beyond TSMC: Asia's AI Hardware Advantage

The semiconductor story extends beyond TSMC. Woo highlighted several other Asian companies positioned to benefit from AI infrastructure buildout:

- **Memory companies in Korea** (Samsung, SK Hynix)
- **Tokyo Electron in Japan** (semiconductor equipment)
- **Various Taiwan-based suppliers** throughout the AI supply chain

"Asia is very good at the hardware and the electrical engineering layer of AI, which is really where a lot of the things that are in scarcity are," Woo explained. "There's a lot of good things to look at in Asian companies, and TSMC is probably at the very top of that list."

### The US Subsidy Factor: Intel vs. Asian Competition

Recent US government initiatives to support domestic semiconductor manufacturing—particularly Intel—have raised questions about whether American companies can eventually challenge TSMC's dominance.

"Intel is very good at making CPUs, and part of the reason why Intel's up is not just because of good management and because they're trying to create foundry-level success in the US, but it's also because there's a scarcity in CPUs with the agentic AI world coming," Woo observed.

President Trump's stated goal of achieving 50% chip manufacturing in the USA adds another dimension. Woo supports domestic manufacturing expansion but remains realistic:

"There's nothing wrong with the president of the United States wanting jobs and manufacturing coming to the US. I think that's a great thing. But they are still far away."

For investors, this suggests a nuanced approach: US subsidies may provide tailwinds for Intel and American chip companies, but the technological gap with Asian competitors remains substantial.

## Building an AI-Focused Portfolio: The Long-Term View

When asked how investors should position their portfolios in this environment, Woo drew a striking historical parallel:

"This is a super exciting time. It reminds me of the internet days. If you can find a portfolio—and you're going to get some of them wrong—but if you can find the Amazon, the Google, and I guess Nvidia of back in the day, just hold it and don't worry about the ups and downs."

For individual investors without professional risk management capabilities, Woo's advice is straightforward:

- **Identify the best companies** in various parts of the AI world
- **Build a diversified portfolio** across the AI stack
- **Hold for the long term** without day-trading volatility
- **Accept that some picks will fail**—the winners will more than compensate

### The Professional Approach: Finding Bottlenecks

For professional investors, Woo sees a different strategy emerging: identifying bottlenecks in the AI supply chain and positioning ahead of them.

"They keep finding the next bottleneck and getting ahead of that. This is why you're seeing these super spikes."

The progression has been notable:

1. **GPU/compute** (Nvidia)
2. **Networking** (companies like Arista)
3. **Photonics** (optical interconnects)
4. **Memory** (Micron)

"Micron was supposed to be a cyclical slow memory business," Woo noted. "On PE basis, it's still cheap. People don't know how long it could last, but yet the stock looks like it should be trading with no earnings, and yet it's trading less than 10 times next year's earnings."

### Navigating the Hype Cycle

The AI space is flooded with announcements promising to disrupt entire industries. Woo urged caution:

"Just because something is announced doesn't mean it's going to destroy that entire industry."

He illustrated the point with an example from his own company: "My team is mostly engineers, and they're very forward thinking. They love to play with new AI tools. It got to the point where as soon as one of my guys in engineering comes to me and says, 'Oh, look how cool we can do this,' literally all of a sudden SAS stocks go down, security stocks go down."

The reality, Woo argues, is more nuanced:

- **Disintermediation is difficult** at the core infrastructure level
- **Legacy systems create inertia**—replacing an entire tech stack is far harder than adding a new component
- **Business networks and supply chains** provide durable moats that pure technology announcements often overlook

"In the internet age, you probably remember every time there was a rumor that Google was going to go into travel, all of a sudden Expedia and Priceline and Booking.com stocks would crash. Twenty-five years later, they're still here and doing better. And somehow Google never was able to get into travel."

## Bitcoin and the New Phase of Crypto

Woo also weighed in on Bitcoin's dramatic price action, discussing the market's evolution as crypto approaches its 18th year.

"Crypto is going into a new phase right now. It's no longer the new new thing."

He drew an analogy to startup founders: "You're asking why there are serial entrepreneurs who take a company from zero to one, hand it off to someone who goes from one to ten. Why don't you just go from zero to ten? Because some people like zero to one, and some people like one to ten."

This maturation explains the changing of the guard Woo observed earlier in 2025: the "old guard" Bitcoin holders who entered years ago have been selling into the new institutional flows via ETFs and wealth management channels. That transition, he believes, has largely completed.

### The Institutional Education Pipeline

Looking forward, Woo sees additional catalysts from the wealth management channel:

- **RIAs (Registered Investment Advisors)** control trillions of dollars
- **Morgan Stanley and other wirehouses** have now been permitted to offer crypto exposure
- **The education process takes 4-6 months** from permission to actual client recommendations

"We're getting to the point where since the beginning of the year, when these channels were allowed to start recommending it to their individual investors, they've been educated. And now the end user knows it enough. So I think you have that flow from those people that's also going to be helpful."

### Valuation in a New Market Environment

The rise of retail participation and quant-driven trading has changed market dynamics:

- **ETF flows dominate** in many sectors
- **Stocks move together** more than ever, regardless of individual fundamentals
- **Individual investors** can both participate in upside and suffer in corrections

"I love the fact that individual investors are involved and they feel like they can participate in this world, which is great," Woo said. "But at the same time, I do worry that they over-speculate and then there's less emphasis on valuation."

The lesson from the internet era remains relevant: "There are still people I remember who bought pets.com or something and have never recovered. And it's a shame because if they just held on to the 10 or 15 really good ones or bought an index, they'd be very well off."

## The Future of Tokenized Assets: Bridging Traditional Finance and Blockchain

A significant portion of the conversation focused on Avalanche's strategy for institutional adoption. Woo explained the vision:

"From day one, we wanted to be both—serve the crypto-native customer but also be the default infrastructure for all institutional assets to come."

The recent partnership with Broadridge Financial Solutions represents a major step in this direction. Broadridge processes $8 trillion in assets monthly and handles critical financial infrastructure including:

- Corporate actions
- Proxy voting
- Settlement and record-keeping

"We're very proud to say a deal where they're going to do the online token on-chain tokenized equities and put that record on an Avalanche blockchain," Woo explained. "They do all the corporate actions and stuff, and that's a big part of traditional equities."

### The Asset Layer vs. Infrastructure Layer

Woo distinguished between two critical components of the tokenized asset ecosystem:

**Asset Layer**

- Stablecoins (billions in circulation)
- Money market and treasury tokens
- Private credit and private equity
- Tokenized public equities

**Infrastructure Layer**

- Wallets and off-ramps for fiat-to-crypto
- Custody solutions
- Corporate action processing (where Broadridge fits)
- Compliance and regulatory systems

"Everyone wants tokenized real-world assets, but they forget like you need systems underneath that to make those things smooth," Woo noted. "Broadridge is one of those partners and pieces that help the infrastructure layer of the future world of tokenized real-world assets."

### The Straits X Model: Regional Stablecoin Distribution

The partnership with Straits X—a Singapore-based company building stablecoin infrastructure—illustrates Avalanche's approach to capturing value across the ecosystem.

"They had their own stable coin, obviously Asia-oriented. They're doing it on top of Avalanche. That goes to show you what they think of Avalabs and Avalanche. They're also connected and plugged into Alipay, Grab Pay. So they have massive distribution."

### Where Value Captures in the Blockchain Stack

When asked about value capture across the ecosystem—still one of the most debated questions in crypto—Woo provided a nuanced answer:

"The apps have to be able to run a business. There's a reason why it's for them to be on top of infrastructure. The layer-ones also have to figure out their token economics so that they can be alive to service all of these applications."

Avalanche's architecture supports multiple capture points:

- **Custom blockchains**: Traditional finance partners can spin up private, closed chains while still connecting to the broader ecosystem
- **Cross-chain connectivity**: Once established, these private chains naturally want to connect to the Avalanche ecosystem for liquidity
- **EVM compatibility**: Connection to the broader Ethereum world and all DeFi infrastructure

"It starts with the customer having to be able to create value for themselves, and they are already doing it. And then once they start connecting, the natural value will flow through to the rest of the Avalanche ecosystem as well."

## Key Takeaways

1. **TSMC remains undervalued relative to AI peers**: Despite commanding critical position in semiconductor supply chains, TSMC trades at a discount due to geographic risk perception and limited US investor awareness.

2. **Asia holds structural advantage in hardware**: Companies in Taiwan, Korea, and Japan dominate the electrical engineering layer where AI bottlenecks create investment opportunities.

3. **Long-term holding beats active trading**: For individual investors, identifying quality companies and holding through volatility—similar to early internet plays—outperforms short-term speculation.

4. **Bitcoin's maturation creates new dynamics**: The transition from early adopter "OG" holders to institutional wealth management flows marks a new phase in crypto's evolution.

5. **Tokenized assets require infrastructure, not just tokens**: Successful blockchain adoption depends on traditional finance infrastructure—corporate actions, compliance, custody—that Avalanche and partners like Broadridge are building.
