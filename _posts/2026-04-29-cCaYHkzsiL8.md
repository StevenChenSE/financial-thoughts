In a wide-ranging interview that touched on everything from government debt to gold prices, from OPEC's unraveling to the future of work, veteran investor and financial commentator Clem Chambers delivered a stark warning about the decade ahead. While markets grind to new highs and headlines celebrate apparent prosperity, Chambers sees a different reality unfolding—one defined by structurally higher inflation, an inevitable bond crisis, and a technological revolution that will reshape who thrives and who merely survives.

"Weather the storm or surf the wave," Chambers counsels. "The answer is to be on the offensive and looking for the opportunities."

## The Bond Crisis That Nobody Wants to Talk About

Jamie Dimon of JP Morgan recently issued what Chambers calls a "very accurate warning"—that rising government debt levels could trigger a crisis in the bond market, forcing policymakers to act only after markets have already exacted their toll.

The mechanics of such a crisis are now familiar, at least in outline. During the 2022 UK gilts crisis, institutional investors had levered themselves to the hilt on government bonds, borrowing cheaply against their holdings to buy more bonds in a virtuous circle that appeared genius as long as interest rates kept falling.

"All the pension funds had levered up government securities to the moon on borrowed money," Chambers explains. "And you can lever to the moon. Now, when interest rates are going down, you appear to be a genius, but the bend in the end kills you because you're levered up."

When a new government signaled policy changes, panic ensued. Interest rates spiked, margin calls multiplied, and only emergency intervention by the Bank of England prevented catastrophe. The lesson, Chambers argues, is not that such crises are unpredictable—they're inevitable cycles of hubris followed by collapse—but that governments can always find ways to manage the aftermath.

### The Government Monetization Loophole

Here is where Chambers' analysis cuts deepest. Governments, he argues, possess an escape hatch that technically violates their own rules but has been exercised repeatedly anyway: they simply buy their own bonds back.

"You need money because you've got a deficit. Well, here's some government bonds. Oh, look, we've lent them to the Fed. Oh, the Fed's given us cash. Oh, we've spent it. Oh, we've made some more. Oh, we've made it into we've monetized those bonds and turned it into cash."

During the 2008 financial crisis, this is precisely what occurred. The government published bonds, "sold" them to the Federal Reserve, received cash in return, and spent it—effectively printing money while maintaining the fiction of legitimate borrowing.

"You're not allowed to do that. No, no, no. Oh, we've done it. Oh, is anybody complaining? Well, no. We're the government. We're the ones that do the complaining, and we need the money, so we're not going to complain."

This monetization, Chambers notes, is "the shortcut to inflation" and, taken too far, leads to hyperinflation. It's the approach taken by governments in developing nations that cannot enforce effective taxation. They print money instead, which flows into the economy, and inflation becomes the equivalent of a hidden tax.

## Why Inflation Is Coming Back—And This Time, It's Different

During the COVID pandemic, Chambers correctly predicted that inflation would arrive and subsequently that it would be tamed. He now sees the opposite trajectory ahead.

"Inflation's coming again now. It's going to be at least as bad as the COVID inflation, and it's going to go for a lot longer because it's not going to be inflation created to pave over something that lasted six months."

The difference, he emphasizes, is structural. The coming inflation will fund not crisis management but an attempted industrial buildout—the kind of manufacturing renaissance that policymakers talk about but which requires staggering capital investment. Add to this the infrastructure demands of artificial intelligence, and the picture becomes clear.

"You're talking about trillions of dollars of money, liquidity, cash that's going to be needed to fund all this. They're not going to say, 'Oh, no, we haven't got the money to fund this. We're not going to print this.' No, they're just going to pull the lever and they're going to pump it out."

The critical distinction Chambers makes is between inflationary types:

- **Hyperinflation**: The worst kind, caused by governments printing money without productive investment to show for it
- **Ordinary inflation**: Money eroding in value with minimal economic activity to compensate
- **Productive inflation**: Money being printed but flowing into assets that generate returns, wages that outpace price increases

Chambers believes the United States will pursue the productive variety, which is "the least worst kind of inflation." This means:

> "You're going to get inflation 7, 8, 9% for five, six, 10 years. And it's here, and it's arrived."

### Why Bonds May Not Be the Disaster Some Predict

Conventional wisdom suggests that structurally rising inflation means structurally falling bond prices. Chambers challenges this assumption directly.

"Interest rates on government bonds are completely and utterly fixable by the government. They just buy them back and push that price down. Simple as that. That's what QE is, right?"

Japan offers the extreme example: interest rates pushed to negative territory through persistent bond buying. The technique works to suppress yields—but it carries an unavoidable consequence.

"You're suppressing those interest rates as far as government debt is concerned, and anybody who holds government debt is going to make out like a bandit, and there's more money in the economy, and there's more liquidity, and it's all going round and round. And of course, the price of housing goes up, and of course the price of everything goes up. And that's what's called inflation."

The deeper insight Chambers offers concerns the nature of American government debt itself. Of the roughly $34 trillion national debt, only about 30%—roughly $12 trillion—is owed to foreign creditors. The rest represents intergovernmental holdings or debt to American citizens and institutions.

"If a government owes money to its own people, is that a big deal? Americans rich, government poor. Money coming off your bonds yield is a reverse taxation. That's not so bad."

The danger emerges when debt is owed to external creditors who won't accept printed currency in settlement—Argentina's predicament, where the debt is essentially 100% foreign-denominated and cannot be monetized without consequence.

"This is the journey to the end of the line. It might not be now. It might not be in five years. It might not be in 10 years. But you're on that journey."

## OPEC's Unraveling and the Oil Price Time Bomb

While financial markets obsess over interest rates and AI stocks, the energy markets face their own upheaval. The UAE has announced its departure from OPEC, a move that Chambers attributes to multiple factors:

- Anger over Iran's missile and bombing strikes on UAE infrastructure
- Fundamental disagreements with Saudi Arabia over production quotas
- Iran's output under threat from potential military action

"UAE produces 12% of OPEC's total output," Chambers notes. "If OPEC starts breaking up one by one, what's going to happen to the price of oil?"

His analysis suggests higher prices ahead, though not necessarily immediately. The dynamics depend on how the Iran situation resolves:

- If the US walks away tomorrow: moderate impact
- If the US invades Iran: significant disruption
- If the US imposes a siege: Iranian oil production collapses for an extended period

"The supply's going to be supply issues, and that means there's going to be price issues."

The irony, Chambers observes, is that other producers may work frantically to fill the gap, driving short-term supply. But budget pressures on oil-producing nations—many of which have accumulated deficits—mean they need high prices more than ever.

## Gold and Silver: The War Premium Explained

Gold and silver have been consolidating for months, hovering in familiar ranges. According to Chambers, the explanation lies in the Taiwan situation.

"Gold is for war. Go look at the Taiwan stock exchange. It's just gone through the roof."

The thesis is counterintuitive: gold surges in anticipation of war but then comes under pressure once fighting actually begins. The reason is practical.

"During war, nobody's going to take your paper. Nobody's going to take your IOU. They're only going to take gold. That gold is for war. Gold is the currency of war. It's the intergovernmental currency."

This explains the January dynamics perfectly. As reports emerged of internal tensions between President Xi and the People's Liberation Army—tensions so severe that military leaders apparently balked at the prospect of an invasion of Taiwan—the invasion risk seemed to recede. Gold sold off while Taiwanese stocks rallied.

"Gold down, Taiwan up. So now that Taiwan's not going to get shelled flat, the Taiwan index is kind of reacting to that good news."

Chambers believes the invasion threat is merely delayed, not cancelled. The PLA's reluctance to suffer casualties storming Taiwan's cliffs has postponed but not eliminated the risk. When the situation stabilizes internally, the war premium in gold will return.

For investors, his advice is pragmatic:

- **Don't sell into weakness**: The geopolitical backdrop remains supportive
- **Dollar-cost average**: Buy a little each month over a couple of years
- **Wait for the trend**: Volatility compression typically precedes directional moves

## Investment Opportunities in the New Paradigm

Chambers identifies several sectors positioned to benefit from the convergence of AI infrastructure buildout, energy demands, and structural inflation:

### Nokia and the 6G Buildout

"Nokia is the biggest joke in town—apart from the fact that Nvidia just put a billion dollars into them to build out 6G because the Chinese infrastructure people aren't trusted anymore."

Nokia represents one of the few Western companies capable of building mobile infrastructure. Nvidia's billion-dollar investment signals institutional confidence in its capabilities. The stock, Chambers notes, has been moving significantly higher on relatively low valuations.

### Nuclear Power: The Bottleneck Everyone Needs

"Energy is the bottleneck. Energy distribution, energy making—all of that stuff is the big choke point. And there ain't going to be enough energy, full stop, until further notice."

Companies that build nuclear power stations face a supply-demand imbalance that will take years to resolve. Fluor, Chambers mentions, is one example—a company that builds nuclear facilities and isn't expensive relative to its future prospects.

### The Copper Imperative

"People that make cables are going to need a lot of cables. Copper, full stop. There ain't enough copper."

Before the AI revolution arrives, the physical infrastructure must be built. Copper demand will surge alongside data centers, power transmission, and the broader electrification of everything.

## The AI Economy: Winners, Losers, and the Truth About Tech Layoffs

Meta is laying off 10% of its workforce. Microsoft is offering voluntary retirement packages to thousands. Nike is cutting 1,500 jobs. The headlines suggest economic weakness. Chambers sees something different entirely.

"Those companies were hiring people for non-existent jobs, and they were doing it for decades. And now rather than hire a load of people that do nothing because it looks good on a spreadsheet, they are having to build out things that cost real money."

The Silicon Valley term for employees, Chambers reveals, is "stock." Companies hired people to keep competitors from hiring them—to maintain human capital inventories in a world of abundant capital.

"They've got to build big sheds full of Nvidias and buy nuclear power stations. All of a sudden, they can't afford stock."

The efficiency revolution enabled by AI compounds the effect. Chambers cites programmers who now accomplish the work of 20 colleagues using AI tools. Their output has multiplied, not diminished—but that same multiplication applies to job requirements.

"How much work does a lot of people actually do? Now, if the answer is two hours a day, AI is going to be pretty brutal to that organization."

The division will be stark:

- **Economically active individuals**: Those who adapt, specialize, and leverage AI tools will create "massive wealth"
- **Economically passive individuals**: Those who merely hope for stability will face serious challenges

## The Federal Reserve: Rhetoric Versus Reality

With the FOMC meeting on the horizon, Chambers offers a clear prediction: expect dovish action wrapped in hawkish language.

"All I know is the Fed is going to turn ultra dovish while claiming to be ultra hawkish. They're going to go on a complete printing bender."

The dynamic stems from political pressure. The new Fed chair, Chambers suggests, will claim to be fighting inflation while simultaneously delivering the rate cuts that policymakers want. The justification will be "deflation"—a convenient fiction that justifies money printing.

"They've told you. I like to run it hot, says Donald Trump. And you reckon that guy's going to come in and say, 'No, no, lower interest rates, Mr. Trump. I'm a hawk.' No, he's going to be on a bend."

The contradiction between stated hawkishness and actual dovishness won't trouble policymakers because, as Chambers explains, inflation serves multiple purposes. It erodes debt burdens, stimulates economic activity, and creates the appearance of prosperity even as purchasing power declines.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Bond crisis is inevitable but manageable**: Jamie Dimon's warning is accurate, but governments will ultimately "fix" the problem through monetization, trading a bond crisis for more inflation.

2. **Plan for 7-9% inflation for the next decade**: The combination of industrial buildout, AI infrastructure, and political unwillingness to curtail spending means structurally higher inflation is here to stay.

3. **"Gold is for war"**: Gold rallies before conflicts and sells off during them. The Taiwan situation has temporarily deflated the war premium, but the geopolitical risk remains and will return.

4. **Energy and infrastructure will dominate**: Whether oil prices, nuclear power, copper, or telecom infrastructure—physical assets that enable the AI revolution will outperform.

5. **The economic divide will widen**: For the economically active—those who adapt, leverage tools, and seek opportunities—the AI era will create unprecedented wealth. For the passive, the outlook is grim.

As Chambers concludes: "Fear is not the answer. Being on the defensive is not the answer. The answer is to be on the offensive and looking for the opportunities."
