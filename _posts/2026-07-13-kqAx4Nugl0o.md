*A conversation with David Hunter, Chief Macro Strategist at Contrarian Macro Advisors, on the Mining Stock Daily podcast*

## Introduction

In a wide-ranging discussion recorded during the July 4th holiday week, contrarian macro strategist David Hunter laid out one of the most ambitious market frameworks on Wall Street: a final parabolic melt-up in equities, followed by a historic global bust, and ultimately a commodity-led supercycle that could redefine the investing landscape for decades. With targets that include the S&P at 10,000, gold at 7,000 this year and 20,000 by the early 2030s, and silver potentially reaching 1,000 in the next cycle, Hunter's thesis challenges conventional thinking at every turn.

## The Contrarian Philosophy: Right at the Turning Points

Hunter, who began his career in 1973, distinguishes his approach from knee-jerk contrary thinking. "My view of contrarianism is it's not knee-jerk contrary," he explained. "It's at the important inflection point, whether that be top or bottom of a market." At tops, most investors are bullish; at bottoms, most are bearish. Hunter flips that script, becoming most bullish at bottoms and most bearish at tops.

This philosophy has been tested throughout a 44-year secular bull market that Hunter traces back to August 1982, when the Dow sat at roughly 780. Today, with the Dow above 50,000 and P/E multiples expanded from mid-single digits to the mid-20s, Hunter believes the secular cycle is approaching its terminal phase.

## The Bull Market Is Still Intact

Despite his bearish long-term outlook, Hunter remains bullish in the near term. He has raised his targets multiple times in recent years:

- **S&P 500:** 10,000
- **Nasdaq:** 36,000 (up from 32,000)
- **Russell 2000:** 4,000
- **Dow:** 67,000

Hunter sees nothing to suggest an imminent rollover. After a consolidation period in response to the Iran conflict in February and March, he expects a strong July and August. Monthly charts, he argues, show the parabolic phase has already begun even if daily action looks choppy.

## A Broadening Market, Not a Narrow One

Contrary to the prevailing narrative that this rally is solely about AI and mega-cap technology, Hunter insists the market is broadening. The Russell 2000 outperformed the Nasdaq and S&P in the first half of the year, up more than 21%. Financials look poised for a major move after years of consolidation. Industrials, driven by reshoring and grid buildout, have been steadily advancing. Even basic materials appear ready to move higher.

"Things like industrials are going unnoticed," Hunter noted, pointing to the XLI as a "wonderful chart." He sees this broadening as bullish rather than a sign of exhaustion, since true tops are typically marked by narrowing leadership, not expansion.

## The Parabolic Semiconductor Trade

Hunter acknowledges that AI and semiconductors are "up on stilts." Micron is up roughly 3.6 to 3.8 times in just a few months, Applied Materials has posted extraordinary gains, and the memory chip cycle is fundamentally driven by earnings that outpace price moves. But history teaches caution: double and triple ordering always accompanies such cycles, and the unwind can be swift. Hunter simply doesn't believe the peak has arrived yet.

## Fuel for the Next Leg Up: Institutional Skepticism

One of the most compelling elements of Hunter's near-term thesis is the persistent skepticism among institutional investors. Since the October 2022 bear market bottom, hedge funds and institutional managers have fought this rally every step of the way. They called 4,000 a bear market rally, then 4,400, then questioned every new high. Retail investors, ironically, have been the more prescient group, buying bottoms while institutions panicked.

Hedge funds reportedly hold record shorts right now. This "wall of worry" is exactly the fuel that powers the next leg higher. When these skeptical institutions finally capitulate and turn bullish, that will be the signal the top is in.

## The Warning Signs: Massive Leverage

While bullish on the immediate future, Hunter is unequivocal about the dangers lurking beneath the surface. He defines leverage in two forms: debt as the leverage on the economy, and derivatives as the leverage on the markets. Both are at historic extremes, far beyond 2008-2009 levels.

- **Private credit** may be the "subprime of this cycle," showing signs of stress that have worsened rather than improved.
- **Private equity**, which Hunter notes is just leveraged buyouts in a prettier package, is embedded in pension fund and endowment portfolios with massive embedded leverage.
- **Global debt** is exploding, with Japan, China, Europe, Canada (now looking like the U.S. did in 2008-2009 from a real estate perspective), and Australia all carrying dangerous leverage levels.

"Leverage works great and enhances on the upside... but leverage decimates on the way down," Hunter warned.

## The Global Bust Scenario

Hunter envisions a bust that could rival or exceed 2008-2009 in severity, potentially triggering a deflationary period initially as the economy enters its worst downturn since the Great Depression. The S&P could fall 80% from peak to trough, the largest bear market in the post-Depression era.

Critically, central banks will be reluctant to respond aggressively. Powell and potential successor Kevin Warsh have repeatedly declared they will not repeat the QE infinity mistakes of 2008 and 2020. But when faced with a free-falling global financial system, they will have no choice but to print, albeit grudgingly and slowly.

Hunter estimates the Fed's balance sheet could expand to as much as $30 trillion (from the current $6.7 trillion), with central banks worldwide printing a combined $50 trillion. This response will initially be deflationary as it stabilizes the system, but the lagged inflationary consequences will be severe.

## The Inflation Aftermath: 25% and Rising

The money printing that saves the system from depression will eventually ignite an inflation cycle unlike anything in modern American experience. Hunter, who managed money during the early 1980s, recalls T-bills at 21% and inflation above 20%. The next cycle could be worse:

- **Inflation:** Potentially reaching 25%
- **Long bond yields:** 18-20% (versus the 15% peak in the early 1980s)
- **T-bills:** Well over 20%

With an additional $15-20 trillion in government debt on top of the current trajectory, servicing the debt at these rates becomes mathematically impossible. At some point, the printing press must be shut down because, as Hunter put it, "you can't pump more gasoline on a raging fire." When that happens, the welfare state, Social Security, and the broader fiscal framework face existential stress.

## Precious Metals and Commodities: The Next Cycle Leaders

This is where the thesis becomes most relevant for the Mining Stock Daily audience. Hunter's commodity targets are striking:

### Precious Metals
- **Gold:** $7,000 this year (pre-bust), with a long-term target of $20,000 by 2032-2033
- **Silver:** $200 this year, falling to roughly $50 in the bust, then surging to $1,000 in the next cycle

During the bust itself, gold could fall 50% from $7,000 to $3,500, and silver could drop 70-80% from $200 to $50. But from those levels, the upside in the subsequent commodity-led cycle would be extraordinary. Mining equities, with their inherent leverage to metal prices, could outperform even those targets.

### Industrial Commodities
- **Copper:** Current target of $8 per pound, with potential for $20-30 in the next cycle
- **Natural gas:** From the current $3 range to potentially $50
- **Oil:** Could fall to $30 in the bust before surging to $500 in the 2030s

Hunter argues the next cycle will be driven by reshoring, grid buildout, data center construction, and AI infrastructure spending, all of which create insatiable demand for industrial commodities. With capacity taking a decade or more to develop, supply will lag demand dramatically, replicating the dynamic currently seen in memory chips.

## The Dollar and the Bigger Picture

A scenario involving $50 trillion in global money printing and the destruction of government fiscal capacity is inherently bearish for the U.S. dollar. Dollar-denominated commodities will benefit from both supply-demand fundamentals and currency debasement. The cycle represents the end of the consumer-driven economy that has defined America since the 1980s and the beginning of an industrial and commodity-driven era.

## What to Watch: Sentiment as the Key Signal

When asked for actionable indicators, Hunter was unambiguous: sentiment is the primary signal. Specifically, investors should watch for the moment when institutional investors, analysts, and portfolio managers universally declare that the bull market has "years to run." That widespread conviction, not any single technical level, will mark the top.

The Bank of America fund manager survey is one useful gauge. Social media sentiment on X and financial media commentary provide another. When the narrative shifts from skepticism to universal bullishness, "that's when I want to run the other way."

Hunter offered a sobering caution: nobody rings the bell at the top. Most investors will neither get out at the top nor avoid catching it on the way back down. The risk of exiting too early is also real; if his targets are correct, there may be another 30-40% of upside remaining before the bust begins. Investors who exit now and chase the market back in at 9,800 on the S&P will have defeated their own purpose.

## Conclusion

David Hunter's framework is not for the faint of heart. It envisions a final parabolic melt-up in equities driven by institutional capitulation, a deflationary bust worse than 2008-2009, a central bank response that initially saves the system but eventually produces 25% inflation, and a subsequent commodity supercycle that could be the most lucrative opportunity of a generation for those positioned in precious metals, mining equities, and industrial commodities.

Whether or not the timeline proves precisely correct, the underlying logic, that 44 years of secular bull market accumulation, unprecedented leverage, and inevitable monetary policy responses must eventually produce a reckoning, demands serious consideration. For investors in the mining and commodities space, the message is clear: accumulation during periods of doubt has historically been the strategy that generates generational wealth, and the coming cycle may offer the most dramatic example yet.
