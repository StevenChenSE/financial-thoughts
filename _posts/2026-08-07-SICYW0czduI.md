## How to profit from gold with less mining risk

Gold royalty and streaming companies have quietly become one of the most powerful ways to profit from a commodity super cycle, and they’re a favorite of veteran investor Rick Rule. While most investors focus on mining stocks, royalty companies operate like venture capital firms for the mining sector, buying a share of future production instead of digging the ground themselves. This simple difference creates an incredible risk–reward profile, especially in gold and silver.

In this article, we’ll break down:

- What royalty and streaming companies actually are
- How they outperform major mining indices like GDX and GDXJ
- Why they avoid many of the pitfalls that hurt miners
- Where the best current opportunities may lie (including mid-tier names)

## What Are Gold Royalty Companies?

Royalty companies don’t own mines. Instead, they provide upfront capital to mining companies to explore, develop, or expand projects. In return, they receive:

- A percentage of production (royalty model)
- Or the right to buy a set volume of metal at a fixed price, regardless of market price (streaming model)

These agreements are typically long-term. Because royalty companies diversify across dozens or even hundreds of projects, a single failure has a minimal impact. They’re essentially a diversified, low-cost way to gain exposure to the underlying commodities—especially gold and silver, which dominate most portfolios.

## Why Royalty Stocks Have Outperformed Mining Stocks

The performance gap between top royalty companies and traditional mining ETFs is staggering over the long term.

- Wheaton Precious Metals vs GDX: Over roughly 20 years, Wheaton has outperformed the GDX (senior gold producers ETF) by about 14x.
- Franco-Nevada vs GDX: Franco-Nevada has delivered approximately 19x outperformance versus the GDX over the same period.
- Versus GDXJ (junior miners): Since the GDXJ’s launch, it has lost around 6%, while Wheaton is up roughly 827% and Franco-Nevada around 967%.

These aren’t isolated anomalies. They reflect the structural advantages of the royalty model: full upside to metal prices, with a fraction of the risk carried by operating miners.

## How the Royalty Model Cuts Mining Risk

Miners face binary risk: if their single project or a handful of projects fail, the stock can collapse.

- Example: Vizsla Silver, once a top silver explorer, saw its share price crash about 58% in six weeks after operational and market setbacks and never fully recovered.
- Royalty companies, by contrast, typically hold only 1%–3% exposure to any single project. A project failure barely dents their portfolio.

At the same time, they still benefit from the full upside of commodity prices. If gold or silver surges, royalty companies profit from dozens of projects simultaneously, without having to manage each mine.

## Lower Costs and Protection from Inflation

One of the most misunderstood advantages of royalty companies is their cost structure.

- Minimal corporate overhead: They don’t need large workforces, heavy equipment fleets, or complex mine-site operations.
- No direct exposure to rising input costs: If oil prices spike to $200, or labor costs surge, miners’ margins suffer. Royalty companies are insulated—they still receive their agreed percentage of production or fixed ounces.
- Fixed-price streaming deals: Streamers buy metal at a contract price that is often far below market, creating powerful margin expansion when prices rise.

Over the life of a mine, operating costs tend to increase as ore grades decline and mines go deeper. Royalty companies don’t care: their revenue is tied to production volumes, not the cost to extract each ounce.

## Full Exposure to Gold and Silver Upside

Most major royalty companies are heavily weighted toward precious metals, especially gold and silver. This means:

- Direct exposure to rising commodity prices
- Diversified exposure across many jurisdictions and projects
- Reduced political, operational, and geological risk compared to single-miner exposure

While some royalty companies also invest in critical metals like copper or even uranium, the core of the sector remains anchored in gold and silver—two assets central to any commodity bull market thesis.

## Why Junior Explorers Are Tempting (But Risky)

Junior mining explorers can deliver spectacular multi-bagger returns when they strike a major deposit. But:

- Most discoveries don’t pan out.
- A single failed project can wipe out the company.
- These stocks are highly volatile and driven by short-term hype.

Royalty companies offer a way to capture a slice of that junior mining upside without the single-point failure risk. They’re involved early in many projects, so they benefit from discoveries and development, but diversified across a broad portfolio.

## The Risks of Investing in Royalty Companies

The royalty model is strong, but not risk-free. The main considerations:

1. Valuation: Royalty companies trade at premium valuations compared to many miners because of their lower risk. If gold, silver, or copper prices stall or decline, these stocks can correct sharply as earnings expectations fall.
2. Lack of “sexy” hype: Royalty companies rarely generate headline-grabbing drill results or dramatic short-term moves. For investors chasing momentum, they can feel boring.

As one German CEO famously said during the dot-com bubble: “Short-term hype can beat quality, but long-term quality always wins.” Royalty companies are a textbook example of that principle.

## Mid-Tier Royalty Companies: Where the Opportunity May Lie

According to Rick Rule, the most interesting opportunities today may not be in the largest names, but in smaller royalty and streaming companies.

- Large royalty companies like Franco-Nevada and Wheaton Precious Metals trade at significant premiums to net asset value (NAV).
- Second- and third-tier royalty companies often trade at discounts to NAV—and increasingly larger discounts relative to market cap.

Rule points to names such as:

- Triple Flag
- Osisko Royalties (OR Royalties)
- Elemental

For investors willing to do the work, these valuations create potential arbitrage opportunities. Either:

- The market corrects the valuation gap, or
- Larger royalty companies acquire the smaller ones—a strong tailwind for shareholders.

## Why ETF Flows and Institutions Matter

Part of the recent surge in top royalty stocks is structural:

- Franco-Nevada and Wheaton are heavily weighted in major mining and precious metals ETFs.
- ETF inflows during bull markets push their prices—and valuations—higher.

Mid-tier royalty companies don’t benefit as much from this ETF bump, leaving them relatively cheaper. At the same time:

- Institutions love the royalty model for its low risk and stable cash flows.
- But many mid-tier names are too small for large funds.
- As these companies grow or merge, they become eligible for institutional money, which can trigger valuation re-rating.

## M&A Is Heating Up in the Royalty Sector

Mergers and acquisitions are already more active in the royalty space than in traditional mining.

- Larger mid-tier royalty companies are acquiring smaller peers.
- The goal is to reach the scale that attracts institutional capital and justifies higher multiples.

For investors, this means mid-tier royalty companies carry an M&A premium that large, already-fully-valued names don’t.

## Final Thoughts: Quality Over Hype in the Mining Sector

Royalty and streaming companies offer:

- Full upside to rising gold, silver, and other metal prices
- Dramatically lower operational and project-specific risk
- Minimal cost inflation exposure
- Strong long-term outperformance versus mining ETFs
- Additional upside potential via M&A and valuation convergence in mid-tier names

If you believe in a commodity super cycle and rising precious metal prices, royalty companies provide one of the cleanest, most efficient exposures available. They won’t deliver the wild, short-term spikes of hyped junior miners, but over time, their quality, cash flow, and risk profile make them a powerful choice for patient investors.
