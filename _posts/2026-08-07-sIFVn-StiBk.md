## How Political Maneuvering and Monetary Reality Are Reshaping Global Markets

In a historic move not seen since the Asian financial crisis of 1998, the United States government intervened to prop up the Japanese yen. Joining host David Linn, Professor of Applied Economics at Johns Hopkins University Steve Hanke dissects the motivations behind this unprecedented action, the return of the bond vigilantes, and the accelerating forces driving US inflation.

### The Historic Yen Intervention

The Japanese yen recently hit a 40-year low, trading at approximately 164 to the US dollar. Suddenly, the currency spiked back to 157 following a coordinated intervention by the US and Japan. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessant authorized the purchase of an estimated 5 to 10 billion yen, a figure hinted at by a note found on his desk at a Camp David meeting.

President Trump cited his relationship with the Japanese Prime Minister as the reason for the support, while Bessant emphasized regional stability. However, Hanke argues that the intervention was politically motivated rather than economically sound. He points out that Japan lacked the "firepower" to intervene alone and needed the US to backstop their efforts.

Hanke is skeptical of the move's long-term efficacy. He notes that the 4% appreciation is likely a "dead cat bounce." Instead of addressing market fundamentals, the intervention introduces a "big player"—a politicized entity unconstrained by profit and loss—into the market. This creates volatility and shifts focus away from economic realities.

### Fundamentals Driving Yen Weakness

To understand the yen's decline, Hanke looks past interest rates to money supply growth. He explains that monetary policy is defined by changes in the money supply, not just interest rates. Japan’s money supply is growing at an anemic 2.2% year-over-year, far below the 6% growth rate needed to hit a 2% inflation target.

This sluggish money supply growth caps nominal GDP growth, resulting in low inflation, low interest rates, and a weak currency. Compounding these issues is Japan’s new Prime Minister, who plans to increase military spending and cut taxes, leading to a larger deficit and a higher debt-to-GDP ratio. These fundamentals suggest the yen is likely to face further weakness despite the temporary intervention.

### The Carry Trade and US Market Stability

The US intervention also aimed to protect American financial markets. A critical factor is the yen carry trade, where investors borrow cheaply in yen to invest in higher-yielding assets like US stocks and bonds. If the yen had collapsed further, the Bank of Japan might have been forced to raise interest rates sharply, causing the carry trade to unwind.

Such an unwind would force investors to sell US assets to buy back yen, potentially triggering a market selloff. Furthermore, Japan is a major holder of US government bonds. If Japan began selling these bonds to support the yen, US bond prices would fall, and yields would rise.

Hanke notes that Treasury Secretary Bessant is keen to keep the 10-year Treasury yield below 4.5%, but it has already breached that red line at 4.637%. Rising yields increase the cost of rolling over the massive US national debt. Interest servicing currently consumes 35% of personal income tax revenue, making rising yields a critical threat to fiscal stability.

### Lessons from the Asian Financial Crisis

The scale of this intervention echoes the 1998 Asian financial crisis. Hanke, who served as Chief Economic Advisor to Indonesian President Suharto during that crisis, recounts the political failures of the time.

He argues that the IMF's recommendation to float the Indonesian rupiah was a disaster that led to hyperinflation and food riots. Hanke had advised Suharto to implement a currency board—a system where the currency is fixed to the dollar and backed 100% by reserves. This would have stabilized the rupiah, which appreciated 25% immediately upon his appointment.

However, Hanke claims the US and IMF rejected the currency board for political reasons. They wanted Suharto removed from power for various geopolitical and domestic political motives. This historical context underscores Hanke's view that current interventions are often driven by political chess games rather than economic prudence.

### The Return of Bond Vigilantes

Turning to the US bond market, Hanke highlights the return of the bond vigilantes. The 30-year Treasury yield has stayed above 5%, reflecting growing concern over fiscal health and inflation.

Hanke attributes this surge to three main factors:
1.  **Accelerating Money Supply:** According to the Divisia M4 measure, money supply growth has accelerated from 4.6% in late 2024 to 8.5% in early 2025.
2.  **Geopolitical Risk:** Tensions involving a potential war with Iran are spooking investors.
3.  **Fiscal Deficits:** The US deficit remains out of control.

This combination is a "deadly cocktail" for bond prices. Hanke emphasizes that rising yields are a direct response to the "inflation genie" being out of the bottle due to rapid money supply expansion.

### Inflation, Borrowing Costs, and Affordability

As long-term interest rates rise, consumer borrowing costs follow. Mortgage rates are already at their highest since the 2008 financial crisis. The 10-year Treasury yield serves as a benchmark for credit cards, mortgages, and other loans.

Hanke suggests that financial stocks may remain strong in this environment. Banks benefit from a steepening yield curve, and their increased profits allow them to lend more, further contributing to money supply growth. Additionally, the deregulation of banks and reduced capital requirements are acting as an easing of monetary policy, even if the Fed raises rates.

Ultimately, the rising cost of living is a significant political liability. Hanke notes that affordability is the "Achilles heel" for incumbents. When median-income earners feel pinched by inflation and high borrowing costs, political sentiment shifts, regardless of party affiliation.

### Conclusion

The US intervention in the yen market is a symptom of deeper structural issues. While it may provide a temporary pause in currency volatility, it does not address the fundamental drivers of the yen's weakness or the rising inflation in the US. With money supply accelerating, deficits soaring, and bond vigilantes active, the path forward points toward higher interest rates and continued economic pressure on households.
