**March 19, 2026** – Global markets are reeling as a dramatic surge in oil prices triggers a widespread sell-off, raising alarms over inflation, supply chain breakdowns, and a potential challenge to the decades-old petrodollar system.

The immediate trigger was an Iranian missile strike on Qatar’s Ras Laffan Industrial City, the world’s largest LNG export complex. The attack, which caused significant fires and damage to facilities responsible for roughly 20% of global LNG supply, sent European gas prices soaring 35% at the open. In response, Brent crude surged nearly 7% to above $113 a barrel, while West Texas Intermediate (WTI) hovered around $96. This created a staggering $17 spread between the two benchmarks—the widest gap in 11 years.

Equity markets plunged globally. The Dow Jones hit a fresh 2026 low, down 300 points, while the S&P 500 extended a week-long slide. European stocks dropped more than 2% across the board. Notably, gold continued a sharp correction, down nearly 18% from its January all-time highs.

The geopolitical landscape grew more volatile. Former President Donald Trump issued a stark warning on Truth Social, blaming Israel for provoking Iran’s retaliation and threatening a massive U.S. response if Iran attacked Qatar again. Meanwhile, the Strait of Hormuz remains effectively closed, with only about 90 ships crossing since conflict began on February 28th, compared to over 100 daily before.

A potentially more profound development was reported by CNN: a senior Iranian official stated Iran would consider allowing tankers through Hormuz on one condition—that the oil be traded in Chinese yuan, not U.S. dollars. This threat strikes at the heart of the petrodollar system, with implications for dollar strength, U.S. sanctions leverage, and global finance.

To analyze the crisis, we spoke with **Joseph Shakar**, president of Shakar Energy Research Services and a 40-year veteran of the investment management industry.

### The Broken Supply Chain: Decoding the Brent-WTI Gap

The interview began by examining the abnormal divergence between Brent and WTI crude. Shakar explained that this signals a severely broken global supply chain.

“WTI is, of course, the U.S. price… Brent, because a lot of the Middle Eastern crude and European crude is priced off Brent. There's a lack of supply of Brent-priced crude,” Shakar said. The normal spread is around $5, but the current $15-$18 premium reflects a “shortage argument because of these straits of Hormuz being closed.”

He clarified that the closure isn't absolute. Iran is still shipping 1.5-1.6 million barrels per day to allies like China and Turkey. “The problem,” he emphasized, “is the IRGC is firing at… tankers” from other nations and, more alarmingly, at critical infrastructure. “If you destroy the facilities, the pipelines, the gathering systems… that's where you have a massive expenditure and a lot of time to repair.” This threat to physical assets, not just transit, is what’s spiking prices.

The damage is already being felt downstream. “Prices of product because of the shortages in Europe and in Asia have gone over $200 a barrel,” Shakar noted, “so the $147 [peak] which was in ‘08 has already been exceeded.”

### Inflationary Tsunami: How High and For How Long?

A key question: if the war ended today, would prices quickly revert? Shakar was skeptical, citing logistical tangles. “Logistics matters… you're going to see a lag effect.” The critical variable is the extent of physical damage. “Once we get to the point where we know that major facilities have not been destroyed… then we're going to come down from these lofty levels.”

However, the conflict has upended all previous forecasts. Shakar’s firm had projected an average 2026 oil price of $70, expecting $80 by Q4. “That all changed March 1st… we're now saying that you don't know what to forecast… until you know when the war ends.”

The disruption extends far beyond energy. Fertilizer components like sulfur come from the damaged Qatari field, threatening planting seasons and future harvests. Shakar warned this could fuel a broader inflationary spike.

“The inflation data that we're seeing… by months from now, if the war has not ended, those numbers will go up and we're looking probably at a **5 to 8% inflation problem** for a period of time as that goes through the supply chain,” he stated, referring to headline consumer price inflation globally.

The duration of this inflation depends on the war’s length and destruction. “If there's not a lot of physical destruction… it could be a couple of months.” But with attacks continuing, “every week that it drags on, add a couple bucks to WTI… if it drags on for a month or two, we could be $120, $130.”

### Recession Risk and a "Reverse COVID" Shock

Could these prices trigger a recession? Shakar referenced a Wall Street Journal economist survey pointing to $138/barrel as a potential tipping point. “I think the key thing is the number gets higher because of… inflation,” he said, suggesting a range of **$125-$130** for WTI could precede a global recession.

He contrasted today’s crisis with the COVID-19 supply shock, calling this the “reverse.”
“At that time we had demand destruction… The price went down because there was too much supply.” Today, the problem is pure supply constraint.

For North America, the Strait of Hormuz closure is “a non-event” for goods imports, Shakar said, but for Europe and Asia, it’s “very material.”

### The Petrodollar Gambit and Investment Shifts

The potential Iranian demand for yuan-denominated oil trades presents a systemic risk. Shakar sees a gradual shift. “Russia is now selling oil to Japan… in yuan. So, they do not want U.S. dollars… In Asia, the yuan is becoming more of a trading currency,” though not yet in major Western financial centers.

The crisis is reshaping investment landscapes. “Energy security has become an important issue,” leading to a surge in North American energy stocks. Shakar highlighted the Canadian energy index hitting record highs, with some stocks doubling or tripling. He sees value in Canadian firms relative to U.S. peers and emphasized Canada’s role as the world’s fourth-largest producer.

“The question is,” he posed, “are we going to use [our resource advantage] or are we going to say politically… we're not prepared to do that?”

### Gold’s Plunge and the Looming Credit Crisis

Despite the turmoil, gold has sold off sharply. Shakar attributed this to a broad “risk-off” trade and potential margin calls stemming from a deeper issue: a burgeoning credit crisis.

“We have a credit crisis in private debt and private equity… In some ways, you could say we have a bigger credit crisis than we had in ‘08,” he warned. As spreads between junk and high-quality bonds widen, investors facing margin calls may be liquidating profitable positions like gold to raise cash.

While gold is traditionally a wartime safe haven, Shakar noted, “who says you can't have a bit of a correction?” Long-term, however, he doesn’t dismiss predictions of $10,000/ounce gold, especially if a debt crisis leads to government defaults.

The surging oil price, he concluded, exacerbates these financial stresses. The initial shock from the Middle East has now rippled out, testing global energy security, monetary systems, and financial stability simultaneously. The path forward depends overwhelmingly on the duration of the conflict and the real-world damage left in its wake.
