The Economy's Hidden Warning Signs Everyone Is Overlooking

What the latest jobs report, oil prices, and inflation data reveal about the true health of the global economy

The gold market has lost trillions in market cap over recent weeks, prompting investors to seek answers about the true state of the economy. Adrian Day, president of Adrian Day Asset Management and portfolio manager at Europacific Gold Fund, recently joined the show to share his analysis on precious metals sentiment and the latest economic indicators—and his perspective is notably cautious.

"The thing that all of the central banks and policy makers seem to be focused on is the inflationary effect of a higher oil price," Day explained. "What they should be focusing on equally, if not more so, is that every time we've had an oil price spike in the last 80 years, we have had a recession."

This observation cuts to the heart of a critical disconnect in how markets and policymakers are interpreting current economic data. While headline numbers may suggest strength, a deeper examination reveals significant vulnerabilities that most investors are missing.

## The Jobs Report: What the Numbers Don't Tell You

The latest employment data presents a confusing picture. Initial jobless claims came in at 225,000 on June 4th—up 13,000 and the highest since early February. Yet non-farm payrolls simultaneously beat expectations by a wide margin. On the surface, this appears contradictory. Day believes the answer lies in looking beyond the headline figures.

"If you look more carefully at it, virtually all of the jobs created were either local government jobs or health care, which in the US is a sort of pseudo government enterprise," Day noted. "So healthcare and local government jobs accounted for virtually all of the new jobs created."

This matters because a healthy jobs market shouldn't depend heavily on government employment. But the concerning details don't stop there. Day cited Bureau of Labor Statistics data revealing deeper structural issues that the headline beat obscures.

According to the BLS report, 4.8 million people are looking for full-time work but cannot find it and are working part-time while being counted as employed. Additionally, 6.2 million people have stopped actively looking for work but still want jobs—meaning they no longer count as unemployed despite being unemployed. Together, these figures represent 11 million people who are either underemployed or effectively unemployed.

"If you're delivering Uber Eats, you're counted as employed, but it's not really what you, as an engineer, want," Day explained. The long-term unemployed—those out of work for more than a couple of weeks—have not seen improvement either, suggesting these workers remain trapped in unemployment for the duration of their benefits.

## Oil Prices and the Strait of Hormuz: A Ticking Clock

With WTI crude trading above $80 per barrel—down significantly from $100 but still elevated—the energy market has become a focal point for economic concerns. The Strait of Hormuz remains a critical chokepoint, with China cutting crude imports from 11.7 million barrels per day in February to under 9 million by late May.

Market strategists suggest China is acting as a pressure valve on global energy prices, but Day questions how much of this is by design versus necessity.

"I don't know if it's chicken and egg," Day said. "But I think part of it is just that they can't get the imports."

The longer-term fundamental picture points toward higher prices, according to Day. Global inventories are being depleted, strategic reserves require rebuilding, and when the strait reopens, countries will rush to restock—including the United States, which depleted its oil stockpile back in 2024.

"Look at the beginning of this year when we were $60 a barrel—that was in the lowest decile of its price, the most hated commodity out there," Day recalled. "People were thinking we could run a modern industrial society without fossil fuels, and you only have to look at Germany and England to see that isn't working out too well."

What the Hormuz situation has highlighted, Day argues, is not just price risk but supply security—particularly for Asian nations. Japan, Thailand, Indonesia, and Malaysia depend heavily on oil imports, and they've seen the risk of not being able to source energy at any price. As a result, coal production and investment have increased in these countries over the past three months, as coal represents a domestic energy source they can control.

## The Inflation Picture: Why Higher Oil Prices Eventually Matter More

The latest CPI data shows headline inflation at 4.2%—the highest since April 2023—with energy up 23% year-over-year. Core CPI, stripping out food and energy, sits at 2.9%, still nearly 50% above the Fed's 2% target.

Day emphasizes that unlike most commodities, oil price increases ripple through the entire economy.

"When its price moves up, that price moves through the entire economy. It moves to every single good in the store," Day explained. "A higher price of oil translates into higher prices for all goods, and even to a lesser extent services, because you have to travel to get to the service."

This matters for monetary policy. While the Fed focuses on core PCE to strip out volatility, persistently high energy prices will eventually feed into core inflation measures. The question is timing—and whether that translation has already begun or lies ahead.

## Central Bank Policy: A Divided Outlook

The ECB recently raised rates for the first time since 2023, drawing comparisons to 2008 when rate hikes preceded economic collapse. Day sees this as potentially problematic.

"When the strait is opened and oil does flow again, I am betting that a lot of countries, but also other large end consumers, will rebuild those stockpiles they should have had six months ago but didn't," Day predicted. "That's one factor. But beyond that, I think the oil price on a longer-term fundamental basis is going to go meaningfully higher over the next few years."

As for the Fed, Day doesn't expect rate hikes this year—particularly with the anticipated change in Fed leadership.

"I think it would be a huge surprise if his first move as Fed chairman was to raise rates," Day said. "What it does mean is they're probably much less likely to lower rates in the near term. As long as the oil price stays high and gasoline and other components of CPI remain high, they are much more likely to be much less likely to lower rates."

The broader message: the era of easing appears over. Bank of England, Bank of Canada, and other central banks have signaled similar positions, though they haven't yet moved.

## Regional Disparities: Not One Global Economy

Perhaps most critically, Day distinguishes between North America's energy position and the rest of the world. The US and Canada are effectively energy independent—they import some oil and export some, but on net, they don't depend on Middle Eastern supplies or Hormuz transit.

"We are nowhere near as reliant on the Middle East as other parts of the world," Day emphasized. "Nor are we as reliant as we have been in the last 50 years."

This creates divergent outlooks. Day wouldn't be surprised if Europe heads into recession, along with some Asian countries, if the current conflict persists. The US, however, remains more insulated—though not immune.

"For half the population, the lower half in terms of income and assets, they don't have a lot of wiggle room," Day warned. "If the price of gas to get to work goes up, it means something else has to go. Unlike for you and me, if the price of gas goes up, we say, 'Look at the price of gas. Isn't it awful?' and move on. For half the population, they've got to adjust somewhere else."

## Key Takeaways

The surface-level economic data presents conflicting signals, but Adrian Day's analysis suggests several underappreciated realities:

The jobs market is weaker than headlines indicate, with 11 million people either underemployed or effectively unemployed despite official statistics suggesting strength. Oil prices, while down from recent highs, remain elevated and will likely grind higher as supply normalizes and restocking begins. Every oil price spike in 80 years has preceded a recession—and while North America is insulated, Europe and Asia face greater vulnerability. Central banks are likely done easing and may struggle to cut rates even if economic weakness emerges.

For investors, the implications extend across asset classes. Gold's recent decline reflects market expectations that central banks will prioritize fighting inflation over supporting growth—a calculation that may prove prescient or premature depending on how these warning signs evolve.
