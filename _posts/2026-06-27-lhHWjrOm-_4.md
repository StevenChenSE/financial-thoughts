## How Beijing's Policy Shift Could Signal a Bottom for Precious Metals

China's monetary policy moves are sending ripples through global financial markets, and smart investors are taking notice. In a recent episode of Capital Calls, hosted by Danny and featuring market analyst Francis Hunt and precious metals expert Eric Yuen, a critical question emerged: Is the bottom for gold and silver closer than most think?

The answer lies in a fascinating interplay between PBOC liquidity dynamics, Hong Kong's new gold infrastructure, and the broader geopolitical chess game being played between East and West.

## The PBOC Liquidity Surge: A Technical Indicator Worth Watching

Eric Yuen has been meticulously tracking People's Bank of China liquidity alongside renowned economist Michael Howell, and their findings paint a compelling picture. According to their analysis, PBOC liquidity injection into the financial system "dropped through a cliff" since the beginning of this year.

However, the tide appears to be turning. Following the conclusion of tensions at the Strait of Hormuz—referred to as the "Iran war" in the discussion—the PBOC resumed releasing liquidity. Most recently, the Chinese government announced that the PBOC is conducting reverse repos, essentially injecting fresh liquidity into the system.

Charts suggest liquidity bottomed in late May and is now ramping back up. This development carries significant weight because Michael Howell's research indicates a strong correlation between PBOC liquidity injection and global gold prices. The theory holds that Chinese buying of gold at the margin drives international gold valuations.

## China's Crackdown on Capital Flight

Within the past month, the Chinese government executed a substantial crackdown on liquidity leaks in both Hong Kong and mainland China. The target: unauthorized brokers facilitating the purchase and sale of Western equities—primarily tech stocks and AI-related securities from the United States.

Authorized Hong Kong brokers were discovered conducting unauthorized trades of US equities on behalf of mainland Chinese customers. This "huge crackdown," as Yuen described it, effectively plugged a significant leak in China's capital control system.

The strategic rationale becomes clear when examining Beijing's broader objectives. During periods of Middle East instability, any excess liquidity China injects risks being "sucked outwards" toward regional "energy black holes." By controlling liquidity during such periods, China ensures its monetary stimulus reaches domestic targets rather than funding external conflicts or arbitrage opportunities.

## The Hong Kong Gold Hub: China's New Financial Infrastructure

Perhaps the most significant development discussed is the launch of a new international gold hub in Hong Kong, scheduled for July. The Hong Kong Metals Central Clearing Company Limited (HKPMCC) will operate this offshore exchange for physical gold contracts, including spot gold and forward contracts—functionally similar to the London Bullion Market Association (LBMA).

Why does this matter? Hong Kong operates under British common law rather than mainland China's legal system, and crucially, it maintains no capital controls. For foreign entities seeking to participate in China's gold market, Hong Kong offers dramatically easier capital account setup compared to mainland China.

The Shanghai Gold Exchange International, while operating in Shanghai's tax-free special trade zone, remains subject to mainland capital controls. This regulatory environment has resulted in "very low liquidity and trading volume," making the Hong Kong alternative far more attractive to international players.

Eric Yuen interprets recent margin requirement increases at the Shanghai Gold Exchange—140% hikes and termination of T+D forward contracts for gold and silver—as deliberate steps to redirect liquidity toward Hong Kong. "When you open like a new shop or new casino or new market, you want to make sure that you have liquidity and players already in place," he explained.

## Physical Gold as Strategic Asset: China's Long Game

Francis Hunt emphasized that China is pursuing "real physical progress" in establishing gold-backed infrastructure. The evidence supports this assessment: China is actively moving 400-ounce gold bars from the LBMA to Hong Kong for re-melting to the higher 9999 purity Chinese standard and recasting into kilo bars.

This physical gold infrastructure serves a specific purpose. China aims to make physical gold "a tier one high quality liquid asset collateral" for trade with BRICS partners. The combination of RMB plus gold, supported by the Hong Kong-backed gold hub, creates an alternative to dollar-denominated trade settlement that doesn't require freely floating, capital-control-free currency.

## The US Gold Revaluation Question

Discussion turned to the speculative possibility of US gold revaluation, with some analysts pointing to high call activity at $10,000, $15,000, and $20,000 strikes by December as evidence of insider knowledge.

Francis Hunt expressed skepticism about America "winning long-term" through such a move, noting that "you just don't get given trust back" after Nixon's 1971 gold window closure. Yuen's analysis focused on the mathematics: at $4,000 per ounce, US gold revaluation would generate approximately $1 trillion, with another $1 trillion possible through gold bonds—totaling roughly $2 trillion against a national debt approaching $40 trillion.

At higher valuations like $30,000-$40,000, the consequences would be severe. "If you revalue it at let's say 30, 40,000 dollars, the entire world is going to ship their gold to you, and you got to print dollars to take the gold," Yuen explained. "You have to defend whatever new peg that you put the gold at. So, they're not going to do that. That's not going to happen."

## The Debt Math: America's Squeeze

The discussion highlighted mounting pressure on US Treasury markets. Japan is aggressively selling US Treasuries to prop up its free-falling yen, while Gulf countries "wrecked by the Iran war" are also reducing Treasury holdings. Meanwhile, new Fed Chair Kevin Walsh has indicated plans for quantitative tightening—selling $4-5 trillion in Treasuries from the Fed balance sheet.

Against this backdrop, the US must sell approximately $8-9 trillion in Treasuries over the next eight to ten months. Eric Yuen characterized this as "almost mission impossible," suggesting that any potential gold revaluation would merely "kick the can down the road" rather than solve fundamental fiscal imbalances.

## What This Means for Investors

The convergence of these factors—PBOC liquidity resumption, China's crackdown on unauthorized capital outflows, and the strategic positioning of Hong Kong as an offshore gold hub—suggests a significant shift in global precious metals infrastructure. China is methodically building the framework for gold to serve as a tier-one asset in international trade, while the US faces mounting challenges in maintaining dollar hegemony.

For portfolio positioning, the technical indicators are becoming increasingly interesting. As Eric Yuen noted, the liquidity dynamics suggest "the bottom closer than we think it is" for precious metals. The question isn't whether China is serious about gold—it's whether the rest of the world recognizes the implications of this strategic pivot.

Investors would be wise to monitor PBOC liquidity data, Hong Kong gold trading volumes, and any announcements regarding the new HKPMCC infrastructure. The pieces are being positioned on the chessboard; understanding the game being played may prove essential for preserving wealth in an increasingly complex financial landscape.
