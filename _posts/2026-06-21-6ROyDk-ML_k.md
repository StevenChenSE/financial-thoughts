The macroeconomic expert explains how fiscal dominance is reshaping the economy and limiting the Federal Reserve's effectiveness

## Introduction

In a recent interview, Lynn Alden, founder of Lyn Alden Investment Strategy, returned to discuss the most significant macroeconomic developments shaping markets today. With a new Federal Reserve chair and a tentative peace agreement in the Middle East, Alden offered her analysis on how fiscal dynamics are fundamentally altering the economic landscape.

The conversation centered on fiscal dominance—a concept that Alden argues the market still doesn't fully appreciate. While traditional economic thinking holds that raising interest rates is the solution to inflation, Alden contends that this approach becomes far less effective when fiscal deficits exceed net new bank loan creation. In such an environment, the Federal Reserve finds itself in what she describes as a very challenging position.

## Understanding Fiscal Dominance

Alden explains that fiscal dominance occurs when a country's federal debt to GDP exceeds 100%, creating a massive stock of existing debt. The United States experienced a 40-year period of rising debt to GDP, but this was offset by structurally declining interest rates until rates hit roughly zero.

"Now that we're trending sideways in interest rates and we still have high debt to GDP, we no longer have that interest rate offset," Alden noted. "Interest expense has outpaced defense spending, and there's really no clear resolution to this. It's going to be an ongoing large part of the federal deficit going forward."

In contrast to the 1970s and early 1980s—a period Alden characterizes as monetary dominance—the current era features a diminished role for the central bank. During monetary dominance, low debt to GDP meant deficits weren't the driving force on money creation. Today, the Federal Reserve's two primary tools, interest rate decisions and balance sheet decisions, have become less impactful as option space narrows.

## The Two-Speed Economy

The implications of fiscal dominance create what Alden describes as a "two-speed economy." Young families seeking to purchase their first home face near-unaffordable conditions, while the wealthy continue to perform well. Deficits primarily flow to social security, Medicare, and defense spending.

"You're in fiscal dominance when fiscal deficits in a given year are bigger than net new bank loan creation," Alden explained. "Interest rates don't do quite as much for that because every time they raise interest rates, while they could put some downward pressure on bank lending, they actually blow out the fiscal deficit even more."

The Fed's current restrictive stance on housing hasn't addressed above-target inflation because the core issue isn't bank lending—it's structural deficits. Techniques to manage this situation include financial repression, where yield curve control submerges all debt below inflation, but this typically accelerates currency debasement and higher inflation levels.

## The Middle East Peace Agreement and Economic Implications

Turning to geopolitics, Alden assessed the partial resolution to the Iran conflict as a positive direction, though she cautions it's only a memorandum of understanding with headlines expected in the coming weeks and months. The peace agreement may ease energy prices, but the longer-term economic impact remains complex.

Regarding the Trump administration's framing of the situation as a "catastrophe avoided," Alden noted that China proved more flexible than anticipated in managing oil supply disruptions. China's willingness to draw down large inventories provided a significant offset to potential shocks.

On the question of US leverage against Iran, Alden acknowledged that military and economic pressure were effective, but suggested the administration miscalculated relative leverage dynamics. Iran demonstrated resilient leadership structures, decentralized enough to recover from targeted strikes, and technological capabilities in drones and missiles that complicate traditional power projection.

The naval blockade, while effective at blocking Iranian exports, also inadvertently affected allies and other countries seeking oil. Alden observed that much of the current situation represents an effort to return to conditions before the conflict began—or broadly back to the previous Iran nuclear deal.

## Key Takeaways

The Federal Reserve faces unprecedented constraints in an era of fiscal dominance. Interest rate policy, the traditional tool for controlling inflation, becomes counterproductive when deficits are the primary driver of economic conditions. The central bank remains essential for bank regulation and banking system oversight, but its monetary policy tools have diminished effectiveness.

For investors and policymakers, understanding this shift is critical. The two-speed economy will likely persist, with housing affordability remaining strained while certain segments of the economy continue to benefit from deficit spending. Financial repression may emerge as a primary tool for managing debt, but it carries significant implications for currency value and long-term inflation.

The tentative peace in the Middle East offers potential relief for energy prices and consumer sentiment, but structural economic challenges remain. As debt-to-GDP ratios continue elevated and interest expenses outpace other government spending categories, the constraints of fiscal dominance will increasingly shape both monetary policy and market dynamics.
