The Great Depression wasn't caused by a single policy failure—it was a "perfect storm" of monetary collapse, banking crises, and protectionist tariffs working in concert. As trade tensions simmer and inflation concerns mount, understanding what actually triggered the 1930s economic catastrophe—and what finally pulled America out of it—has never been more relevant. Economist George Seljin, author of *False Dawn: The New Deal and the Promise of Recovery*, offers a historically grounded perspective on why modern trade wars, despite their severity, are unlikely to replicate the conditions that produced the worst economic disaster in American history.

## The Perfect Storm: What Actually Caused the Great Depression

Most people assume the Smoot-Hawley Tariff was the sole culprit behind the Great Depression. While those tariffs—enacted in 1930 and raising duties on thousands of imported goods—certainly mattered, Seljin emphasizes that they represent only one piece of a much larger puzzle.

The Great Depression resulted from multiple converging failures, each amplifying the others:

### The Fragile Post-WWI Gold Standard

Before World War I, the gold standard operated under relatively stable international arrangements. The post-war version, however, was fundamentally different—a fragile system "designed to economize on gold" that allowed central banks worldwide to operate with less gold reserves than the pre-war system required. This arrangement depended on sustained international cooperation, which began breaking down in the late 1920s.

When the gold standard finally unraveled, it triggered cascading collapses in money supplies, credit availability, and overall spending across the Western world.

### The Vulnerability of the American Banking System

Meanwhile, the United States faced a compounding crisis: its banking system was severely weakened, a condition that had been developing since World War I. Throughout the 1920s, thousands of banks failed annually—remarkable given that the country started with approximately 32,000 institutions. When the 1929 stock market crash hit, bank failures accelerated dramatically.

The result: a simultaneous collapse of the international monetary system and large swaths of the American banking system, creating a feedback loop of monetary destruction that the Smoot-Hawley tariff, enacted in the midst of these problems, only exacerbated.

### Policy Mistakes That Prolonged the Crisis

Once the Depression took hold, policy responses often made things worse. The National Recovery Administration (NRA), a centerpiece of New Deal efforts, implemented industry-wide price codes essentially mandating the formation of cartels. By forcing businesses to charge higher prices and pay higher wages through government decree rather than allowing market forces to generate recovery naturally, unemployment worsened rather than improved.

## Why a Second Great Depression Remains Unlikely

Despite trade wars, tariff hikes, and protectionist policies now spreading globally, Seljin argues that conditions today differ fundamentally from the 1930s in ways that make another Great Depression improbable—though recession remains entirely possible.

Several factors provide structural protection:

- **More resilient banking systems**: Post-Depression reforms, including deposit insurance and better regulation, have made financial institutions more robust—though Seljin cautions that recent banking stresses prove the system isn't failsafe
- **Learning from historical mistakes**: Policymakers now understand both what to do (stimulate demand during collapses) and what not to do (impose counterproductive price controls and cartel arrangements)
- **Active stimulus willingness**: Unlike the 1930s when the Fed remained passive and fiscal stimulus was minimal, modern central banks and governments demonstrate willingness to deploy aggressive monetary and fiscal tools

The critical insight, Seljin notes, is that "it was something of a perfect storm"—meaning the simultaneous occurrence of multiple catastrophic factors. Even though trade barriers today echo 1930s conditions, "I don't think that enough of the things that went wrong in the 1930s are likely to go wrong again."

## The New Deal Didn't End the Depression—And Neither Did WWII

Conventional wisdom credits either New Deal programs or World War II military spending with ending the Great Depression. Seljin dismisses both explanations as insufficient.

### The New Deal's Limited Impact

Roosevelt did stabilize the banking system through his bank holiday and gradual reopening, and he loosened gold standard constraints that had become untenable as the public ran on gold. These steps held genuine promise for recovery. However:

- Fiscal stimulus during the 1930s was "relatively little" despite common perception
- Federal Reserve credit remained completely flat throughout the Roosevelt years—the Fed made no deliberate effort to compensate for the monetary collapse that had occurred
- Recovery that did occur was primarily driven by massive gold inflows starting around 1933, as European war fears (especially regarding Hitler) made American holdings appear safe

Rather than welcoming this gold influx, the Roosevelt administration viewed it as threatening to generate "another boom-bust cycle." Officials actually implemented policies to counteract gold's positive influence, effectively sabotaging the mechanism that was driving whatever recovery materialized.

### WWII: A Temporary Fix, Not a Cure

World War II did eliminate unemployment—temporarily. Enormous numbers of people joined the military while factories churned out military equipment. This solved the unemployment problem "for the time being."

But as soon as the war ended in 1945, troops returned home seeking jobs, military contracts ceased, and defense spending collapsed as a percentage of GDP back to pre-war levels. Economists feared the Depression would return with a vengeance.

It didn't. And Seljin identifies the reason: the relationship between business and government fundamentally changed during wartime, shifting from the New Deal's hostility toward a spirit of cooperation. Legislation and rhetoric that had previously scared business investors away was replaced by an environment where:

- The NRA had been repealed
- Communist threats seemed distant
- Businesspeople felt confident they could reap profits from their investments

Private investment "took off like a rocket" after the war, having been "almost dead for a decade." This private investment boom—enabled by policy certainty and business-friendly conditions—is what actually ended the Depression permanently.

## Regime Uncertainty: The Modern Drag on Growth

The host raises a critical point about today's economy: despite historically low unemployment and steady jobless claims, businesses remain reluctant to hire aggressively. Seljin connects this to the concept of "regime uncertainty"—doubt about what policies will look like going forward.

During the New Deal, this uncertainty was a major factor preventing recovery. Wartime resolved that uncertainty, creating confidence about the policy environment. Today, multiple sources of uncertainty have emerged:

- Ongoing tariff wars with unpredictable trajectories
- Broader policy instability
- Geopolitical conflicts affecting critical resources

When businesses face high regime uncertainty, they postpone investments and hiring decisions. The result is muted employment growth despite low unemployment—the economy isn't generating the robust demand for workers it would if confidence were higher.

The path to stronger growth requires "more stable policies" and greater "certainty about what the government is up to." The tariff situation has stabilized somewhat, which helps, but other uncertainties—particularly ongoing conflicts affecting oil supplies—continue to weigh on business confidence.

## Inflation, Oil Shocks, and the Federal Reserve's Dilemma

The conversation turns to inflation concerns, with the host noting that oil prices have climbed toward $100 per barrel following disruptions to Strait of Hormuz shipping lanes, and CPI has risen to multi-year highs.

Seljin's assessment is sobering: "The future is not a happy future."

### The Inescapable Logic of Oil Shocks

Oil scarcity has historically produced inflation—this is "one of the more well-established facts of modern economic history." Current conditions make elevated inflation likely to persist "well above the 2% target" that central bankers consider ideal.

The critical distinction is between monetary inflation and supply-shock inflation:

- Monetary inflation results from central banks creating excessive money
- Supply-shock inflation stems from real resources becoming less available (in this case, oil)

When oil becomes scarce due to geopolitical disruptions, prices rise regardless of monetary policy. The Federal Reserve cannot manufacture additional oil supply.

### The Impossible Choice

This creates an unenviable situation: "Between a rock and a hard place."

- If the Fed tolerates inflation, prices rise as supply constraints drive them
- If the Fed tightens to fight inflation, it risks "very easily create a recessionary environment"

The 2008 financial crisis illustrates the danger. Inflation at that time was mainly driven by adverse supply conditions (including oil prices), yet the Fed pursued a hawkish stance, tightening monetary policy based on inflation fears. That tightening contributed significantly to the severity of the 2008 crisis.

Seljin emphasizes that supply shocks cannot be neutralized by monetary policy. Fed tightening can reduce price increases by suppressing demand, but it does so by creating economic pain—unemployment, output loss—rather than addressing the underlying supply problem.

"Is inflation enough to cause a great depression?" the host asks.

"No," Seljin responds unequivocally. The Great Depression's defining characteristic was a collapse in total spending exceeding one-third of the economy. While severe inflation causes genuine hardship, it produces "a very different sort of calamity"—one where money supply remains adequate or excessive, unlike the Depression's monetary contraction.

High inflation also doesn't necessarily accompany high unemployment. Severe unemployment episodes—1929, the Great Recession, COVID, the 1920-1921 depression—all resulted from "inadequate spending, not excessive spending."

## The Real Source of Economic Discontent

The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index has reached historical lows. Survey data reveals that Americans' primary concerns center on unaffordability—housing costs, wages perceived as lagging behind inflation.

Seljin distinguishes between perception and reality here. Careful statistical studies often contradict popular beliefs about declining real wages, particularly for medium-income workers. Some of the pessimism reflects "misperception."

Nevertheless, real factors are genuinely reducing prosperity. Ultimately, "you need real production, real GDP growth" for people to feel economically secure. When wars, supply constraints, and tariffs artificially reduce production below its potential, people genuinely do become poorer.

The crucial point: "It's not so much the inflation... not so much the Federal Reserve creating too much money" that drives economic hardship. Rather, it's "things that interfere with production that reduce total production of real goods and services." Wars and trade barriers—artificial constraints that prevent resources from flowing productively—rank prominently on this list.

## Dollar Depreciation and the Bitcoin Reserve Question

Public interest in "dollar debasement" surged toward late 2025, coinciding with capital flows into gold and heightened attention to Federal Reserve independence following public disputes between political figures and Fed leadership.

Seljin clarifies terminology: true debasement occurs when governments reduce precious metal content in coins while maintaining face value. What people likely mean by debasement is simply currency depreciation—dollars buying fewer goods and services.

The Bitcoin strategic reserve proposal—analogizing a government Bitcoin reserve to existing gold reserves—doesn't withstand scrutiny for one fundamental reason: "The government doesn't have to hedge against depreciation of the US dollar. It gets to depreciate the dollars and profit from it."

Private citizens need inflation hedges; the government doesn't. When central banks accumulate gold, they're typically hedging dollar exposure from their own currency reserves. The Fed, issuing the world's reserve currency, doesn't hold substantial foreign exchange reserves because it doesn't actively manage exchange rates.

This principle extends to any potential alternative reserve currency. As long as the United States doesn't attempt to stabilize exchange rates, it has little need to hold foreign currencies—even widely used ones. "We have to distinguish what changes in ordinary citizens portfolios or what changes would be rational in those portfolios and what changes would be rational in government's portfolios."

## What the Next Crisis Might Look Like

While uncertainty remains high, Seljin identifies the most probable near-term challenge: sustained inflation driven by oil supply disruptions from ongoing conflicts. The appropriate policy response, he argues, is counterintuitive but essential:

**Tolerate supply-shock inflation.** When rising prices reflect genuine resource scarcity, attempting to suppress them through monetary tightening only inflicts additional economic damage without addressing the underlying problem. The goal should be "letting that be reflected in higher prices" while ensuring no irresponsible policies (excessively easy monetary policy) accelerate price increases beyond what scarcity justifies.

The solution to supply-shock inflation is addressing the supply constraints themselves—ending conflicts, reopening critical shipping routes, stopping resource-wasting military expenditures.

## Rethinking Keynesianism: When Government Intervention Makes Sense

The final question concerns the appropriate role for government stimulus. Keynesian economics saw renewed interest following both the 2008 crisis and 2020 pandemic.

Seljin offers a nuanced perspective that transcends simplistic debates:

**Stimulus is justified only when private spending collapses.** During normal economic conditions, government should maintain appropriate monetary and fiscal settings. When a spending collapse occurs—often itself a result of prior policy failures—then monetary or fiscal stimulus to replace missing demand becomes appropriate.

Monetary stimulus, Seljin suggests, is generally "the wisest because it's less distortionary and less wasteful" than fiscal stimulus.

The important distinction: Keynes argued for macroeconomic stabilization through broad monetary and fiscal levers, not for microeconomic interventions. His actual critique of the New Deal was precisely that it micromanaged the economy through price controls, industry cartels, and agricultural restrictions—interventions that "disrupted the market economy" far more than monetary and fiscal policy ever could.

"What Keynes was arguing was don't do any of that stuff. Just get the monetary and fiscal levers right, so to speak. Just get spending where it needs to be."

Modern Keynesianism, properly understood, isn't about extensive government intervention in economic decisions—it's about maintaining macroeconomic conditions where private investment and spending can flourish, while avoiding the policy errors of the 1930s that transformed a severe recession into a decade-long catastrophe.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The Great Depression was a perfect storm**, requiring simultaneous collapse of the international gold standard, failure of thousands of American banks, and counterproductive policy responses—tariffs alone could not have caused such a catastrophe.

2. **Private investment ended the Depression**, not government programs or war spending. Post-WWII certainty and business-friendly conditions enabled an investment boom that permanently replaced wartime government demand.

3. **Regime uncertainty suppresses growth**. Today's muted hiring despite low unemployment reflects business hesitation about policy trajectories. Stability and predictability matter more than any specific policy choice.

4. **Supply-shock inflation cannot be solved by monetary tightening**. Oil-driven price increases reflect real scarcity; Fed tightening merely adds recession to scarcity without addressing the underlying problem.

5. **Government should stabilize, not micromanage**. The lesson from both the Great Depression and modern crises is that broad monetary and fiscal stimulus during demand collapses beats detailed intervention in specific markets and prices.
