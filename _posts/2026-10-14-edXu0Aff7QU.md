**Subtitle:** A deep dive into the end of the semiconductor commodity cycle, the rise of defensive sectors, and the hidden bull market in the "Unmagnificent 493."

In a recent interview, Thomas Hayes, managing member of Great Hill Capital and author of *Hedge Fund Tips*, delivered a stark warning to investors: the "commodity trade" within the broader AI narrative is finished. While the transformative potential of artificial intelligence remains intact, Hayes argues that the speculative frenzy surrounding semiconductor stocks—particularly memory and commodity chips—has reached its peak.

With the US 10-year Treasury yield hovering around 5.2%, Hayes identifies a critical inflection point in the market. As capital flows out of crowded tech plays and into bonds, utilities, and defensive equities, investors face a bifurcated market where major indices may stagnate while significant opportunities emerge in overlooked sectors.

## The Semiconductor Cycle: High Prices Cure High Prices

Hayes’ primary thesis rests on a timeless economic principle: "High prices cure high prices." For the past year, the market narrative suggested that AI required specialized GPUs, fueling a monopoly narrative around Nvidia and a boom in memory stocks like Micron and SanDisk. However, Hayes points out that history repeats itself in the semiconductor industry every four years.

The cycle is predictable:
1.  **Shortage:** Demand outstrips supply, driving prices and margins up.
2.  **Capacity Expansion:** Competitors (including Chinese, Korean, and Japanese manufacturers) invest heavily to flood the market.
3.  **Collapse:** Supply catches up just as demand normalizes, causing stock prices to collapse.

Hayes notes that China, Korea, and Japan are not sitting idly by while US firms enjoy superior margins. They are aggressively ramping up production of memory and commodity semiconductor chips. Reports of rapid technological progress by Chinese rivals have already triggered sell-offs in chip stocks, with Micron dropping 6% in early trading. Hayes argues that the "DeepSeek moment"—where inference costs on CPUs rivaled GPUs—was just the beginning. As open-weight models and more efficient computing methods gain traction, the demand for expensive proprietary hardware may soften, further pressuring the semiconductor commodity trade.

## The Bond Rotation and Pension Pressure

A major driver of the current rotation is the attractiveness of fixed income. With the US 10-year Treasury yield at approximately 5.2%, it is now competing directly with equities. Hayes highlights a crucial calculation for institutional investors: the S&P 500’s earnings yield is roughly 5% (based on a P/E of 20), but this yield is not guaranteed. In contrast, a 10-year bond offers a guaranteed 5.2% return.

This dynamic is particularly compelling for pension funds, which often have 6% discount rates they must meet to remain solvent. For these managers, locking in a guaranteed yield reduces the risk of underperforming their liabilities. Hayes explains that pension managers face asymmetric risk: if they take risks in equities and lose, they are fired; if they lock in guaranteed returns, they secure their jobs. Consequently, there is a material bid for bonds, utilities, staples, and healthcare, as large institutions reallocate away from the volatile equity market.

## The "Unmagnificent 493" and the Hidden Bull Market

Hayes draws a parallel to the dot-com bubble of 1999-2000. During that period, the indices held steady due to the massive gains of a few tech giants, while 54% of S&P 500 stocks were actually in a bear market. He predicts a "monstrous bull market" will occur "under the surface" over the next 6 to 12 months, driven by the "Unmagnificent 493" (the S&P 500 minus the top tech winners).

As yields stabilize or fall, sectors like consumer discretionary, small caps, and real estate could experience explosive growth. Hayes cites the Russell 2000, which has underperformed significantly but is poised for a rebound as it acts like a "coiled spring" held underwater. With $8 trillion sitting in money market funds, there is ample fuel for a broader rally once the momentum shifts away from mega-cap tech.

Hayes views **Alibaba** as the cheapest AI play globally. The company owns equity in numerous AI startups in Asia, including a significant stake in "Moonshot," which is reportedly preparing for a $50 billion IPO. This equity value is not fully priced into Alibaba’s stock. Additionally, Alibaba serves as a toll-taker on the recovery of the Chinese middle class and is growing its AI revenue by triple digits per quarter.

However, Hayes notes that Starlink Mobile cannot effectively serve indoor customers without cell towers. With Musk’s spectrum purchases confirming the need for terrestrial infrastructure, Crown Castle’s value is being repriced upward.

## The AI Beneficiaries vs. Capex Spenders

Hayes distinguishes sharply between the "capex spenders" (those building the AI infrastructure) and the "beneficiaries" (those using AI to increase productivity and margins). Historically, the companies investing in new technologies are often the losers, while those implementing them to cut costs are the winners.

He cites **GXO Logistics** as an example of a beneficiary, using robotics and AI to dramatically increase margins. Similarly, he points to **Jeff Bezos’** strategy of investing in industrial and manufacturing companies that will leverage AI, rather than funding more AI startups. Hayes warns that the market is currently chasing the wrong side of the trade—the commodity hardware providers—while undervaluing the companies that will actually deploy the technology to improve their bottom lines.

## Conclusion

The current market environment is characterized by a "Great Rotation." The AI trade is not over, but the *commodity* aspect of it—semiconductors and memory—is rolling over. Investors who chase the high-flying tech stocks may face significant downside, while those who position themselves in defensive sectors, international value plays, and AI beneficiaries with protected downsides are likely to outperform.

As Hayes puts it, "Beta might be negative" in the crowded tech sector, but "alpha" is abundant in the neglected corners of the market, from small caps to value stocks. The key is to insulate oneself from the potential unwind of the semiconductor bubble while capturing the long-term productivity gains AI will deliver to the broader economy.
