## Introduction

On Friday, August 28th, global markets experienced a sharp sell-off following a speech by Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh at the Jackson Hole Symposium. Risk assets, including gold, Bitcoin, and major equity indices, declined significantly as investors reacted to a more hawkish tone than anticipated. This article explores the drivers behind the market volatility, the Federal Reserve's stance on monetary policy, the Treasury Department's intervention in bond markets, and the implications for corporate credit and long-term yields.

## The Jackson Hole Shock

The immediate trigger for the market downturn was Kevin Warsh's address at the Jackson Hole Symposium. While Warsh acknowledged that recent Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) and Consumer Price Index (CPI) readings were better than expected, he emphasized that inflation has remained high for 65 months. He argued that the underlying trends have not shifted sufficiently to warrant a change in policy stance, noting that a significant percentage of PCE sub-indices remain above 3%.

This hawkish tilt spooked markets, leading to a belief that a Federal Reserve rate hike in September was more likely than previously thought. Consequently, gold dropped by 3.5%, Bitcoin fell by 3%, and the S&P 500 and NASDAQ moved into negative territory. Treasury yields also rose, with both the 10-year and 30-year yields climbing.

## Monetary Policy Outlook

Colin Martin, Head of Fixed Income Research and Strategy at Charles Schwab, noted that while Warsh's speech was hawkish, it did not necessarily signal an immediate rate hike. The Federal Reserve is expected to remain on hold for now, a low-conviction call given the current inflation data. However, several committee members, including dissenter Jeffrey Schmid, favor hikes. The key variable will be the August CPI report; if it comes in hot, it could move the needle toward a September hike.

Prediction markets, such as Koshi, reflect this uncertainty. Traders are pricing in a 69% chance of a rate hike before 2027 and a 77% chance before July 2027. Despite political pressure from the administration, Martin believes Warsh will follow his own compass, though he is more inclined to hold rates steady than to hike aggressively.

## Treasury Intervention and Yield Curve Dynamics

The rise in long-term yields has prompted the Treasury Department to intervene. On August 19th, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced a doubling of the bond buyback program from $2 billion to $4 billion per operation. This program, originally introduced under Janet Yellen, is designed to manage liquidity by buying back older, less liquid Treasury securities and funding them with Treasury bills.

While Bessent has drawn a line in the sand at just under 5% for the 10-year yield, Martin argues that current yields are consistent with economic fundamentals. With the Fed funds rate near neutral and the economy resilient, a positively sloped yield curve is expected. The 10-year Treasury is likely to hold in the 4.25% to 4.75% range, with 5% acting as a psychological ceiling. The Treasury's buybacks, while significant, are small relative to the $32 trillion marketable debt and may not fundamentally alter the yield curve's trajectory.

## Corporate Credit and Inflation Protection

Warsh also highlighted that corporate credit spreads are near historical lows, indicating strong corporate fundamentals. Investment-grade corporate bonds offer yields of 5% or more, while high-yield bonds offer 7% to 7.5%. However, there are risks associated with the massive debt issuance by hyperscalers and tech companies funding AI buildouts. These issuers, which historically had high credit ratings, are now issuing large volumes of long-term debt, potentially widening spreads due to supply-demand dynamics and uncertainty over long-term returns.

For investors concerned about inflation, Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) are an attractive option. With 10-year TIPS yields between 2.25% and 2.5%, they offer real yields that outperform the annual change in the CPI. TIPS are currently underowned by many investors and provide direct protection against inflation, making them a valuable component of a diversified portfolio.

## Conclusion

The market reaction to Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech underscores the sensitivity of financial markets to Federal Reserve communications. While the immediate outlook suggests a hold on rates, the potential for future hikes remains a key risk. The Treasury's intervention in bond markets is a manageable tool for liquidity but may not fully address the structural drivers of rising long-term yields. Investors should remain cautious, diversify their credit exposure, and consider inflation-protected assets to navigate this environment of elevated inflation and fiscal uncertainty.
