In a striking assessment of U.S.-Iran relations, one analyst argues that American intervention has inadvertently unified Iranians under a banner of nationalism—delaying the very political change Washington may have hoped to encourage.

The core claim is provocative: had the United States refrained from involving itself in Iran’s internal affairs and allowed events to unfold organically, the country might have seen a new government emerge “much sooner.” Instead, external pressure has forged a collective identity centered on resistance.

“Having attacked it, everyone in the country, whether they like the regime in Tehran or not, is a nationalist,” the analysis states. “They are all determined to defend their country. They are determined to defeat us.”

This sentiment underscores a critical unintended consequence: by applying pressure, the U.S. has “forced cohesion on Iran and Iranian society.” In the face of external threats, internal divisions are often set aside, rallying citizens around the defense of national sovereignty.

Yet the commentary does not rule out change entirely. There is a prediction that “probably by Christmas next year or maybe the spring of next year, we will see change in Iran that we will probably welcome from purely from the standpoint of what makes sense socially, culturally, politically.”

However, this anticipated shift will not translate into friendship. The analyst concludes soberly: “We’re not going to have a friendly regime there. Not for a very, very long time. And that’s tragic.”

The takeaway is a lesson in the paradox of foreign intervention: actions meant to destabilize a regime can instead solidify its popular support, breeding lasting resentment and postponing the possibility of genuine diplomatic rapport. In seeking to accelerate change, the U.S. may have ensured a longer, colder peace.
