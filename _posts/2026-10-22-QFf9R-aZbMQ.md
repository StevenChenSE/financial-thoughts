The conflict involving Iran has escalated to a critical juncture, marked by burning tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, Houthi missile attacks on Saudi Arabia, and increased NATO involvement. As Washington weighs a major military escalation—including the potential use of the "Mother of All Bombs" (MOAB)—global markets face uncertainty over oil prices and supply chain stability. To understand the geopolitical and military implications, we spoke with Hal Kempfer, a retired Lieutenant Colonel with 24 years of experience as a Marine intelligence officer and CEO of Global Risk Intelligence and Planning.

## The Strait of Hormuz: Supply Returns but Risks Remain

Despite President Trump’s claims that the US has "won" the Iran war, market data presents a nuanced picture. According to shipping data from maritime intelligence firm Kepler, Middle East crude oil exports rose above pre-war levels in late September, reaching between 19.5 million and 22.5 million barrels per day. This surge occurred despite continued attacks on commercial vessels around the Strait of Hormuz.

Kempfer attributes this resilience to US Navy operations. Initially providing guidance, the Navy now actively escorts vessels through a new southerly route hugging the coast of Oman. The US has employed clever tactics, such as using ships with smaller radar signatures and conducting ship-to-ship transfers south of the strait. While 97% of vessels pass safely, approximately 3% are targeted by Iranian forces.

**Why Prices Haven't Dropped:**
Although volume is high, the risk premium remains baked into oil prices due to elevated maritime insurance costs. For prices to return to pre-war levels, the threat of missiles hitting tankers must cease entirely. Kempfer notes that Iran possesses a vast arsenal of missiles and drones launched from numerous locations, making it difficult to neutralize the threat completely without significant ground operations or advanced interception systems.

## Military Escalation: The MOAB and Underground Targets

Reports indicate the US military has developed plans potentially involving the GBU-43/B Massive Ordnance Air Blast (MOAB), the largest non-nuclear bomb in the US arsenal. Kempfer explains that while the MOAB is a massive ordnance penetration weapon, its announcement serves a significant psychological and political purpose.

**Potential Targets:**
*   **Pickaxe Mountain:** Suspected underground nuclear facilities.
*   **Missile and Drone Cities:** Underground storage complexes used by Iran to house ballistic missiles and drones. The MOAB could effectively destroy tunnel entrances and exits, trapping these assets.

Kempfer emphasizes that the US does not need to cross the nuclear threshold. Modern non-nuclear ordinances have advanced enough in the last two decades to achieve effects previously requiring tactical nuclear weapons. However, he argues that a full-scale ground invasion is not currently necessary or feasible due to Iran's geography and dispersed missile launch sites.

## Regional Escalation: The Houthis and NATO Involvement

The conflict has expanded beyond US-Iran dynamics. Houthi forces in Yemen have intensified attacks on Saudi Arabia, including strikes on Riyadh airport. In response, the UK is deploying Typhoon fighter jets to provide defensive support against drones and ballistic missiles.

**Strategic Shift:**
Kempfer suggests this deployment may free up Saudi aircraft to conduct offensive operations within Yemen. Additionally, British military advisers are expected to work closely with Saudi forces. Kempfer predicts that Saudi Arabia may eventually put "boots on the ground" in Yemen, potentially leading to the liberation of Sana'a from Houthi control.

The Bab el-Mandeb Strait, another critical chokepoint for European and Asian oil flows, remains under threat. However, anti-Houthi militia forces aligned with the internationally recognized government are reclaiming coastal areas, limiting the Houthis' ability to target vessels accurately. Kempfer believes this strait will likely return to near-normal traffic levels as Houthi capabilities degrade.

## Russia, Ukraine, and the Diesel Deal

A separate but connected geopolitical development involves the lifting of US bans on Russian diesel exports. This move has caused significant friction with European allies and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky. Kempfer notes that while Trump seeks a high-profile summit with Putin and Chinese leader Xi Jinping to resolve the Ukraine war, such talks are unlikely to yield a deal without Ukrainian participation.

**Ukraine’s Asymmetric Response:**
In response to Russian infrastructure strikes, Ukraine has launched massive drone waves—sometimes exceeding 1,000 drones—against Russian refineries, data centers, and logistics hubs. These attacks exploit gaps in Russia’s air defense, which relies heavily on expensive surface-to-air missiles (S-300/S-400 series) that are cost-ineffective against cheap FPV drones.

Kempfer highlights the paradox of the current US stance: supporting Ukraine with intelligence to hit Russian refineries while simultaneously facilitating Russian diesel exports for domestic price control. He warns that political forces in Washington may eventually reverse this "diesel deal" due to bipartisan opposition and alliance tensions.

## Supply Chain Vulnerabilities: China’s Critical Minerals Lock

A critical deadline looms on November 27, when China’s temporary suspension of its ban on exporting antimony and other critical minerals expires. These minerals are essential for ammunition production.

**China’s Strategic Leverage:**
Kempfer explains that China dominates the processing of rare earths not because they lack deposits elsewhere, but because they subsidized their industry to undercut global competition. The US is now scrambling to re-establish mining and processing capabilities, a process that takes years. Until then, the US must mend relations with allies like Canada and Australia to secure alternative supply chains for lithium and other minerals.

## Future Warfare: Drones vs. Tanks

The war in Ukraine has demonstrated the efficacy of First-Person View (FPV) drones against heavy armor, creating "drone dead zones" where movement is nearly impossible. However, Kempfer argues that tanks are not obsolete. Armies are adapting by integrating laser defense systems onto tanks and using armored vehicles as platforms for launching interceptor drones.

## Conclusion: The Path to Lower Oil Prices

Kempfer concludes that oil prices will eventually return to pre-war levels, but this depends on the stabilization of security risks in both the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab el-Mandeb. As Iran’s ability to strike shipping diminishes—potentially through the deployment of directed-energy weapons (lasers) and interceptor drones currently being tested in Ukraine—the risk premium will drop.

Until then, markets must weigh the cost of continued kinetic escalation against the diplomatic off-ramps that remain stalled by political timelines and regional leverage plays.
