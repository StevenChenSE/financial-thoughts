**April 6th, 2026** – In a recent episode of Capital Cause, host Danny welcomed renowned precious metals expert David Morgan to discuss the escalating global tensions, particularly the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, and its profound implications for oil, metals, and the broader financial system. Morgan painted a stark picture of an economy teetering on the edge, driven by energy and the potential for unprecedented societal shifts.

### The Critical Chokepoint: Strait of Hormuz and Oil's Dominance

Morgan wasted no time in highlighting the gravity of the situation, emphasizing the Strait of Hormuz as a critical chokepoint. "You're hitting the nail on the head," Morgan affirmed, "When you cut off 20% of the world's oil supply going through these straits and you realize how important oil is to not only transportation but also the entire food industry... we're going to start to see an acceleration in the distortions between the have and have nots."

He underscored that oil's importance extends far beyond just moving goods. It's intrinsically linked to the food supply, from nitrogen input for fertilizers and natural gas, to diesel for plowing fields and petroleum-based crop spraying. With global food reserves already strained, a prolonged closure could trigger a severe food shortage, exacerbating existing water crises in regions like Iran.

### Metals' Unexpected Calm Amidst the Storm

Host Danny observed that despite the escalating conflict in Iran and the Strait of Hormuz situation, metals prices had largely moved sideways, even slightly downward. This seemingly counterintuitive reaction was a point of discussion.

Morgan acknowledged the surprise, referencing other market commentators like Michael Oliver and Martin Armstrong, who often note that war doesn't always move metals predictably.

> "The market does what it does and putting a why on it is often intent and there's so many intentions... the market is an accumulation of a bunch of votes for different reasons," Morgan explained. "Somebody bought gold because they fear inflation. Somebody bought gold because of a crisis head. Somebody bought gold because of war. Somebody sold it because of war. So there that's the market."

He reiterated a fundamental principle:
> "Oil is the king of all commodities as we all know. Energy is more important than the metals and that's what's taking reaction."

Drawing a historical parallel to the 1980 Afghan war, which provided the "last oomph" to the market before a significant sell-off in gold and silver, Morgan suggested that currently, oil is the primary driver, not metals. He anticipates a period of consolidation for metals: "My personal view is six weeks sideways, probably between 70 maybe up to 90 on silver, maybe more like 70 to 82, and then gold the same type of thing consolidation and then we're going to break out most likely to the upside and you'll probably see oil prices continue higher."

### The Shadow of Depression and the Invisible Crisis

The potential for a prolonged closure of the Strait of Hormuz led Morgan to a grim prediction.

> "If this stays closed, we're going to have so many more problems that all revolve around oil use... we are going to have unfortunate and I will say the d-word, a depression."

He clarified that this depression might not be as visibly jarring as the 1930s, thanks to modern assistance programs like EBT cards, which allow people to buy groceries with less overt struggle. "It's really not that visible, but nonetheless, it exists and is growing," he warned.

### Echoes of the Past: Iraq War and the Gold-Oil Link

Danny presented a chart of gold's performance during the Iraq War in February-March 2003, showing an initial sell-off for about three months before bottoming out. This historical analog resonated with Morgan's current outlook.

> "I'm getting excited because I love to verify... it does confirm it really for my people. I mean, in the private work I said, you know, I think it's a minimum of six weeks and I think it could go three months."

Morgan elaborated on the "scare you out or wear you out" nature of precious metals markets, especially silver. Extended sideways trading periods can exhaust investors, leading them to exit for other, more volatile assets like tech stocks or AI plays. "The functional bull markets shake people off," he noted.

Danny then overlaid oil prices onto the Iraq War chart, revealing a strong correlation where gold and oil tracked each other from 2003 to 2008. This suggests that if oil prices continue to rise, gold should eventually follow suit once the current consolidation subsides. Morgan concurred, estimating a potential "peak up in the metals" around early June, but cautioned against trading solely on this opinion.

### The Yuan's Emergence and Gold's Enduring Role

The conversation shifted to the geopolitical landscape, specifically the emergence of a "pro-yuan" toll for passage through the Strait of Hormuz, amounting to $1 per barrel or roughly $2 million per ship. Danny questioned if this shift of capital flow from the dollar to the yuan could reprice metals in dollar terms.

Morgan acknowledged the potential impact, referencing Jim Rickards' view that while a BRICS currency is unlikely due to the absence of a robust bond market, the BRICS nations already possess gold. He agreed that a long-term requirement to pay in gold for oil passage would have a significant, albeit subtle, impact.

> "Look, we know that the markets usually look ahead. The central banks were looking ahead to gold a couple years ago. I was talking about... the run to gold has begun and everyone's like the run to go 2000, you know, and of course it was, you know, 2,000's a good price, but contrast that to the 5500 we've had recently."

He described the central banks' accumulation as a "walk, a brisk walk, a jog, a run, an all-out sprint," suggesting we are "far from an all-out sprint, but not that far." The underlying question, Morgan posited, is whether the new global financial system will still be controlled by the Anglo-American Empire or if BRICS nations will have a more equitable voice.

### Silver's Time to Shine and the "FOMO" Top

Regarding silver, Morgan expects it to "come along for the ride until we're in the final phase." He anticipates a gold-silver ratio below 30:1 (currently around 50:1) before silver truly outperforms. The ultimate top for the metals market, he believes, will be signaled by widespread public "FOMO" (Fear Of Missing Out).

> "The psychology is if I don't own some precious metals, I am in big trouble... People that buy that have never bought before. People buy that used to call us tinfoil hack conspiracy theorists. Those people come into the market and rush in in a short duration of time. That will be the top and that will be the signal to probably get out and move into a different asset class."

### The Yen Carry Trade's Demise and Global Liquidity

A concerning development discussed was the Japanese bond market, with 10-year and 30-year yields reaching new highs (3.75% for the 30-year). Morgan declared the "yen carry trade" – where investors borrowed yen at near-zero interest rates to invest in higher-yielding assets – "over. It's done. It's kaput."

The consequence is that yields must continue to rise to attract buyers, causing existing bond prices to plummet. This situation carries significant counterparty risk, reminiscent of the Lehman Brothers or Bear Stearns failures of 2008.

> "When you're looking at these great big money center banks or large brokerage houses... and they cannot make good on their promise... we've got trouble. And when that happened in 2008, we were on the precise of going down substantially... We were probably days away from having a real seizure of the market, meaning things would freeze up."

Morgan believes we are in an "endgame," where the system will either revert to honest money (unlikely this time) or a new system will be implemented, potentially after a major crisis, when public fear would lead to acceptance of almost any solution.

### US 10-Year Yield Breakout and the Dollar's Fate

Danny presented a chart showing the US 10-year yield breaking above a multi-year resistance line, signaling a potential sustained rise in yields. Morgan agreed, stating that the market now holds more sway than the Federal Reserve or political rhetoric.

With national debt pushing towards $40 trillion, Morgan warned, "the currency usually is the one that takes the brunt of the problem, which means that the currency starts to accelerate its depreciation." He cited the 1980 film "Rollover," depicting a scenario where Arab nations simultaneously exited the US dollar, causing rapid market shifts.

### Preparing for the Unforeseen

Throughout the discussion, Morgan consistently advocated for preparedness.

> "You need metal. Everyone does. But how much? That's the big question. And the answer is enough to where you sleep well at night and not so much that you're worried about the movement of price all the time."

His rule of thumb: 10% of one's portfolio in "hard metal," not mining shares. He cautioned against being "naked short" on metals, referencing historical examples like Venezuela, Turkey, and Argentina, where physical metal became the only reliable asset.

> "Could be a day where that metal is the only thing that you've got that's worth anything for a small perhaps time duration... You've got to be open-minded and you've got to prepare. And if you prepare and you don't need it, great... but that's the world I see ahead, unfortunately."

David Morgan's insights paint a picture of a global financial system under immense strain, where energy, geopolitical shifts, and the integrity of currencies are converging to create an unpredictable future. While the immediate reaction of metals may be subdued, the underlying forces suggest a period of significant change, making preparedness a paramount concern for investors.
