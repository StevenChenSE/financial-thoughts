In November 2024, Boris Churny, head of Claude Code at Anthropic, wrote his last line of code by hand. Since then, 100% of his code has been generated by AI—anywhere from 10 to 40 pull requests shipped daily, all reviewed and authored by Claude. "I have never enjoyed coding as much as I do today," Churny says, "because I don't have to deal with all the minutia."

This isn't a glimpse of some distant future. It's the present reality for one of the most influential figures in AI-assisted development, and according to Churny, it represents only the beginning of a transformation that will fundamentally reshape what it means to build software.

## The Numbers Behind the Revolution

The scale of AI's invasion into software development is staggering. According to research by SemiAnalysis, **4% of all GitHub commits** are now authored by Claude Code. Their prediction: by the end of 2025, that figure will reach **one-fifth of all code commits worldwide**.

When Spotify recently announced that their best developers hadn't written a line of code since December, it wasn't a headline about the future—it was a report on the present. The most senior, most experienced engineers at the world's most sophisticated technology companies are stepping back from code entirely.

"The day that we're recording this, Spotify just put out this headline that their best developers haven't written a line of code since December, thanks to AI," Churny notes during the conversation. "More and more of the most advanced senior engineers, including you, are sharing the fact that they don't write code anymore."

What makes these statistics even more remarkable is their trajectory. Claude Code's growth isn't just upward—it's accelerating. In just the past month, daily active users have doubled. And these numbers only capture public commits. Churny estimates that **private repository usage is "quite a bit higher"** than the public figures suggest.

## The Origin Story: From Hack to Phenomenon

The Claude Code that now powers millions of code commits started as what Churny calls "just a little hack." When he joined Anthropic after working in Big Tech, he spent his first month building "a bunch of weird prototypes" to understand the boundaries of what the models could do.

Then came a crucial month learning the research side through post-training. "For me as an engineer, I find that to do good work, you really have to understand the layer under the layer at which you work," Churny explains. "If you're working in AI, you just really have to understand the model to some degree to do good work."

The breakthrough came unexpectedly. During an early prototype demonstration, Churny gave Claude a bash tool and asked what music he was listening to. The model figured out how to use the tool to answer—a task Churny hadn't explicitly instructed it to handle.

"The model was given this tool, and it figured out how to use it to answer this question that I had that I wasn't even sure if it could answer," he recalls. "I didn't instruct the model to say, 'you know, use this tool for this.' The model just was given this tool, and it figured out how to use it."

The initial launch in February wasn't an immediate hit. "Claude Code was not initially a hit," Churny admits. "When we released it, it got a bunch of users, there was a lot of early adopters that got it immediately. But it actually took many months for everyone to really understand what this thing is." The terminal-based interface felt alien to developers accustomed to sophisticated IDEs. Only when Claude Code expanded to mobile apps, desktop applications, IDE extensions, and integrations with Slack and GitHub did adoption truly accelerate.

The team deliberately maintained the terminal form factor longer than expected. "The biggest reason was the model is improving so quickly," Churny explains. "We felt that there wasn't really another form factor that could keep up with it."

## The Cursor Detour: Why Mission Matters

Six months before this podcast was recorded, Churny made what he calls "the fastest job change" of his career. He joined Cursor—the AI coding tool he still describes as "awesome" and built by a team he considers "really impressive"—before returning to Anthropic just two weeks later.

"I joined Cursor because I'm a big fan of the product," Churny says. "I met the team, and I was just really impressed. They're an awesome team. They saw where AI coding was going, I think, before a lot of people did."

So why leave? "As soon as I got there, what I started to realize is what I really missed about Anthropic was the mission," Churny explains. "The thing that drew me to Anthropic was the mission. And it's all about safety. When you talk to people at Anthropic, just find someone in the hallway—if you ask them why they're here, the answer is always going to be safety."

For Churny, mission-driven work isn't optional. "I just know personally it's something I need in order to be happy," he says. "Whatever the work might be, no matter how exciting, even if it's building a really cool product, it's just not really a substitute for that."

This mission alignment proved especially relevant given Claude Code's trajectory. The tool isn't just writing code anymore—it's beginning to act in the world, using Gmail, Slack, and other tools autonomously. This capability is precisely why safety becomes increasingly critical as the technology advances.

## The Transformation of the Software Engineer

Churny's prediction is stark: "The title of software engineer is gonna start to go away. It's just gonna be replaced by builder, and it's gonna be painful for a lot of people."

The data supports his concern. Productivity per engineer has increased **200%** according to those already deeply integrated into AI coding workflows. Product managers—who Churny describes as "probably sweating" as they listen—are about to find the landscape shifting beneath them even more dramatically.

"I think by the end of the year, everyone's gonna be a product manager and everyone codes," Churny predicts. This doesn't mean technical skills become irrelevant. Rather, the nature of technical work shifts from syntax and implementation to intent, review, and direction.

Looking at his own workflow, Churny still reviews code carefully. "I don't think we're at the point where you can be totally hands-off, especially when there's a lot of people running the program," he explains. "You have to make sure that it's correct. You have to make sure it's safe."

The human role is evolving from writer to architect, reviewer, and evaluator. At Anthropic, Claude reviews 100% of pull requests automatically, but human review still plays a crucial role in the pipeline. The key distinction Churny makes is between production code requiring careful oversight and "pure prototype code that isn't going to run anywhere."

## What's Next: AI That Thinks and Acts

The frontier is no longer code generation—it's code conception. "Claude is starting to come up with ideas," Churny describes. "Looking through feedback, it's looking at bug reports, it's looking at telemetry for bug fixes and things to ship—a little more like a co-worker."

This represents a fundamental shift in the human-AI dynamic. Previously, AI tools responded to human instructions. Now, they're beginning to identify opportunities humans might miss, proactively suggesting improvements based on accumulated context.

For Churny, this evolution traces a clear path: "It's coding, then it's tool use, then it's computer use." The progression that began with Claude Code writing code has expanded to AI using tools effectively. The next phase—already visible in products like Computer Use—is AI acting autonomously in digital environments.

"For a lot of people that use conversational AI, this might be the first time that they're using the thing that actually acts," Churny observes. "It can use your Gmail. It can use your Slack. You can do all these things for you. And it's quite good at it. And it's only going to get better from here."

## Key Takeaways

- **AI already writes 100% of code for leading practitioners**: Senior engineers at top companies have stopped writing code manually, with some shipping 10-40+ pull requests daily through AI collaboration.

- **The growth trajectory is exponential, not linear**: With 4% of GitHub commits now AI-authored and predicted to reach 20% by year-end, adoption is accelerating faster than most predicted.

- **Coding is becoming a solved problem**: Churny predicts that within a year or two, whether someone "knows how to code" won't meaningfully differentiate their ability to build software.

- **The software engineer title is evolving**: The role is shifting from writing code to directing, reviewing, and architecting—increasingly described as "builder" rather than engineer.

- **The next frontier is AI-generated ideas**: Claude is beginning to proactively identify bugs, suggest features, and find improvements based on feedback and telemetry, not just execute human instructions.

The revolution in software engineering isn't coming—it's here. And as Churny's own journey demonstrates, adapting to this new reality requires not just new tools, but new mental models about what it means to build.
