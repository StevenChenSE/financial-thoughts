## Joint intervention targets 40-year low to prevent global market contagion

Last Friday, July 31st, the United States government bought Japanese yen for the first time since the 1998 Asian financial crisis. This unprecedented move was a direct response to the Japanese finance ministry, which had sold foreign currency from national reserves the day prior to support the rapidly weakening yen. Each purchase was designed to slow the currency's collapse. Japan's finance ministry confirmed the coordination on Monday, stating it acted pursuant to the US-Japan finance ministers' joint statement issued in September 2025 against excessive volatility and disorderly movements. That statement also commits both governments to disclose their interventions monthly.

The yen had fallen to near 164 to the dollar, its weakest level in 40 years, before rebounding to around 157 by Friday. On CNBC earlier this week, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent explained the rationale behind this historic move. In his view, the overly weak yen partly triggered the Asian financial crisis of the late '90s. He argued that a stable yen is vital not only for the US but for the entire region, as a substantial weakening could drag down other currencies like the Korean won and complicate perceptions of the Chinese RMB. "We are proud to stand with them and implementing their policies, and help them stabilize the region," Bessent said.

President Trump echoed this sentiment, citing a strong financial relationship with Japan and noting that a stable yen is beneficial for the world economy. However, a crucial detail often overlooked by media outlets is that the New York Fed, acting for the Treasury, did not sell dollars to buy yen. Instead, it sold euros. The goal was to lift the yen without signaling that Washington wants a weaker dollar. This twist drew criticism; former Treasury official Edwin Truman called the move "weird," while Brookings Institute economist Robin Brooks argued it undercuts the efficacy of US participation.

While official figures remain unconfirmed, a Reuters photographer captured Treasury Secretary Yellen's to-do list at Camp David last week, which read: "Buy Japanese yen, 5 to 10 billion dollars." This figure fueled weekend speculation.

### Why the Yen Has Been Tanking
To understand why this bailout occurred, one must look at the yen's five-year slide. In 2021, the yen averaged about 110 to the dollar; last month, it touched 164. The standard explanation is interest rate differentials. In 2021, the American 10-year Treasury paid 1.45%, while the Japanese 10-year paid 0.07%. By 2023, the US yield nearly tripled to 3.96%, while Japan's remained capped at 0.57% by the Bank of Japan (BOJ). This gap fueled the "carry trade," peaking at 3.5 points in 2023.

Although the gap has since closed to around two points, the yen continued to fall. The IMF noted in February that the yen had consistently decoupled from the US-Japan yield differential since mid-2025. Meanwhile, Japanese yields are creeping higher: the 40-year bond exceeded 4% in May for the first time, and the 10-year hit 2.8% in July, its highest since 1997.

Fiscal risk and energy costs are also driving weakness. Deficits are widening; in June, the government passed a supplementary budget of over 3 trillion yen funded by debt issuance. On July 30th, it revised its debt forecast upward to 187.6% of GDP for this fiscal year. Additionally, following US and Israeli strikes on Iran in late February, Brent crude spiked from $71 to $138 a barrel. As an energy-importing nation, Japan's petroleum import bill rose 59% year-on-year by June. Furthermore, Japanese households are sending money abroad via tax-free NISA accounts, with net inflows into retail investment trusts topping 6 trillion yen in the first quarter alone.

Steve Hanke, Professor of Applied Economics at Johns Hopkins University, argues that this intervention amounts to market manipulation. "Fundamentals point to more yen weakness," Hanke said. "When the big player comes in to the picture... they stop looking at fundamentals."

### Japan’s Structural Constraints
Japan cannot simply raise rates to fix its currency due to deep structural issues. Japan is the world's fourth-largest economy but has the most indebted government. Gross government general debt stands at about 207% of GDP. The Bank of Japan owns roughly 48% of these bonds, though this share has been falling as the central bank tapers its holdings.

Demographics pose the ultimate constraint. Last year, Japan recorded 706,000 births against 1.59 million deaths, a natural decline of nearly 900,000 people. Nearly three in ten Japanese are over 65, and the working-age population has shrunk from 87 million in 1995 to 73 million today. This aging, high-saving society holds 2,386 trillion yen in household financial assets, 47% of which sits in cash earning almost nothing. While the BOJ links aging to lower interest rates, some economists argue it should push rates up.

### The Danger of the Yen Carry Trade Unwind
The Japanese yen carry trade is significant for US markets. Investors borrow in low-yielding yen to invest in higher-yielding assets like US Treasuries. This strategy only works while exchange rates remain stable. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) describes these trades as "picking up nickels in front of a steamroller." Between February 1999 and March 2024, the BOJ's policy rate never exceeded 0.5%. That era ended with a rate hike in March 2024.

While the BIS counted $14.2 trillion in yen swaps and forwards in late 2023, only about $160 billion represented speculative positioning. However, estimates of the carry trade size vary, with the BIS placing a middle estimate at $250 billion in August 2024, likely biased low.

An "unwind" occurs when investors sell assets to buy back yen, taking losses if the yen has risen. If the BOJ raises rates, borrowing costs increase, forcing mass sell-offs. This happened on August 5, 2024, when the Nikkei fell 12.4% in a single day and the S&P 500 dropped 3%. Michael Gayed, founder of the Lead-Lag Report, had predicted this in 2023, calling Japan "Mothra" in the global financial system. He warned of a monetary cycle mismatch between the BOJ and other central banks, leading to a squeeze that could cause nasty global reverberations.

Gayed noted a catch-22 for the Federal Reserve: if the Fed lowers rates to combat volatility, it narrows the interest rate differential with Japan, which is yen-supportive and could accelerate the reverse carry trade further. He suspects temporary interventions or swap lines will be announced to cool things off, but warned, "the cat's out of the bag."

### Outlook and Key Dates
Currently, the rate gap is about 2.6 points, half of what it was in 2024. However, carry-to-risk ratios on currencies like the Mexican peso and Brazilian real against the yen peaked earlier this year, signaling rising risk. On July 28th, speculators held a net short of 163,000 yen contracts, 89% of the peak seen before the August 2024 crash.

Spillovers into the US occur through margin calls and risk limits rather than direct wires. Yet, the Federal Reserve's recent financial stability report does not mention the yen, Japan, or the carry trade.

Three key dates to watch are:
1. End of August: Japan publishes its official intervention total.
2. September 17-18: The BOJ meets for its next policy rate decision.
3. Next Treasury holdings release: To see if Japanese investors are returning funds home.

Peter Schiff, chief market strategist of Euro Pacific Asset Management, warns that if Japan acts correctly to shore up the yen, it may sell US Treasuries, causing a crash in US stocks and bonds. Steven Nakasky, CIO of Vermillion Research, suggests the US intervention might be forcing the unwind of the yen carry trade, as current conditions are disadvantageous for the trade.

As markets navigate this volatility, traders must decide whether to buy or sell the yen. Whether this marks the start of another Asian financial crisis remains to be seen, but the interconnection between Japan's currency and global stability has never been more apparent.
