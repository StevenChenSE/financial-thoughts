### Dr. Mark Thornton on gold, oil, interest rates, and the end of dollar dominance

The global monetary system is undergoing a profound transformation. In a recent interview on *Capital Cause*, economist Dr. Mark Thornton, a senior fellow at the Laissez Faire Institute, outlined the structural forces eroding the petrodollar system and reshaping markets. Drawing on Austrian economics and historical monetary trends, Thornton argued that three major developments—unlimited government spending, geopolitical realignments in the Middle East, and the breakout of long-term interest rates—are signaling the end of an era.

## Gold as the Market’s Leading Indicator

Gold has long served as a barometer for monetary health. Thornton noted that gold rallies often precede major inflationary moves, a pattern evident in 2020–2022 and again in the summer of 2026. While short-term fluctuations were influenced by geopolitical events, including tensions with Iran and the nomination of Kevin Walsh, the underlying trend remains intact.

“The macro thesis has been and continues to be still in play here,” Thornton said. “You have the government spending money, borrowing money, and seemingly without limits.”

This relentless expansion of fiscal deficits forces the Federal Reserve to accommodate by increasing the money supply, fueling inflation. Gold, as the “market form of money,” rises in response to this debasement. Thornton emphasized that as long as government debt and money creation continue unchecked, gold is unlikely to experience a sustained downturn.

## The Breakdown of the Petrodollar System

For decades, the petrodollar system ensured global demand for U.S. dollars by requiring oil sales to be conducted in greenbacks. Middle Eastern nations would recycle these dollars into U.S. bonds, real estate, and equities, reinforcing the dollar’s dominance.

That system is now fracturing. Thornton pointed to several developments:

- Oil-exporting nations are no longer required to sell exclusively in dollars.
- Geopolitical shifts, including Iran’s rising influence in the Persian Gulf, have weakened U.S. military and economic leverage in the region.
- A self-defense pact between Pakistan, Turkey, and Saudi Arabia reflects growing regional independence from U.S. security guarantees.

“Now that whole system seems to be breaking down,” Thornton explained. “They’re going to be more reluctant to reinvest all of their extra dollars into U.S. government bonds and real estate and stocks.”

This withdrawal of foreign capital support threatens to depress demand for the dollar, accelerate inflation, and undermine U.S. financial stability.

## Long-Term Interest Rates and the End of Bond Suppression

A third critical sign of systemic stress is the behavior of long-term interest rates. For over 40 years, bond prices rose as rates fell, but that trend has reversed. The 10-year Treasury yield has broken above the 4.5% resistance level, and the 30-year yield has surpassed 5%, despite the Fed’s efforts to suppress borrowing costs.

“This 40-year period in which bond prices rose and long-term interest rates fell is over now,” Thornton said.

The market is signaling that investors no longer trust the Fed to maintain price stability. Rising long-term rates reflect inflation expectations, reduced demand for U.S. debt, and concerns about fiscal sustainability. Thornton warned that while the Fed may cut short-term rates to support equities, the bond market could react negatively, further destabilizing financial conditions.

## Implications for Commodities and Investors

The combination of monetary debasement, geopolitical uncertainty, and rising rates creates a favorable environment for commodities. Thornton highlighted silver, which has benefited from:

- A decade-long supply shortage due to underinvestment in mining.
- Surging industrial demand from AI, electronics, solar power, and electric vehicles.
- The depletion of hoarded silver coins previously held by individuals worldwide.

“Don’t be surprised if the price of silver sets a new record high in inflation-adjusted terms,” Thornton said, noting that such a move could push silver well above its previous nominal peak of $120 per ounce.

Oil, too, is likely to rise as higher financing costs constrain production and supply remains tight. Historically, rising rates and rising commodity prices have coincided during periods of monetary instability, and current conditions mirror those episodes.

## The Path Forward: Education and Sound Money

Thornton concluded by emphasizing the importance of understanding the foundations of monetary policy. He urged investors and citizens to study sound money principles, pointing to free resources such as Murray Rothbard’s *The Case for 100% Gold Reserve Money*.

“Education about real economics is what really matters,” he said. “It keeps you focused and also keeps you calm about all of the chaos that governments are creating around the world.”

As the petrodollar system weakens and inflationary pressures intensify, informed decision-making will be more critical than ever. Gold, silver, and other real assets are likely to play a central role in preserving wealth in the years ahead.
