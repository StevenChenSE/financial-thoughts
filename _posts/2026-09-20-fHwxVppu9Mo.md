## Navigating a Broken Monetary System

In a September 2026 interview with Capital Cosmos host Danny, macroeconomist and investor John Rubino outlines a grim but specific roadmap for the future of global finance. The conversation centers on the "spectacle" of the Federal Reserve attempting to tighten monetary policy while the Treasury Department simultaneously tries to lower long-term interest rates through bond buybacks. Rubino argues that this contradiction is a sign of a monetary system that is fundamentally "breaking down," driven by a US national debt that has surpassed 120% of GDP.

## The Fed vs. The Treasury: A Policy Contradiction

The discussion begins with the recent 25-basis-point interest rate hike. Rubino notes that while the market initially reacted negatively, it quickly retraced its losses. He identifies a "discontinuity" between the short end of the yield curve (controlled by the Fed) and the long end (controlled by the Treasury and bond market sentiment).

Rubino points out that the 10-year Treasury yield dropped to 4.95% despite the rate hike, a move attributed to the Treasury's $4 billion bond buyback program. He describes this as "chaos," where one part of the government is trying to raise rates to combat inflation while another is artificially suppressing yields to manage the massive debt load. For Rubino, this is an "admission of defeat," as the market no longer trusts the management of the dollar.

## The Energy Crisis and the 1970s Parallels

A major portion of the interview is dedicated to the current state of energy markets. With WTI crude at $102 and diesel shortages reported in states like California, Texas, and Florida, Rubino argues that the current environment mirrors the energy crises of the 1970s.

The driver of this volatility is not just supply and demand, but a "military revolution" in the Middle East and Eastern Europe. Cheap drones are overwhelming expensive missile defense systems, allowing groups like the Houthis to attack Saudi Arabian energy infrastructure with impunity. Similarly, Ukraine is using drones to strike oil refineries on the outskirts of Moscow.

Rubino highlights the "crack spread"—the difference between diesel and oil prices—as a critical inflationary driver. Because diesel is used to transport almost all goods, its high cost is being passed down to consumers. The Fed, he argues, has "no power" over these geopolitical supply shocks, making traditional monetary policy tools largely ineffective in the face of a military-driven energy crisis.

## The Frozen Housing Market and the Negative Wealth Effect

Turning to the real estate sector, Rubino describes the US housing market as "frozen." With 30-year mortgage rates at 7.24%, homes are unaffordable for most buyers. He predicts a future where three major groups will flood the market with inventory:

1.  **Boomers:** Older homeowners who can no longer manage large houses.
2.  **Airbnb Investors:** Owners of short-term rental portfolios whose cash flow has dried up and who can no longer service their mortgages.
3.  **Wall Street Landlords:** Private equity firms who may be forced to sell as government regulations tighten and home prices begin to fall.

Rubino warns of a "negative wealth effect," where homeowners who panic-sell at lower prices feel poorer and reduce their spending, further contracting the economy.

## The Bull Case for Precious Metals

Despite the short-term volatility, Rubino presents a strong long-term case for gold and silver. He argues that as the US debt exceeds 120% of GDP, the government will eventually be forced to "inflate away" its obligations. In a currency system that is breaking down, precious metals become the primary store of value.

He sets a target of $15,000 to $20,000 for gold and $200 for silver, noting that even $200 for silver is considered "over-conservative" by some. He advises investors to ignore the "squiggles" of daily price action and focus on the long-term trajectory of the debt-to-GDP ratio and currency creation.

For those looking to gain exposure, Rubino suggests focusing on high-quality mining companies and royalty firms. These companies are currently generating massive free cash flow, paying down debt, and building cash balances that earn interest, making them resilient to short-term energy cost spikes.

John Rubino’s analysis paints a picture of a financial system in late-stage dysfunction. With the Fed and Treasury at odds, energy markets under threat from drone warfare, and the housing market on the brink of a correction, the path to stability is unclear.
