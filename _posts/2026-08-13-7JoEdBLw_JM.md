## Why the Old Keynesian Framework Is Failing

Jim Thorne, Chief Market Strategist at Wellington Altus Private Wealth, argues that the global financial system is entering a fundamentally new era. He is highly bullish on equities, gold, silver, and Bitcoin, while warning that holding cash is a losing strategy. Central to his thesis is the belief that fiat money is being debased at a rate of 8–9% annually based on M2 growth averages since the 1970s, a rate far exceeding official CPI figures. Thorne contends that the Bretton Woods system is over, and investors must navigate a new world where traditional central banking models are no longer sufficient.

## The Case for a Major Equities Bull Market

Despite widespread fears of a recession, Thorne believes the U.S. stock market is in a secular bull market driven by real economic growth and supply-side policies rather than demand-side liquidity. He projects the S&P 500 could reach 10,000 in the near term and potentially 14,000 to 15,000 by the next presidential election. His reasoning is based on expected earnings growth of around $440 per share next year, applying a standard multiple of 22–23x.

Thorne notes that the anticipated four-year market correction cycle has been negated by the significant drawdowns seen in previous years. He views the current economic phase as an escape from secular stagnation, fueled by supply-side policies focused on the real economy, contrasting sharply with the old playbook of asset bubbles and reflation. While he warns the market may be front-end loaded, he believes the earnings growth phase is just beginning.

## The AI Capex Boom and Productivity Growth

A key driver of Thorne’s optimism is the massive capital expenditure (capex) boom in artificial intelligence. He describes AI as a matter of national security that will drive productivity growth. Thorne points out that we are currently in the operating leverage phase of this buildout, with productivity gains yet to fully materialize.

The involvement of major financial institutions like BlackRock, Blackstone, and Apollo in mobilizing hundreds of billions for AI infrastructure signals a long-term commitment. Thorne compares the potential impact to GE Capital under Jack Welch, suggesting that the infrastructure buildout could sustain economic growth into the next decade. However, he cautions that this capex cycle will eventually end, and investors should be mindful of valuations in already parabolic semiconductor names.

## The Japan Risk and the Yen Carry Trade

One of Thorne’s primary concerns is the stability of Japan’s financial system. He warns that a potential crisis in Japan, driven by rising long-end yields and losses in Japanese Government Bonds (JGBs) held by banks and insurers, could trigger a global event akin to the U.S. regional bank crisis.

Thorne highlights the importance of U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent’s intervention in the currency market to support the yen. Given Bessent’s background with Soros Fund Management during the Asian financial crisis, Thorne believes he understands the systemic risks of the yen carry trade unwinding. If the yen were to depreciate further without intervention, Japanese institutions could be forced to sell U.S. Treasuries, causing an extreme steepening of the yield curve and a potential crisis. Thorne views Bessent’s actions as a clear signal that policymakers will not allow yields to rise unchecked.

## Gold, Silver, and the Debasement Trade

Thorne remains highly bullish on precious metals, projecting gold could reach $9,000. He argues that the recent pop in gold prices signals that the long end of the yield curve is being capped, either through yield curve control or other interventions. Thorne emphasizes that Bitcoin, gold, and silver should be seen as part of a “debasement trade” in response to ongoing money creation and debt accumulation.

He notes that while gold has inelastic supply, Bitcoin offers perfect scarcity. Thorne believes that once the narrative catches up to price action, as it did with gold, Bitcoin will experience a significant rally. He advises long-term investors to accumulate these assets rather than chasing parabolic moves in individual stocks.

## Central Banking Errors and the Path Forward

Thorne is critical of current central banking strategies, particularly the misdiagnosis of inflation. He argues that supply shocks, wages, and tariffs are not inherently inflationary in the way central banks have assumed. He believes the Federal Reserve and Bank of Canada have made historic mistakes by raising rates to combat temporary inflationary pressures, thereby damaging interest-rate-sensitive sectors like real estate.

To resolve this, Thorne advocates for lowering interest rates to neutral levels (around 2.75% in the U.S.) to allow the economy to breathe and real estate to stabilize without reigniting inflation. He compares the current situation to an “Alice in Wonderland” scenario where policymakers are using outdated Keynesian models to interpret a supply-side driven economy.

## Canada’s Stagnation and Need for Structural Change

Turning to Canada, Thorne is far less optimistic. He describes the Canadian economy as entering secular stagnation, similar to the U.S. in 2008–2009, hindered by excessive public sector spending and a lack of pro-growth supply-side policies. He criticizes the Canadian government for relying on Keynesian economics while failing to make the country attractive for global capital.

Thorne argues that Canada needs structural adjustment, including better business conditions and a focus on real economic growth beyond energy and public sector expansion. He notes that Canadian banks are trading at historically high valuations despite the weak economic outlook, suggesting a disconnect between markets and reality.

## Final Thoughts: Tactical Allocation in a New Era

In conclusion, Thorne advises investors to be tactically active, rotating capital based on technical charts and fundamental strength. He suggests focusing on high-quality names like Microsoft, Caterpillar, and Palantir, while avoiding overextended parabolic stocks. For those seeking significant upside, Thorne points to the potential doubling of the S&P 500, gold, and select sectors like semiconductors and digital assets.

Ultimately, Thorne’s message is clear: the old playbook is dead. Investors must adapt to a new era defined by supply-side growth, strategic capital allocation, and the ongoing debasement of fiat currency.
