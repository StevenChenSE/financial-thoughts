## Expert Analysis on the US-Iran Conflict, the Strait of Hormuz, and the Path to Diplomacy

In a candid discussion on the ongoing conflict between the United States and Iran, Trita Parsi—co-founder and executive vice president of the Quincy Institute and author of the *Trita Parsi* Substack—breaks down the geopolitical realities on the ground. Addressing questions about control of the Strait of Hormuz, the collapse of recent ceasefire agreements, and the strategic endgames of the US, Israel, and Iran, Parsi argues that military victory is unattainable and that a diplomatic resolution remains the only viable path forward.

### The Illusion of Control in the Strait of Hormuz

A central theme of the discussion is President Trump’s claim on Truth Social that the US has "total control" over the Strait of Hormuz. Parsi categorically rejects this assertion.

According to Parsi, the US is "stuck" and lacks viable military options. While the Trump administration bombed Iran for 13 days, Parsi notes this left the US in a weaker position than before the campaign. Iran continues to block the strait, controlling traffic in and out, while the US enforces its own blockade against some Iranian ships. The result is a significant drop in overall traffic, meaning the core problem Trump aimed to resolve remains unsolved.

While Parsi acknowledges that Iran has suffered tactical defeats—including losses in its navy and air force, the killing of IRGC leadership, and high inflation—he argues these have not translated into strategic US success. Crucially, the IRGC is not fleeing but has actually consolidated greater control over the country.

### How the War "Saved" the Islamic Republic

Parsi offers a provocative historical parallel: much like Saddam Hussein’s 1980 invasion inadvertently consolidated Ayatollah Khomeini’s power during the early stages of the Iranian Revolution, the current war may have "saved" the Islamic Republic.

Facing massive protests in January, the theocracy was in a vulnerable position. The external threat from the US and Israel allowed the regime to clamp down internally and trigger a "rallying around the flag" phenomenon. Parsi warns, however, that this unity is temporary. If the war drags on, the severe economic toll on civilians could shift public perception, leading Iranians to view their own leadership as the obstacle to peace rather than Donald Trump.

### Failed Diplomacy and the Collapse of the MOU

The breakdown of the recent Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) is a critical point of analysis. Parsi attributes the failure to vagueness in the agreement and a lack of a neutral third-party arbiter, unlike the IAEA’s role in the nuclear deal.

Tensions escalated because the US simultaneously pursued a military strategy to create a fortified southern corridor through Omani waters while the MOU was technically in effect. Iran interpreted this as a violation and an attempt to bypass the agreement, leading to shootouts with ships and ultimately a return to 13 days of US bombing. Parsi concludes that both sides rushed to kinetic force over disputes that should have been resolved through structured negotiation mechanisms.

### Iran’s Demands and Regional Escalation

Iran has stated it will not negotiate with the White House until Trump is out and will not reopen the Strait of Hormuz until its demands are met, including over $300 billion in war reparations and the complete withdrawal of US military forces from the region.

Parsi interprets these as a "wish list" rather than strict preconditions. While Iran genuinely desires US withdrawal, Parsi argues this is likely to happen organically as the US abandons damaged bases in the region due to cost and lack of GCC support. However, he cautions that demanding withdrawal explicitly could backfire, making the US hesitant to concede.

Regarding Iran’s "horizontal escalation" against Gulf states like the UAE, Oman, Kuwait, and Bahrain, Parsi explains that Tehran views these strikes as justified responses to US use of their territories for attacks. However, he warns that if Iran is perceived as an uncontrollable threat, it could spur regional rivals like Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan to form an anti-Iran bloc, replicating the isolation policies previously driven by the US.

### The Role of China and Asia

Contrary to speculation of a secret US-China deal, Parsi asserts that China is simply protecting its own interests. By drawing down strategic reserves and diversifying oil sources, China helped stabilize global oil prices. Parsi notes that China did not exploit the conflict to weaken US credibility but actively engaged in behind-the-scenes diplomacy to end the fighting.

Asian allies, particularly Japan, which relies heavily on Middle Eastern oil, are frustrated with the US inability to reopen the strait. However, since no Asian nation can militarily force the strait open, their only leverage is diplomatic pressure on Washington to negotiate.

### Israel’s Endgame and the Road Ahead

Parsi argues that Israel’s goals extend far beyond disarming Hezbollah. Citing statements from Israeli cabinet members, he points to ambitions to annex parts of southern Lebanon and expand control in Syria and Palestinian territories. Israel benefits from the prolongation of the war to weaken Iran, placing its interests at odds with the rest of the world.

Looking forward, Parsi identifies two key political events: the US midterms and the upcoming Israeli election in October. While a change in Israeli leadership might alter tactics, Parsi expects no fundamental shift in Israel’s hardline stance, as challenges to Netanyahu are coming from the right for not doing enough, not for doing too much.

### Conclusion: A Negotiated End is Inevitable

When asked when and how the war will end, Parsi was definitive: it will end through negotiation. Military force cannot permanently open the Strait of Hormuz without a massive ground invasion that has no political pathway in the US.

Parsi predicts the conflict will remain somewhat active at least until the US midterms. Ultimately, both sides need the strait open—especially Iran—and only a committed diplomatic process involving compromises from both Washington and Tehran can deliver a stable resolution.
