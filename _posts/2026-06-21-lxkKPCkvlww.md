## Legendary investor reveals his contrarian strategy in today's concentrated tech market

Rick Rule, the veteran investor behind Rule Investment Media and former CEO of Sprott US, recently joined the show to share his candid insights on navigating turbulent markets. With decades of experience in resource investing and market cycles, Rule offers a perspective that challenges conventional Wall Street wisdom.

"The market is not a subject—a source of information. I consider the market to be a facility, a place where I buy and sell fractional ownership of a business," Rule explains. This fundamental shift in thinking separates successful contrarians from the crowd.

## The SpaceX Test: Why Rule Didn't Participate

The conversation opened with discussion of SpaceX's blockbuster IPO, which priced at $135 but opened around $175, valuing the company at over $1.77 trillion—nearly the entire GDP of Canada. Despite the frenzy, Rule didn't participate in the offering.

"I don't know how to value stocks like SpaceX," Rule admits. "In my practice, money is made on the delta between price and value. Given that I had no idea what SpaceX is worth or what it might be worth, I decided not to participate."

Rule sees the enthusiasm around SpaceX as emblematic of a broader shift in market behavior since 1982. "There are a lot of people who invest in a very different fashion. They invest based on their want for excitement or where they think a stock is going rather than what a stock is worth."

The critical risk Rule identifies is market concentration. "If SpaceX and Mag 7 companies at any point decide to miss expectations, that could have the potential to bring down entire indices," he notes, citing Broadcom's sharp decline as a cautionary example.

## Why Market Crashes Delight This 73-Year-Old Investor

At 73 years old, Rule acknowledges he's "well past my prime, closer to the end than to the beginning." Yet he maintains a vicarious wish to become wealthier—and market crashes are exactly what enable that goal.

"When the market as a whole falls dramatically, what that means is that some of the fractional ownership in businesses, otherwise known as shares, in companies that I understand well enough to want to buy them falls. That circumstance delights me," Rule says with conviction. "It absolutely positively delights me."

His reasoning is straightforward: to become wealthier, he must buy businesses selling for less than their worth. Broad-based declines create exactly those opportunities.

"So, to the extent that there's a broad-based market decline, if I maintain both my sanity and my solvency, that's actually to my advantage, not to my disadvantage."

## The Iran Conflict: The Hangover Hasn't Arrived Yet

When asked whether the apparent de-escalation between the US and Iran improves his economic outlook, Rule's response is emphatic: "No."

"Think of a very good party—or in this case a very bad party. It might be that collectively we stop drinking tonight at midnight. In other words, we don't get drunker. But the hangover is what follows."

Rule calculates that the Iran conflict has cost American taxpayers a half a trillion dollars in direct expenditure, pushing the annual budget deficit from $2 trillion to $2.5 trillion. "The economically damaging impacts of this conflagration will last longer. It will need to be financed."

The critical indicator Rule points to is the persistent elevation of long-term interest rates despite political pressure on short-term rates. "That tells you everything you need to know about the skepticism of private capital relative to US inflation and the purchasing power of the US dollar."

Even if peace is achieved, Rule warns that the damage will take months and probably years to undo. Higher energy prices function as an economic tax, diverting money that would otherwise flow to productive activities. "The suggestion that because he says so that the conflict is over is not an assumption that's well supported by fact if you look historically."

## Gold Miners: Extreme Pessimism at Historic Highs

The show highlighted a striking technical indicator: the Gold Miners Bullish Percent Index currently sits near 7—one of the lowest readings ever recorded. Historically, readings below 30 indicate oversold conditions, while single digits mark periods of extreme pessimism.

This extreme bearishness exists despite gold trading near historic highs. The divergence between gold prices and miner sentiment creates a classic contrarian opportunity.

Central banks have noticed. The European Central Bank recently reported that gold has surpassed US Treasuries as a reserve asset held by central banks worldwide. History supports this rotation: after the dot-com bubble burst in 2000, gold rose from roughly $250 per ounce to nearly $1,900 over the following decade.

## Canada's Economic "Own Goal"

The conversation turned to Canada's technical recession—two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth. Rule, who has maintained a residence in Vancouver and invested in Canada for nearly 50 years, pulls no punches in his assessment.

"Canada would love to blame their malaise on Trump. Americans can do that with a straight face. The truth is in Canadian parlance, the Canadian difficulties is an own goal."

Despite possessing great educational levels, a strong resource base, and an intelligent immigration policy, Canada has struggled. Rule attributes this to "an undue reliance on one trading partner, the United States" and policies that have prevented the country from capitalizing on its natural resources.

"The idea that Canada is experiencing weak growth in a period of very high energy prices is an astonishment. It took real to screw up a country as great as Canada."

The former prime minister's failure to understand the business case for Canadian oil and gas, when all trading partners were "screaming for it," represents a missed opportunity that continues to constrain the economy today.

## The Deficit Myth: What Really Matters for Investors

The host noted a curious correlation: federal deficits have ballooned since 2000, yet both the S&P 500 and gold have performed exceptionally well over the same period. Could government spending actually benefit investors?

Rule counters with an adjustment for purchasing power. "If you looked at the S&P in gold terms, the performance of the S&P since the year 2000 has not been all that good."

The real measure isn't nominal returns but what those returns can actually buy. If earnings increase 5% annually while purchasing power decreases 7-8% annually, investors are falling behind regardless of what the headline numbers show.

## The Bottom Line: Maintain Sanity and Solvency

Rick Rule's investment philosophy boils down to a few timeless principles. First, view the market as a facility for transacting, not as an oracle to follow. Second, focus on the gap between price and value, not excitement or momentum. Third, embrace volatility when it creates opportunities to buy quality assets at discounts.

Most importantly, survive the downturns. "If I maintain both my sanity and my solvency, that's actually to my advantage, not to my disadvantage."

For investors willing to think differently than the crowd, market crashes aren't disasters—they're invitations.
