## The Fundamental Economic Reality

In an era of mounting geopolitical tensions and trade wars, one analyst offers a counterintuitive argument that has captured attention across political and economic circles: a full-scale war between the United States and China is not merely unlikely—it is economically and strategically impossible.

George Freeman, founder and chairman of Geopolitical Futures and author of several New York Times bestsellers including *The Storm Before the Calm*, brings decades of experience briefing the US military and intelligence communities. His assessment cuts through the noise of military posturing and ideological rhetoric to reveal a stark geographical and economic reality that makes large-scale conflict between these two superpowers fundamentally irrational.

"China's economy is vast. It is the second largest economy in the world," Freeman explains. "Per capita income in China ranks it as 71st—very low level. So in other words, it is impossible for the product that they produce to be consumed in China. The domestic market can't support that economy. They must be exporters. The largest country to which they export is the United States."

## The Geography of Deterrence

The ancient concept of the "Thucydides Trap"—the idea that rising powers inevitably clash with established powers—assumes geographic proximity and the possibility of one nation conquering the other. Freeman argues this framework fails spectacularly when applied to the US-China relationship.

"Sparta and Athens were close neighbors," Freeman notes. "They had the technology to wage war against each other, and there was a possibility of one defeating the other and therefore extracting the wealth of the other."

The situation between the United States and China could not be more different. These two nations are separated by the vast Pacific Ocean—a body of water primarily controlled by the United States and its naval capabilities. The fundamental question becomes not whether conflict is desirable, but whether it is even conceivable:

- **How do you occupy China?** The logistics of projecting ground force across the Pacific against a nation of 1.4 billion people are staggering.
- **How does China occupy the United States?** The same ocean that protects America also shields it from Chinese military projection.
- **What would either side gain?** Unlike World War II, where defeated nations could be exploited for resources and labor, modern economies cannot be "extracted" in any meaningful sense.

"The facidities trap where they would lust after each other's economies, the wealth of the nations, the ability to wage war—other than a devastating nuclear war, which would be of no one's interest—is simply not there," Freeman states.

## The Interdependence Imperative

China's economic structure creates its own powerful deterrent against military adventurism. Freeman paints a picture of an economy that, despite its massive scale, depends fundamentally on the American consumer:

1. **Export dependency**: China's domestic consumption cannot absorb the massive industrial output its manufacturing sector produces. Without export markets—particularly the American market—China's economy would face catastrophic demand collapse.

2. **Foreign investment needs**: Beyond exports, China requires continued foreign investment to fuel economic growth. This means maintaining relationships with Western capital markets and corporations.

3. **Resource vulnerability**: China depends heavily on imported energy and raw materials, much of which flows through sea lanes that could be disrupted in any conflict scenario.

4. **Oil dependency**: China imports massive amounts of oil, with significant quantities passing through the Strait of Hormuz—a chokepoint that would be immediately threatened in any US-China military confrontation.

"The Chinese badly need outside investment as well in order to make their economy grow," Freeman observes. "They must have a relationship with the United States that's favorable to them."

## The Death of Ideology as a Conflict Driver

One of Freeman's more provocative arguments concerns the nature of the Chinese state itself. While official rhetoric continues to invoke Marxism-Leninism and the legacy of Maoism, Freeman sees a fundamentally transformed economic system:

"China is now a major economy and as such, it has to operate in those terms. It can't decide to go to war with its greatest customer, the United States. The principle of Marxism-Leninism, aside from the authoritarianism to some extent declining inside of China, is irrelevant. Maoism is gone."

This is not to suggest that China has become a liberal democracy. Rather, Freeman argues that China has emerged as "a massive capitalist country with great inequality"—a characterization that suggests shared economic interests rather than irreconcilable ideological differences.

"We should get away from the ideology part," Freeman advises. "The Chinese will officially claim to be whatever they want to be. But in practice, they have emerged as a massive capitalist country."

## Tariffs as Bargaining Chips, Not Weapons

Freeman offers a distinctive interpretation of President Trump's trade war with China, suggesting that tariffs were a deliberate tool of economic statecraft rather than mere protectionism:

"I think he came to power because I say this not knowing him and knowing that everybody thinks he's an idiot. I think he came to power thinking that he had to have a collaboration with China that China would not be collaborating unless he put pressure on it."

The strategy, according to Freeman, was to impose tariffs broadly on other countries as well, thereby avoiding the appearance of singling out China for humiliation—a crucial diplomatic consideration. The tariffs served as leverage to force China into a more cooperative economic relationship while maintaining face for both sides.

## The New Geopolitical Paradigm

Freaman argues that the world is witnessing a fundamental transformation in the nature of geopolitical power itself. The post-1945 order was built on military alliances and the ever-present threat of nuclear war between the United States and Soviet Union. That system is crumbling, replaced by something qualitatively different.

"The fundamental difference being that the economies of each of these countries are in some way blocking the possibilities of war," Freeman observes. "It is more moving toward economic power than from military power."

This represents a historic shift:

- **Cold War paradigm**: Military confrontation with relatively minor economic issues between the Soviet bloc and the Western world
- **Emerging paradigm**: Primarily economic confrontation where neither side has meaningful military desires against the other

"Free trade was a mantra, something we believed in fundamentally as a moral principle, but it really only took place in the context of the Cold War," Freeman explains. "Just as aid to foreign nations evolved as a tool in the Cold War, not out of charity. So when we look at this age, it's not the last age with different players. It is fundamentally different in its behavior."

## The Decline of Traditional Alliances

If economic interdependence is replacing military alliances as the stabilizing force in world affairs, what happens to the institutional infrastructure built around the post-World War II order?

Freeman offers a blunt assessment of international institutions:

- **The United Nations**: "Non-existent. It's a place you go to have meetings. It doesn't do anything. It doesn't have any influence in the world."
- **NATO**: The military alliance that defined the Cold War is becoming increasingly irrelevant as the Russian threat has proven far weaker than anticipated. "If [Russia] could not take Ukraine, it can't take Poland, which has a massive military at this point."

The Warsaw Pact is gone, NATO's original adversary has fragmented, and the question becomes what purpose these military alliances serve in an era when the major powers are bound together by commerce rather than divided by ideology.

## The Russia-China Myth

A common assumption in Western policy circles is that China and Russia form a natural alliance against Western interests. Freeman dismantles this assumption with historical context and strategic analysis:

"China and Russia are not friends. Even under communism, they fought border wars against each other. Even while Khrushchev was in power, Mao called him the revisionist and deplored him and everything."

More significantly, their strategic interests diverge dramatically:

- **Russia benefits from the Iran war** through higher oil prices as global supply disruptions increase demand for Russian exports
- **China loses from the Iran war** as it depends on oil imports and benefits from stable, low energy prices
- **Chinese territorial claims**: China officially claims the eastern part of Russia—the area of Vladivostok—which Russians occupied in the late 19th century. Chinese maps still show this region as part of China.

"China wants the price of oil to go down," Freeman notes. "The idea that they would take the pain of an ongoing war so that Russia might be able to sell oil at higher prices—that's not there."

## The Arms Merchant's Dilemma

Freeman doesn't argue that military power has become irrelevant. Rather, he suggests that its function is changing fundamentally. Defense spending, rather than being a dead weight on the economy, has historically been the engine of American technological innovation:

"One of the virtues of defense spending is the foundation of such things as AI. So much of the technology that emerged—for example, the microchip—was first developed in the 1950s by Texas Instruments for the US Air Force. So there's a vast spin-off from military technology development and the contemporary economy."

The defense industry serves a dual purpose:

1. **Security function**: Maintaining military capabilities for genuine defense needs
2. **Innovation function**: Funding research and development that eventually transforms the civilian economy

"These things emerged out of defense," Freeman emphasizes. "It's not a marginal thing. It's been a central pivot of the American economy and one of the ways in which the American economy grew and spread."

## The Iran Nuclear Question

The conversation turns to the Iran conflict, which Freeman characterizes as rooted in legitimate American security concerns but executed with fundamentally flawed intelligence and strategy:

"The reason for the war was a genuine fear of nuclear weapons in Iran—and a reasonable one. The American primary demand is that the Iranians give up their nuclear weapons."

However, Freeman argues that the strategy misunderstood Iran's political structure. The initial strike killed what appeared to be government officials, but the real power structure—the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC)—was not destroyed. Iran has developed what military analysts call a "mosaic structure" where each region can defend itself under its own command, making decapitation strikes ineffective.

The deeper concern, according to Freeman, involves ideology and the principle of Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD):

"The principle of mutual assured destruction depends in part on ideology. The Soviets had the ideology—we do not want to be devastated. The Americans had the ideology. The fundamental question is if you believe what the Iranians say and how they act, that ideology is not present."

This represents a significant departure from Cold War deterrence theory, where MAD worked precisely because both sides shared a fundamental desire for survival:

"There is in the Islamic world a tendency towards suicidal actions and actions that they will never be destroyed for. The Hamas invasion of Israel was suicidal in the sense that Hamas in the end would be destroyed and they're willing to take that risk."

## The Path Forward: Economic Interdependence as Peacemaker

Despite these tensions, Freeman sees a path toward resolution that mirrors the US-China dynamic:

"The fundamental decision has to be that neither side be humiliated. The United States does not want to be humiliated. Iran doesn't want to be humiliated."

China, deeply invested in stable global oil markets, has signaled willingness to help broker a resolution:

"If the United States makes it clear that it will not end the war, it is in the interest of China in particular to intervene, and they have been involved in negotiations and they've said they would use their offices not only in Ukraine but to end the Iranian war."

## America's Internal Storm

The conversation concludes with reflection on America's domestic situation. Freeman's book, *The Storm Before the Calm*, was published in early 2020, just as the COVID-19 pandemic was beginning to reshape global affairs.

"We're certainly in a storm internally," Freeman acknowledges, drawing parallels to the 1950s, when America faced internal crises over the Vietnam War, civil rights, and anticommunist paranoia.

However, Freeman sees historical patterns suggesting resolution:

"I said this is a storm before the calm, and toward the end, we're nearing the point where the storm should subside. Why it should subside is historical. I see some elements of it dying down."

His framework treats these periodic American crises as part of a larger cycle:

"This goes back to the founding—that every 50 years there's this sort of crisis. America is an invented country. We live with the principle of obsolescence and reinvention in every dimension of our lives, personal and economic. This is creative destruction, then reconstruction of something new."

## Key Takeaways

1. **Economic interdependence makes US-China war irrational**: China's export-dependent economy and the United States' role as its largest customer create mutual detterence far more powerful than any military alliance.

2. **Geography supersedes ideology**: The Pacific Ocean makes military conquest between these nations impossible, eliminating the core dynamic that drove past great power conflicts.

3. **The world order is shifting from military to economic power**: International relations increasingly revolve around trade relationships and market access rather than military alliances and ideological competition.

4. **Traditional alliances are becoming obsolete**: NATO and the United Nations, designed for a world of ideological blocs and military confrontation, are losing relevance as economic interdependence becomes the primary stabilizing force.

5. **America's internal crises follow historical patterns**: Periodic domestic upheaval followed by reconstruction has characterized American history, suggesting the current political crisis will eventually give way to renewal.

---

*The most important question," Freeman concludes, "is not what happens to any particular conflict or region. It is what happens to Europe—a fragmented continent whose combined GDP exceeds China's but whose political disunity limits its global influence. And it is what happens to America, which remains the pivotal country militarily, economically, and ideologically—and which, despite its current internal storm, has historically emerged from each crisis stronger than before.*
