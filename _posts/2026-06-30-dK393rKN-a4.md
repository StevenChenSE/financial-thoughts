## Richard Smith says the 65-month liquidity cycle points to further downside, with Bitcoin potentially testing $30,000-$35,000

The cryptocurrency market's correction is far from finished, according to Richard Smith, executive director of the Foundation for the Study of Cycles. Speaking on the heels of his accurate prediction of Bitcoin's cycle top in late 2025, Smith warns that the digital asset remains under pressure as a critical liquidity cycle continues to unwind.

"We're going to likely see a major correction until at least the second half of 2027, if not 2028," Smith stated. "Bitcoin is not done going down, I'm afraid. I absolutely expect Bitcoin to be below 50."

The prediction markets appear to be pricing in similar sentiment, with traders on Kashi assigning a 61% probability that Bitcoin will end the year below $50,000—a figure that currently trades around $60,000.

Smith's bearish outlook rests on his analysis of the 65-month liquidity cycle, a framework developed by Michael Howell that Smith has championed. According to Smith, the cycle shows liquidity tightening as money flows out of the financial sector and into the real economy—a shift that has significant implications for risk assets.

"Bitcoin is kind of the canary in the coal mine for liquidity," Smith explained. "It's one of the first to benefit and the first to suffer from changes in liquidity. The fact that liquidity is decreasing and is likely to do so for a couple of years doesn't bode well for the near-term prospects of Bitcoin."

### Understanding the Five-Wave Cycle

Smith outlined a framework for understanding Bitcoin's volatility: a typical five-wave cycle of approximately 200 days to the upside, followed by a three-wave correction of similar duration. These corrections, he noted, typically range from 75% to 80%.

"If you take 125 and you go down three-quarters of that, what does that put you? Somewhere around 30-35," Smith said, suggesting Bitcoin could potentially test those levels during this correction phase.

The current market environment, characterized by rising interest rates globally—the ECB raising rates, Japan raising rates, and the Federal Reserve expected to follow—creates headwinds for the liquidity-dependent asset class. Yet Smith believes the forces pushing toward tighter conditions are being met with equally powerful forces attempting to maintain the debt machine.

### The Debt Wall and Credit Expansion

Central to Smith's analysis is the unprecedented debt situation facing the global economy. According to the Congressional Budget Office's February 2026 outlook, U.S. debt will reach a record 120% of GDP by 2036—less than eight years away. The CBO projects that debt held by the public will grow by $25 trillion, with deficits exceeding $3 trillion annually.

"There's a massive wall of debt that needs to be refinanced over the next five years," Smith noted. "When the Fed took interest rates down to zero, nobody in 4,000 years of recorded interest rate history had ever taken rates below zero. So of course, all the people who could do so terming out their debt. And now that debt wall that needs to be refinanced is dead smack ahead of us."

The refinancing needs create what Smith describes as existential pressure to maintain the debt system. He points to the explosion of AI data center investment as partly motivated by this necessity.

"The debt explosion with AI is also motivated by the need to just keep the debt system alive. It's existential. The debt has gotten so extreme and the refinancing needs are so great now that that machine has to be kept going."

Smith expects an aggressive attempt by Treasury and the Fed to expand credit because, in his view, "there's going to be tightening in the private sector, so I think that credit is desperately needed."

### Japan as a Template

When asked how to analyze an economy at such unprecedented debt levels, Smith points to Japan as the closest model.

"I think Japan is a pretty good model for what we'll be seeing in the U.S.," he said. "But the U.S. has this unique position of being the world's reserve currency with the dollar and having treasuries be the collateral foundation of the global financial system."

Short-term treasuries, Smith explained, form the basis of how trade happens and how money flows globally. That position, however, is "absolutely being called into question right now and being competed for."

This competition manifests in multiple ways: debates over the dollar's role in global trade, Middle East conflicts tied to maintaining the petrodollar system, and the dispute over critical trade routes. Jamie Dimon recently highlighted Europe's relative decline—from 90% of U.S. GDP to 70%—as evidence of shifting economic power dynamics.

"We're in the big cycle away from 1972," Smith noted, referring to the Kondratieff cycle framework. That year, Nixon removed the U.S. from the gold standard, fundamentally altering the global monetary system. "We are literally one Kondratieff cycle from that seminal event of the past 55 years, and here we are again having a global debate over the role of the U.S. dollar."

### Inflation and External Pressures

Adding complexity to the outlook, Smith cited research from fellow cycle analyst Larry Williams showing a strong correlation between solar flares and inflation. With solar activity forecasted to increase over the coming years, this could compound existing inflationary pressures.

"Solar flares affect weather and agricultural products," Smith explained. "That definitely is part of the picture—the prospect that inflation is likely to increase over the next couple of years."

The Atlantic Multi-Decadal Oscillator cycle also enters the equation, with both factors driving money flows from the financial sector toward real economy needs including food and energy.

### Navigating the Next Phase

The combination of tightening liquidity, massive refinancing needs, geopolitical tensions, and potential inflationary pressures creates what Smith describes as a more dangerous environment.

"Just from a complex adaptive systems point of view, the system is more brittle right now with the level of debt and refinancing needs and inflation being on the rise," he warned.

Yet Smith remains measured in his outlook. Despite the challenges, the U.S. retains advantages that may prevent immediate crisis.

"The U.S. is still largely the cleanest shirt in the dirty laundry," he noted. "We have a lot of geographic benefits and tax base benefits. So we're still the most reliable place to have the global financial system be rooted in."

For traders and investors, Smith sees the dollar strengthening as a key theme, with implications for all dollar-denominated assets. Gold outperforming the dollar index—currently assigned a 75% probability by prediction markets—fits within this framework of flight to safety and real assets.

The fundamental question, Smith suggests, is whether the system can be kept alive through continued credit expansion despite structural headwinds. His answer is pragmatic: "There is no alternative. It has to be done. So what we can expect is that it will be done, and we will see sluggishness everywhere else as people spend more money servicing debt."

### Summary

Richard Smith's analysis points to several key themes for investors to watch:

- **Bitcoin faces further downside** as the 65-month liquidity cycle continues its contraction phase, with potential test of $30,000-$35,000 levels
- **Credit expansion efforts** by the Fed and Treasury will attempt to offset private sector tightening but will likely result in economic sluggishness
- **The debt refinancing wall** over the next five years represents an existential challenge for the financial system
- **The dollar's reserve status** remains contested but intact for now, driving geopolitical competition
- **Inflationary pressures** may intensify due to solar activity cycles and real asset repricing
- **Real economy assets** may outperform financial assets as the cycle progresses

The consensus from the recent Cycles in the City conference, attended by leading analysts including Michael Howell and Larry Williams, pointed to the second half of this decade being "absolutely going to be a challenging time." For market participants, Smith's framework suggests positioning defensively while recognizing that the systems driving global finance will likely be preserved—at considerable cost.
