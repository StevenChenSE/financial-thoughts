## The economist warns of growing market manipulation and explains why traditional investing wisdom no longer applies

---

Economist Steve Hanke has issued a stark warning: if current trends continue, the United States faces an economic catastrophe. The professor of applied economics at Johns Hopkins University joined the show to discuss the interconnected crises threatening American prosperity—from manipulated markets and soaring gas prices to deepening wealth inequality and runaway inflation.

The conversation explored how a new era of "big players" has fundamentally altered market dynamics, why traditional investing strategies have become obsolete, and what monetary reforms might actually address the growing divide between the wealthy and everyone else.

---

## The Big Players Have Taken Over

Hanke introduced a theoretical framework that explains much of what investors observe in today's markets: the Big Players Theory. This academic concept, developed in part by his co-author Leland Lintott along with Fisher Black, describes how powerful actors—whether private individuals like Elon Musk or political leaders like Trump and Xi—can move markets in ways that disconnect asset prices from fundamental value.

"The big players operate and they can be private if they're real big, like Musk for example. Trump's a big player, Chairman Xi in China is a big player," Hanke explained. "They're big enough to influence supply and demand and expectations about supply and demand. They're insensitive to profit and losses."

Unlike rational market participants who calculate fair value based on free cash flows and discount rates, big players make announcements and pronouncements that are "pretty much arbitrary. They're not based on any rules or anything like that."

The consequences are profound. When big players dominate, markets experience what economists call "herding behavior" and "noise trading." Investors abandon fundamentals and technical analysis, instead trading "off of hype, rumors, other factors that are not associated with fundamentals." This creates higher volatility, increased likelihood of bubbles forming, and greater chances of those bubbles popping violently.

Hanke pointed to Trump's own G7 summit statement as a textbook example of big player manipulation: "Every time we talked about the possibility of peace, the stock market shot up like a rocket ship. It never went down." Trump himself appeared to acknowledge his ability to move markets at will—and Hanke suggests this pattern is now being exploited systematically.

## Gas Prices and the Strait of Hormuz Crisis

One of the most pressing economic concerns facing Americans is the price at the pump. Trump recently called for gasoline retailers to lower prices to $2.50 per gallon, threatening legal action against companies he accused of price gouging. Hanke calls this "jawboning" and considers it largely delusional.

The EIA breakdown of gas price components reveals the reality: crude oil accounts for 51% of the pump price, federal and state taxes comprise about 17%, refining costs and profits make up 14%, and distribution and marketing add another 17%. Retailers control only the distribution and marketing margins—roughly 17% of the total cost structure.

"They can not make any profits, and the state has to take out the taxes," Hanke noted. "But yeah, the margin which they're not going to do."

Beyond the structural issues, Hanke predicts crude oil prices will actually rise significantly. His baseline forecast puts WTI crude at $85-90 per barrel, up from the current ~$70. The reason lies in the geopolitical crisis triggered by US and Israeli attacks on Iran.

"Trump caused the problem and now he's trying to say that the oil companies have caused the problem," Hanke said. "The attack was made, prices jumped up. The Strait of Hormuz was shot and as a result of that, the world basically borrowed 1.2 billion barrels of oil from the future, sucked that into the present, put it into the present market, lowering inventories."

The world must now replenish these borrowed barrels. Even if the Strait fully reopened—which Hanke considers unlikely—demand for oil would surge as inventories are rebuilt. Prediction markets currently show a 98% chance gas prices remain above $2.50 and only a 2% chance they drop below that threshold.

## The Path to Economic Catastrophe—Or Away From It

Hanke believes Trump understands the stakes. "He's smart enough to know if things continue, we are going to have an economic catastrophe," Hanke observed. "He doesn't want to be Herbert Hoover. He doesn't want an economic catastrophe."

The only way out, according to Hanke, involves opening the Strait of Hormuz, which Iran currently controls. This would likely require implementing what Hanke calls "the surrender agreement"—a memorandum understanding that would see Iran emerge as the victor, controlling the strategic waterway and potentially charging tolls for passage.

Ironically, this arrangement would resemble the Montreux Convention of 1936, which explicitly allows Turkey to charge fees—called management fees—for ships passing through the Bosphorus and Dardanelles. "There is one international waterway in which tolls, management fees are charged," Hanke noted. "I just gave it to you."

## The Inflation Genie Escaped—And Wealth Inequality Follows

Beyond geopolitical concerns, Hanke addressed domestic economic dysfunction. The inflation rate currently sits at 4.2%—more than double the Fed's 2% target. "The inflation genie has escaped. It's out of the bottle," he declared.

More striking is the connection Hanke draws between monetary policy and wealth inequality. Consider the trajectory of billionaire wealth: in December 2019, it represented 13.7% of GDP. Today, it stands at 26.3%—nearly doubling in just over five years.

"What happened? The Fed goose the money supply. And once they goose the money supply, what happens? Asset prices go up with a lag, which they did. Stock prices went up, land prices went up, real estate went up—all asset prices zoomed up after the Fed goose the money supply."

Simultaneously, labor's share of national income has fallen to its lowest level since 1947. While wages for those above the median income of ~$83,000 have performed reasonably well, the bottom 50% of workers have seen their consumption power shrink. "The little guy isn't consuming much," Hanke noted. "That's why the little guy is complaining about affordability."

The top 10% of American wage earners now account for 50% of all consumption in the country. Even Walmart has shifted its strategy toward more affluent customers and higher-end products, while Saks Fifth Avenue returns to luxury retail.

## The Fed's Three Fatal Flaws

Hanke identifies three critical failures at the heart of Federal Reserve policy:

**First, the Fed rejects the quantity theory of money.** It does not acknowledge that substantial changes in the money supply cause substantial changes in asset prices, economic activity, and inflation.

**Second, the Fed ignores the money supply entirely.** Rather than monitoring M2 growth, the Fed focuses on the federal funds rate—a blunt instrument that fails to capture what's actually happening to the money circulating through the economy.

**Third, monetary policy is not neutral.** Changes in the money supply disproportionately favor certain groups. Asset holders (the wealthy) benefit when money supply expands, while wage earners and savers bear the costs.

"Monetary policy is behind this," Hanke explained. The solution requires the Fed to embrace quantity theory principles, targeting steady M2 growth of 5-6% annually—consistent with the 2% inflation target and stable nominal GDP growth. Critically, this growth should be designed neutrally, "not favor some one group or another group."

---

## Why Minimum Wage Hikes Won't Solve Inequality

Some propose raising the minimum wage to address labor's declining share of national income. Hanke dismisses this approach as counterproductive.

"If it happens to be over what a normal market equilibrium wage would be for the type of employment that's involved, you end up squeezing in particular low-skilled workers out of the market," he warned. "They become unemployed. Then they go on welfare."

Doubling the minimum wage would eliminate millions of jobs, pushing workers onto government assistance—a net negative for the very people it intends to help.

---

## The Warning Signs Beyond America's Borders

The United States is not alone in facing fiscal strain. The UK recently reported spending more on social welfare than it collected in tax revenue—a first in the nation's history. "The UK is in trouble big time," Hanke concluded. "There's no question about it."

## Summary

Steve Hanke's analysis paints a troubling picture of interconnected economic challenges:

- **Markets have become disconnected from fundamentals** due to the influence of big players whose arbitrary pronouncements drive herding behavior and noise trading
- **Gas prices will likely rise further** due to the Strait of Hormuz crisis and the need to rebuild depleted oil inventories
- **The equity market is in a bubble** that most observers refuse to acknowledge
- **Wealth inequality has worsened dramatically** because of Fed monetary policy that favors asset holders
- **The inflation genie has escaped** and won't return to the bottle without fundamental reform at the Federal Reserve
- **The only way to address inequality sustainably** is monetary neutrality: steady M2 growth of 5-6% per year that doesn't favor any particular group

For ordinary citizens concerned about their economic future, Hanke's prescription is clear: until the Federal Reserve abandons its current approach and returns to sound monetary principles, the trends favoring the wealthy at the expense of everyone else will continue. The economic catastrophe Trump fears—and Hanke believes is coming—can only be averted by addressing the root cause: a monetary system that has abandoned its moorings.
