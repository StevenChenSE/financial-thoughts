---

The S&P 500 is hitting new all-time highs, and nobody—including the professionals—can quite explain why. It's a paradox that has investors scratching their heads: geopolitical tensions are escalating, oil prices are elevated, and the global landscape looks more uncertain than it has in decades. Yet the market refuses to cooperate with the bears.

"This is rather surprising," Brad McFaten admitted during a recent episode of Capital. "It's a new record high. Go back a few years, this sort of thing would have happened and you would have been on your knees. But it doesn't seem to want to go down."

The question on everyone's mind: what's driving these highs, and more importantly, what happens when reality finally catches up?

## The Irrational Market

The old Wall Street adage comes to mind: *the market can remain irrational longer than you can stay solvent*. Chris Minoshi, co-host of the Capitalist Exploit Insider newsletter, sees several forces at work keeping the rally alive.

"When you go back and think about the dot-coms—when you and I were cutting our teeth—there were points in time where you went, 'What's going on? This doesn't make sense,' and yet the market continued higher," Minoshi observed. "That irrational exuberance has momentum."

One critical factor: passive management dominance. "Capital largely is being run by passive managers now. Most of this stuff is algo-driven and very much trend-following. It's like one of those super tankers—difficult to turn around because they just sort of move very slowly."

Beyond algorithmic momentum, there's a lack of attractive alternatives. "If you look at your investable universe that has deep pools of liquidity, Europe looks like a mess," Minoshi explained. "You wouldn't necessarily want to be investing into the GCC countries at the moment. Europe has had problems with the conflict with Russia, and prior to that, they've had all manner of socialist problems—which the bureaucrats in Brussels keep piling on top of them, whether it be higher taxes, more regulation. All of these things are long-term poor for economics."

The result: "You look and say, 'Where are the markets that if I'm going to stay in equity markets have depth of liquidity that do not look to be problematic?' So the North American market still, on that basis, looks less bad than many others."

## The Bond Market Never Lies

While equities confound logic, the bond market tells a different story—one that should concern every investor.

"The bond market is always the smarter of the two," Minoshi stated. "And if we look at the bond market, that does look problematic. Bonds are rolling over across the board. In fact, the only bond market in the world that's posting positive returns is China."

McFaten sees the technical setup as ominous: "If you bring up the yield on the US 10-year or 5-year, it's been sort of consolidating. The next move is to the upside. It's been in a trading range since early 2024—two years. Now you're seeing a compression. You have one, two, three, four, five humps, and now you're ready to go. Higher lows. Trading range is compressing. We're about to see a massive move in the bond market, bond yields to the upside."

The implication is stark: "Yields should be 5% already on the US 10-year. That's where it probably wouldn't make sense. But in terms of the price action, this is getting ready to go."

## Private Equity's Quiet Crisis

Beneath the surface of record equity highs, a seismic shift is occurring in private markets—and it's being largely ignored by Main Street investors.

"Private equity has now been gating," Minoshi revealed. "There's over 20 different funds that have all gated now. They won't give investors their capital back. That's private credit and private equity—the two tied at the hip."

This isn't a minor technical glitch. Many of these private credit transactions were funding data centers. "I saw a report two days ago that something like 83% of all planned data center investments were on hold," Minoshi noted. "When you translate that all the way back through into this whole AI bubble, the front end of that particular train is these investments. And if they're all being halted—where they're saying, 'We're concerned this doesn't make any sense, maybe we don't have the energy to actually—we're going to pull these data centers in'—then you can't power the damn things."

This is the consequence of years of underinvestment in energy infrastructure, a theme McFaten and Minoshi have been hammering for years. The chickens are coming home to roost.

## The AI Bubble Mirrors Past Manias

The parallels to previous market manias are striking—and troubling.

"I recall when the marijuana bubble was happening," McFaten said. "I had affiliates contacting us saying, 'We want you to get into pot stocks.' And I was like, 'Go away.' These were retarded valuations. It's a weed. It literally grows anywhere. There's no barrier to entry. And it was trading at ridiculous valuations. That was the top."

Now, the same dynamic is playing out with AI. "I'm seeing the same sort of things taking place in AI which make no sense."

One example stands out: "There's a company called Allbirds. What were they before they became an AI company? They're a shoe company. A shoe company. They ceased operations on shoes because they were literally broke. They did a $50 million convertible financing and switched the name to an AI stock, rebranding themselves. The shares quadrupled."

"The consequences are coming," Minoshi warned. "Pension funds have been buying this garbage. A lot of them are still marking it to market on an asset that doesn't have value anymore. Come year-end, you're going to have to start actually marking the stuff to real market value."

## The Energy Thesis: Where the Real Opportunity Lives

While passive algorithms chase the latest high-beta names, the Capitalist Exploit team has been quietly positioned in energy—specifically, oil and gas services. It's a position that has paid off well but, in their view, is just getting started.

"We think there's a massive decadelong tailwind behind us for the resource sector, for hard assets," Minoshi said.

McFaten elaborated on the specific opportunity: "One sector that is we just do not hear boo from is oil and gas services. Look at the ETF XES—all service stocks. It's not far from making new highs. Then look at XOP, the average oil and gas producer stock. It's popped up but pulled back on a daily basis."

The critical insight: "Exxon doesn't go and drill. Exxon doesn't do subsea service work. Exxon doesn't have OSVs. Exxon doesn't have FPSOs—or very few. And that's where all that capital absorption happens."

The infrastructure simply isn't there. "If you look at the capacity utilization of these different subsectors—OSVs, drillers, drill ships, FPSOs, subsea service companies—you look at their capacity utilization before the war took hold, it's near full capacity. As an example, drill ships sitting about 90% capacity. Ninety-five percent would be considered full capacity. So there's just not the infrastructure, the resources that there once was to absorb all this increase in capex coming down the line."

## The Global Refining Crisis

The world is about to undergo a fundamental reconfiguration of its energy infrastructure—and most investors aren't paying attention.

"Australia had six refineries at one stage. Now they're down to two," McFaten noted. "We're only producing about 10% of the oil we produced 20 years ago. We've been increasingly relying on oil coming from imported sources—South Korea, Singapore, Malaysia, and China—which has now stopped."

The irony is acute: "Australia's got plenty of oil. We could be self-sufficient. But the greenies and all the lefties have put a close on developing oil and gas assets. Now you're starting to get different states going, 'Hey, we've got all this oil here. We need to be starting to develop it.'"

This is the birth of what McFaten and Minoshi coined years ago: energy nationalism.

"We said, 'Look, five years from now, the big thing will be energy nationalism—or energy security—as opposed to ESG. It's a little buzzword that'll fly away. And that's what's happening."

Refineries present a particularly thorny problem. "The US imports Saudi oil and exports the light stuff to Asia. It's not oil is not oil to the degree that people think it is. The US doesn't have the refineries set up for the heavy crude which is what is used for the production of diesel. And diesel is what really operates and makes the industrial economy operate."

## Food Security: The Coming Crisis

The Strait of Hormuz crisis has created a slow-motion catastrophe that won't fully materialize until late 2026—a full year after tensions escalated.

"Everything we've been receiving—all the petrol, gas, diesel, jet fuel—comes from tankers that left the Strait of Hormuz or passed through before the war broke out," McFaten explained. "That's inventory we've been surviving on up until now. That starts running out towards the end of April. So we're probably going to see rationing of diesel particularly here in Australia within the next few weeks."

The food system faces an even longer lag. "Farmers don't plant because they can't get the diesel. They can't get the fertilizer. That doesn't show up until 12 months later."

Fertilizer shortages will compound the problem. "There's two forms of fertilizer—ammonia-based and phosphate-based. Thirty percent of ammonia comes through the Strait of Hormuz. With phosphates, you need sulfur—which comes from refining. Fifty percent of the world's sulfur comes through the Strait of Hormuz."

"When people start getting hungry, that's when the pitchforks come out," Minoshi observed grimly. "That's when revolutions start happening."

## Where to Position: The Geographic Question

With all this uncertainty, where should investors actually position themselves?

Minoshi sees Latin America as particularly well-positioned: "You've got where the government chooses to do something, the following from a populace to whatever ridiculous rules they might choose to put into place by and large doesn't happen. It's interesting the conversations I have with our Argentine partners—when there's some legislature, the immediate response is, 'How do we get around this?' Because they've been doing it for 80 years. There's an internal resilience built into the populace because they've just been dealing with it for generations."

Contrast that with the West: "People are like, 'Oh, but what if I have to report this or I have to do that.' The mindset is one of slavery for Westerners because they've never had to sort of stand up and go, 'Hang on. I'm going to do what I want to do.'"

McFaten's advice is more pragmatic: "Live where it makes you happy. Live where your family is or where you want your family to grow up. Where it's safe. And then just concentrate on making good money."

The key is sector selection. "I can think of one sector where you don't have to worry about where you are geographically because it's a global sector. It's energy—coal, oil and gas exploration, production, shipping, tankers, services. Concentrate on making money and just live in places where maybe the tax rates are a bit higher, so you got a little bit more money."

## Volatility Is the Price of Admission

For those ready to act on the energy thesis, Minoshi offers a crucial word of caution.

"Markets don't go in a straight line," he emphasized. "We had a run from $54 oil to $90-and-something in spot. It's entirely possible that we can have $75 oil in the next few months. But that's not the story. That's your traders with leverage moving in and out of futures contracts. That's not the end story. The end story is much more likely that we got $100, $200-plus oil."

Emotional discipline is everything: "People will see some wild swing in something and then they automatically go, 'Oh, I'm wrong' or whatever. And they get out. They let their emotions drive their decision-making. That's a big mistake."

The energy trade requires conviction and patience. "Understanding the big trends unfolding and ensuring that you don't miss out on them—that's one of the things we try to teach in the insider program. Position sizing and managing that risk of volatility while still staying in the game."

## The Bottom Line

The S&P at all-time highs in 2026 represents a market divorced from fundamental reality—a confluence of passive momentum, algorithmic trend-following, and a lack of attractive alternatives. The bond market tells a different story, one of higher yields and ultimately tighter financial conditions.

Meanwhile, the real economy is facing a slow-motion crisis: energy infrastructure in disarray, fertilizer supplies tightening, and food production facing headwinds that won't show up in the data for months.

The opportunity, as McFaten and Minoshi see it, lies in hard assets—specifically, the oil and gas sector, with particular emphasis on the overlooked oilfield services space. It's a trade that has underperformed the headline indices for decades and sits at the nexus of energy nationalism, supply chain reconfiguration, and geopolitical realignment.

"We think there's a massive decadelong tailwind behind us," Minoshi said. "And we're saying the same thing, year after year, because nothing changes in the fundamental thesis."

Sometimes, the smart money isn't about being clever. It's about being consistent—and staying in the game long enough to let the thesis unfold.
