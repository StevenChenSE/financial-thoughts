**Brad McFaten and Chris Minto on the Markets That Make No Sense—and the Ones That Do**

---

On April 16th, 2026, the S&P 500 reached new all-time highs. For most market observers, this defied logic. The Middle East was engulfed in conflict, oil prices had spiked to multi-year highs, and geopolitical tensions were running hotter than they'd been in decades. By historical precedent, equities should have been crumbling.

Instead, they kept climbing.

"It's a new record high," Brad McFaten observed during a recent episode of the Capital podcast. "You go back a few years, this sort of thing would have happened, and you would have been on your knees. But it doesn't seem to want to go down."

The question that puzzled everyone—why exactly is the market ignoring what should be catastrophic headlines?—became the focal point of a wide-ranging discussion between McFaten, his colleague Chris Minto, and host Danny.

---

## The Passive Revolution and Irrational Markets

Chris Minto offered one of the more compelling explanations for the disconnect between market action and fundamental reality: the rise of passive investing and algorithmic trading.

"What we're seeing is that capital largely is being run by passive managers," Minto explained. "Most of this stuff is just algo-driven, and it's very much trend-following. So it's like one of those super tankers—difficult to turn around because they just sort of move very slowly."

This structural shift means that once a narrative takes hold—particularly the AI-driven growth story that has dominated markets for years—immense capital continues flowing in regardless of valuation concerns or deteriorating fundamentals. The market, in this view, can remain irrational far longer than skeptics can remain solvent.

"When I look back on the dot-com era," Minto recalled, "there were points in time where you'd go, 'What's going on? This doesn't make sense.' And yet the market continued higher. That irrational exuberance, or whatever you want to call it, has momentum."

Beyond sentiment, Minto pointed to a more practical consideration: where else is a global investor supposed to put money to work?

"Europe looks like a mess. You wouldn't necessarily want to be investing into the GCC countries at the moment. And Europe of course, we know what's happening there—they've had problems with respect to the conflict with Russia for some time, and prior to that, they've had all manner of socialist problems which the bureaucrats in Brussels just keep foisting on top of them, whether it be higher taxes, more regulation, yada yada—all of these things which are long-term poor for economics."

The North American market, by this relative measure, still looks "less bad than many others," Minto suggested. That relative attractiveness, combined with passive fund flows that simply follow price momentum, helps explain the persistent strength in U.S. indices even as geopolitical conditions deteriorate.

---

## The Bond Market Knows Something

While equities painted an optimistic picture, Minto directed attention to a different market—one that he believes offers clearer signals.

"The bond market is always the smarter of the two," he noted. "If we look at the bond market, that does look problematic. Absolutely. So, we've got bonds rolling over across the board. In fact, the only bond market in the world that's posting positive returns is China."

McFaten agreed, describing the technical setup as ominous. "It just seems like a question of time before the yields actually break out and start marching off again. If you bring up the yield of the US 10-year or five-year, whatever—it's been sort of consolidating. The next move is to the upside."

The technical pattern McFaten described—a long consolidation period followed by compression—is typically a precursor to significant moves. "Trading range is compressing, and we're seeing that across the board, other bond markets as well. So that's where things would make sense to us."

In their view, bond yields should already be substantially higher. "You should be looking at 5% on the US 10-year," McFaten suggested. "That's where it probably wouldn't make sense. But in terms of the price action, this is getting ready to go."

---

## Private Credit's Silent Warning

One of the most concerning developments neither investor mentioned lightly was the growing crisis in private markets.

"Private equity has now been gating," Minto revealed. "There's over 20 different funds that have all gated now and won't give investors their capital back. That's private credit and private equity—the two tied at the hip."

This gating—when funds suspend redemptions to prevent a run on assets—signals deep structural problems. Many of these private credit transactions were predicated on assumptions that now appear wildly optimistic.

"I saw a report two days ago that something like 83% of all planned data center investments were halted," Minto continued. "When you translate that all the way back through into the whole AI bubble, if you will, the front end of that particular train is these investments. And if they're all being halted, if they're saying, 'We're concerned this doesn't make any sense, maybe we don't have the energy even to actually—' We're going to pull these data centers in. And then we can't power the damn things anyway."

The consequences extend far beyond data centers. Pension funds across the country have loaded up on these private market assets, often marking them to model prices rather than actual market values. "Come your end, you're going to have to start actually marking the stuff to real market value," Minto warned. "And it looks problematic."

---

## The AI Bubble: Same Story, Different Decade

The conversation then veered toward what both guests view as one of the most dangerous speculative manias in recent memory—and they invoked the ghost of bubbles past.

"I recall when there was the marijuana bubble," McFaten said. "These were ridiculous valuations, but I had all number of people saying, 'If you could just give us some content related to marijuana, I know we could sell your newsletter.' And I was like, 'Go away.' And that was the top. That was like the top."

He paused. "And now I'm seeing the same sort of things taking place in AI, which just makes no sense."

The specifics were almost absurd. "There's a company called Allbirds. They're a shoe company. A shoe company. They ceased operations on shoes because they're literally broke. They were struggling. And they did what was like a $50 million convertible financing, switched the name to an AI stock, rebranded themselves as an AI stock—and the shares quadrupled."

From his perspective, this pattern is the hallmark of late-stage speculative frenzies. "When you see that... what we like to try and concentrate on is not getting so caught up with the here and now—the S&P making new highs, the Russell 2000, which no one talked about either. What we're trying to do is sort of look through this and say, 'Okay, well, what's the big thing? Where will things be 12 months, two years, three years from now?'"

---

## The Trade Nobody's Talking About

Despite their bearish outlook on the broader market, both investors remained fully invested—but in sectors they believe have barely begun to move.

"We've been very, very bearish on the S&P, bearish on the NASDAQ," McFaten confirmed. "But we're still fully invested, particularly in the energy space. There's no reason for us to not be fully invested in the things we like: shipping stocks, energy stocks, agricultural stocks, miners."

The most compelling opportunity, in their view, lies in oil and gas services—a sector that has received almost no attention despite fundamentals that should be sending prices dramatically higher.

"Energy stocks are up, but if you look at the valuations of these stocks, it's very, very difficult to justify buying them—your Magnificent 7, AI stocks. The worst thing is high interest rates, which is driven by inflation. And obviously, high energy prices would suggest that inflation is going to spike. So again, no one believes in what is happening in the Middle East as a game-changing event."

The energy services sector represents a different animal entirely. "You take OSVs, drillers, drill ships, FPSOs, and even subsea service companies—you look at their capacity utilization before the war erupted in the Middle East: near full capacity. No one's talking about it. Drill ships sitting about 90% capacity—95% would be considered full capacity—is essentially full capacity now."

This tight capacity means that when capital eventually flows toward energy development—as both investors believe it must—the specialized service providers will benefit disproportionately.

"Exxon doesn't go and drill. Exxon doesn't do subsea service work. Exxon doesn't have OSVs or very few FPSOs," McFaten explained. "That's where the money goes. You pick whichever sub-sector you want—subsea drillers, FPSOs, whatever—it's hard to pick which ones are going to outperform. Just pick the damn sector. That's a sector that's going to rocket, and it's rocketing, and no one's talking about it."

## Energy Nationalism: The Call They Made Years Ago

The shift both investors are anticipating has a name: energy nationalism. They claim to have coined the term years ago when ESG investing dominated headlines.

"We said, 'Look, five years from now, the big thing will be energy nationalism,'" Minto recalled. "Or energy security, as opposed to ESG. That's a little buzzword that'll just fly away like little butterflies do."

The thesis is simple: nations that surrendered their energy independence in the rush toward green energy policies will scramble to restore domestic production. Australia represents a stark example.

"Australia had six refineries. In fact, I think they had more—eight at one stage. Now they're down to two. Now down to one because one essentially blew up recently. They're only producing about 10-15% of the oil they produced 20 years ago. So they've been increasingly relying on oil coming from imported sources—South Korea, Singapore, some from Malaysia, and also China, which has now stopped."

The political calculus is shifting. "The different states are starting to go, 'Hey, well, look, we've got all this oil here. We need to start developing it.' There's going to be a push now towards taking away all the red, green, purple, orange tape to develop these assets."

This pattern won't be unique to Australia. "When Australia is just one example, we think other nations will start doing the same thing. More emphasis will be placed on developing oil and gas assets outside of the Straits of Hormuz."

ExxonMobil's recent $22 billion investment in Western Africa and similar moves by Shell and BP confirm that major players are positioning for this shift. "No one's really picking up on this," McFaten noted. "So who actually absorbs all that capital? It's oil and gas service stocks."

---

## The Food Time Bomb

Beyond energy, both investors see a crisis brewing in global food supplies—a crisis with a long fuse that most markets haven't yet acknowledged.

"There's a long lead-in lag time," McFaten explained. "Shortages of diesel—crops don't get sown. The bigger thing is probably fertilizer. And no one really talks too much about that."

The details reveal just how fragile global agricultural supply chains have become. "There's two forms of fertilizer. There's your ammonia-based fertilizers—30% of the ammonia comes through the Straits of Hormuz. Then there's your phosphate-based fertilizers. The typical grade most farmers would use is called super phosphate. It's not just phosphate—it's sulfur, which comes from refining. And 50% of the world's sulfur comes through the Straits of Hormuz."

The implications are severe. "Farmers don't plant because they can't get the diesel, they can't get the fertilizer, or a combination of both. That doesn't show up until 12 months later. So higher food prices and shortages of food is going to only show up towards the end of the year."

This food inflation will arrive at exactly the wrong moment, combining with elevated energy prices to create what McFaten called "a toxic, toxic thing."

"Energy prices still above $100 a barrel 12 months from now. Combine that with higher food prices. That's inflation. Now you can say, 'Oh, it's cost-push versus demand-pull or whatever—stagflation, whatever.' I'm not caught up in that crap. It's just inflation. It's higher prices."

The social consequences could be profound. "When people start getting hungry, that's when the pitchforks start coming out," Minto observed, referencing the Arab Spring of 2011. "That's when revolutions start happening. Goodness knows what happens on the back of that."

## The Slow Burn or the Sudden Crash?

Given all these structural weaknesses, what does the eventual unwinding look like? Both investors have drawn on their experience with previous market crises to frame their expectations.

"Prior to any conflict in the Middle East, Chris and I thought what was going to happen would be a play out of what happened in 2000 versus 2008," McFaten explained.

In 2000, tech stocks collapsed spectacularly while value stocks—old economy companies in sectors like industrials and homebuilding—held up relatively well or even gained ground. "If you weren't exposed to TMT stocks, at very worst, from March 2000 to March 2003, you would not have lost any money. In fact, if you invested in classic old economy industrial stocks, you would have actually made money."

The alternative—2008—saw almost everything fall together. "We thought that would be the outcome as opposed to 2008, where, get out away, man—everything's on its way down."

They've been encouraged by Katherine Austin Fitz, who recently suggested that rather than the sudden crashes of 2008, markets may face a "slow burn" collapse. Either way, their positioning remains the same.

"Who cares?" McFaten said flatly. "Just stay away from that stuff. Stay away and invest in the stuff that no one else wants to touch. That's held by strong hands, as opposed to weak hands. And we think that's the energy sector in various different forms."

Jeff Curry, the energy analyst who left Merrill Lynch, offered a longer-term perspective that resonated with both investors. "He said 10 years from now, Exxon will be the biggest stock in the world, and Apple will be a fraction of what it was—a complete reversal of what we've seen in the last 20 years."

## The Question of Geography

Danny posed a question that many Westerners have been asking in an era of political instability and perceived decline: Is there anywhere to go?

Minto's answer may surprise those who assume Western democracies represent the pinnacle of human liberty.

"Broadly speaking, Latin America is well-positioned and going to do quite well, to some degree," he began. "It's because nobody trusts their government there. So when the government chooses to do something, the following from the populace to whatever ridiculous rules they might choose to put into place, by and large, doesn't happen."

The contrast with Western populations, in his view, is stark. "The conversations I have with our Argentine partners—whenever there's some particular thing that is maybe some legislature or whatever it is, the immediate response is, 'How do we get around this?' Because they've been doing it for like 80 years. There's an internal resilience built into the populace because they've just been dealing with it for generations."

When contrasted with Westerners, who instinctively ask, "What do I have to report? What do I have to do?" the difference in mindset becomes apparent. "The mindset is one of slavery for Westerners because they've never had to sort of stand up and go, 'Hang on a second. I'm going to do what I want to do.'"

This isn't about political freedom, Minto argued—it's about economic freedom. "What actually matters is economic freedom. There are countries that provide relatively more economic freedom than others while not necessarily providing political freedom."

His example: Singapore. "There's no political freedom, but there's relative economic freedom. Even Hong Kong."

McFaten's perspective was more pragmatic. "Latin America is fine as long as you can speak Spanish or Portuguese. Argentina, great place. But my Spanish ain't too hot."

His broader point: where you live matters less than what you're invested in.

"You concentrate on making money. Money doesn't buy love, but my god, it sure makes things a lot easier. You live where you live and as long as you're happy living there, that's fine. You live where your family is, where your community is, where you want to put roots down."

The key is ensuring that your capital is positioned correctly. "There's one sector where you don't have to worry about where you are geographically because it's a global sector. It's energy—be it coal, be it oil and gas exploration, production, be it shipping, tankers, be it services. There we go. That's what we concentrate on."

## Volatility Is Part of the Process

For those persuaded by their analysis, both investors offered a word of caution about the journey ahead.

"Markets don't go in a straight line," Minto warned. "We had a run from $54 oil to something like $95 in spot. It's entirely possible that we can have $75 oil in the next few months. But that's not the story. That's your traders with leverage moving in and out of futures contracts. That's not the end story. The end story is much more likely $100 to $200-plus oil."

Understanding this volatility—and not abandoning positions during temporary drawdowns—separates successful investors from those who miss the big moves.

"One of the things we see so much is where people will see some wild swing in something, and then they automatically go, 'Oh, I'm wrong,' and they get out. They just let their emotions drive their decision-making. And that's a big mistake."

"You've got a company like Apple that's $3 trillion, and then you've got a lot of these sectors—they're tiny. They can and do move around wildly. You've got to understand that volatility is part of the game."

## The Bottom Line

For McFaten and Minto, the S&P 500's resilience in the face of mounting global challenges represents a disconnect that won't last forever. The bond market tells a different story than equities. Private credit is gating redemptions. AI valuations have reached absurdity. And beneath the surface, structural changes are underway that will reshape energy markets for a decade or more.

"We've been sort of overweight energy-related sectors. We've done very, very well—probably doubled the performance of the S&P 500," McFaten acknowledged. "But it's still been rather frustrating because we still really haven't got liftoff. All the things we're looking at are just coming off the bottom. So we think we've got a massive, decade-long tailwind behind us."

Whether the ultimate unwinding comes as a sudden crash or a slow burn, their prescription remains unchanged: stay away from what everyone else owns, and position yourself in front of the trends that will define the next era of global markets.

"We think there's a massive 10-decade long tailwind behind us for the energy sector, for the resource sector, for sort of hard assets," McFaten concluded. "And you don't have to do too much for like 10, 15 years—just watch the money clock in."
