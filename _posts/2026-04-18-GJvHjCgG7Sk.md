## The Market That Makes No Sense

The S&P 500 is making new all-time highs, and nobody—including the professionals—can quite explain why.

"It's a new record high," Brad McFaten observed during a recent episode of Capital. "You go back a few years, this sort of thing would have happened and you would have been on your knees. But it doesn't seem to want to go down."

The timing is curious. Oil is trading near $90-95 per barrel. Tensions in the Middle East continue to escalate. Global supply chains face unprecedented disruption. By any reasonable measure, the market should be struggling. Instead, it's surging.

Chris Minoshi offers one explanation: "When you go back and think about the dot-coms, there were points in time where you went, 'What's going on? This doesn't make sense,' and yet the market continued higher. So there's that irrational exuberance or whatever you want to call it—it has momentum."

But there's something more structural at work. "Capital is largely being run by passive managers now," Minoshi explains. "Most of this shit's just algo-driven, and it's very much trend following. So it's like one of those super tankers—difficult to turn around because they just sort of move very slowly."

## The Liquidity Trap

When global investors search for safe harbors for their equity capital, they're left with an unappealing menu. Europe remains mired in the Russia-Ukraine conflict, weighed down by socialist policies, high taxes, and increasingly onerous regulation from Brussels. The GCC countries face their own complications. But the North American market, despite everything, still looks "less bad than many others."

"I think that has something to do with it," Minoshi acknowledges. "The just—very quickly—the element that you kind of need to look at is the statement that the bond market is always the smarter of the two."

And what the bond market shows is decidedly problematic. "Bonds are rolling over across the board," Minoshi observes. "In fact, the only bond market in the world that's actually posting positive returns is China."

The 10-year Treasury yield has consolidated since early 2024—a two-year trading range that's compressing. "Trading range is compressing, things getting ready for a massive move," Minoshi notes. "We're about to see a massive move in the bond market, bond yields to the upside. The yield should be 5% already on the US 10-year. That's where it probably wouldn't make sense."

## Private Equity's Hidden Crisis

Behind the scenes, a quiet crisis is unfolding in private markets. "Private equity has now been gating," Minoshi explains. "There's over 20 different funds that have all gated now and won't give investors their capital back. That's private credit and private equity—the two tied at the hip."

The implications extend far beyond the funds themselves. "A lot of those private credit transactions were things in data centers. I saw a report two days ago—something like 83% of all planned data center investments were halted."

This represents the leading edge of the AI bubble's unwinding. "When you translate that all the way back through into the whole AI bubble, if you will, the front end of that particular train is these investments," Minoshi continues. "If they're all being halted, if they're saying, 'Well, we're concerned this doesn't make any sense, maybe we don't have the energy even to actually power the damn things,' then—then there's all of these consequences."

The consequences trace back to a decade of underinvestment in energy infrastructure. "Gosh, 10, 15 years ago with the lack of capital expenditure into the energy space—which we've been talking about for years now—the consequences to that are all coming through."

## The AI Dot-Com Parallel

Minoshi sees eerie parallels to previous bubbles. "You're still seeing some of that crazy shit that we used to see in the dot-coms. Remember when you could just add .com to the end of your name and you'd probably get a valuation?"

The marijuana bubble followed the same pattern. "It was a weed. It literally grows anywhere. So there's no barrier to entry in terms of growing the bloody stuff, and then it's highly regulated anyway. And it was trading at ridiculous valuations."

Now, the same phenomenon appears in AI. McFaten highlights one particularly egregious example: "There's a company called Allbirds. What were they before they became an AI company? A shoe company. A shoe company. They ceased operations on shoes because they were literally broke. They did a $50 million convertible financing, switched the name to an AI stock, rebranding themselves as an AI stock, and the shares quadrupled."

It gets worse. "It actually did way better than a quadruple at the high. It did almost a 10x from the bottom to the peak."

"These are the exact sort of things that you see at the tail end of insanity," Minoshi observes. "Whether it stops tomorrow or whether it takes another 6-12 months, I have no idea."

The danger lies in interconnectedness. "We've got private equity—pension funds have been buying this garbage. A lot of them are still marking it to market on an asset that doesn't have value anymore. Come year-end, you're going to have to start actually marking the stuff to real market value. And it looks problematic."

## The Energy Trade Nobody Wants

Despite the S&P's gains, energy stocks—which should be thriving in this environment—have disappointed. "When we look at energy stocks, which should be doing exceptionally well, they've done okay, but just... look at Exxon. They've done okay, but they're not really taking off."

McFaten sees the reason clearly: "No one really believes this is long-term sustainable. That's the thing I can take away from this."

The market's failure to rotate into energy reflects a broader attachment to the AI narrative. "We've been talking about this great rotation into what we call the halo stocks—heavy asset, obsolete light. So your typical miners and energy stocks outperforming relative to your IT stocks and your Magnificent 7 stocks. That's a thing we've been observing happening, but at a very, very slow pace. It's just that no one really wants to give up on this whole AI thing, the Magnificent 7 thing, the whole growth stock thing."

The headwinds for growth stocks are severe. "The worst enemy for investing in the Magnificent 7 or the AI stocks is highly priced growth stocks. The worst thing is a higher discount rate—which is inflation or interest rates, which is driven by inflation. You don't want that. You don't want high interest rates. And obviously, high energy prices would suggest that inflation is going to spike and likewise interest rates."

McFaten fears the S&P's rally is a false one. "I've seen this before, Danny. I saw this back in... gosh, 2002—23 years ago, back when we had the bounce from the 9/11 smackdown. The NASDAQ rose very, very significantly from September 11th right through until about March 2002. But that was then—it ran out. And the downside from March, April 2002 right through to October 2002 was huge. Not many folks talk about that, but that was nasty. I got to—I fear the same sort of thing is staring down that barrel now. It's a false rally. I wouldn't believe in it."

## The Oil Services Opportunity

Within the energy sector, McFaten sees a particularly compelling sub-sector that nobody is discussing: oil and gas services.

"We like to concentrate on this," he explains. "XES is the oil services ETF. It's not far from making new highs. XOP—which is the average oil and gas producer stock—has popped up but pulled back on a daily basis."

The contrast is stark. While everyone chatters about Exxon and Chevron, the drilling companies, service providers, and infrastructure firms operate almost entirely under the radar. "We hear bullish commentary on energy stocks, but that's somewhat limited to people talking about Exxon and Chevron, maybe getting exotic and talking about Shell or Total. Very few are talking about the sort of smaller cap energy stocks, and no one's talking about oil and gas services."

McFaten's thesis centers on a fundamental capacity shortage. "Take drill ships—sitting about 90% capacity. Ninety-five percent would be considered full capacity. It's essentially full capacity now. There's just not the facilities, the infrastructure, the resources that there once was to absorb all this increase in capex coming down the line."

When countries begin developing domestic oil and gas assets—moving away from dependence on the Strait of Hormuz—they won't be able to rely on major integrated companies. "Exxon doesn't go and drill. Exxon doesn't do subsea service work. Exxon doesn't have OSVs. Exxon doesn't have—or very few—FPSOs. And that's where the capacity is constrained."

This is the essence of energy nationalism, a concept McFaten and Minoshi identified years before the current crisis. "When the whole ESG was the big thing, we said, 'Look, five years from now, the big thing will be energy nationalism.' Or energy security as opposed to ESG. Gosh, it's a little buzz word that'll just fly away like little butterflies do."

The pattern is playing out globally. "Australia—they had eight refineries at one stage. Now they're down to two, and now down to one because one essentially blew up the last few days. They're only producing about 10-15% of the oil they produced 20 years ago. They've been increasingly relying on oil coming from imported sources—South Korea, Singapore, some from Malaysia, and also China, which has now stopped."

Australia is waking up. "If you look at Australia, you're starting to get the different states going, 'Hey, well, look, we've got all this oil here. We need to be starting to develop it.' There's a push now towards taking away all the red, green, purple, orange tape to develop these assets."

This isn't just an Australian phenomenon. "What Exxon's doing—I think it was a $22 billion increase in efforts in Western Africa. Nigeria, I think. Shell's doing the same thing, likewise with BP. No one's really picking up on this. Who actually absorbs all that capital? It's oil and gas service stocks."

## The Fertilizer Time Bomb

The energy crisis has a downstream twin: food. The connection runs through diesel and fertilizer—the two primary costs of food production.

"Shortages of diesel—you know, crops don't get sown, and the bigger thing is probably fertilizer," McFaten explains. "There's two forms of fertilizer. There's your ammonia-based fertilizers—30% of ammonia comes through the Strait of Hormuz. Then there's your phosphate-based fertilizers. The typical grade most farmers would use is called super phosphate. It's not just phosphate—it's sulfur, which comes from refining. And 50% of the world's sulfur comes through the Strait of Hormuz."

The lag effect is critical. "So farmers don't plant because they can't get the diesel, they can't get the fertilizer, or a combination of both. That doesn't show up until 12 months later. Higher food prices and shortages of food are going to only show up towards the end of the year."

Minoshi adds context on supply chains: "Malaysia produces oil, but most of the oil to their refineries comes from the Middle East. There's a difference in blend. Malaysia's oil gets exported because they have refineries built to accommodate heavier crude from the GCC countries, and that oil is now problematic. So they have oil, but they basically export most of it. Back to the point Brad was making—that's going to become another issue as well."

The United States faces similar complications. "The US is a net energy exporter, excepting that it exports the wet fuels and a lot of the light sweet crude. But heavy crude—which is what is used for the production of diesel, and diesel is what really operates and makes the industrial economy operate—the US imports Saudi oil. It exports the light stuff to Asia. So it's not oil is not oil to the degree that people think it is."

The inflation implications are severe. "Energy prices above $100 a barrel 12 months from now, combined with higher food prices—that's inflation. That's a toxic, toxic thing. Interest rates much, much higher. That's not a good outcome for highly valued AI stocks."

## The Hunger Factor

Historical precedent suggests the social consequences could be severe. "Recall the Arab Spring—2011," Minoshi reminds listeners. "When people start getting hungry, that's when the pitchforks start coming out. That's when revolutions start happening."

The comparison is apt. "Revolutions don't tend to happen in Australia, New Zealand, and so on. But some of these emerging nations—there's going to be a lot of unrest when people start getting hungry."

McFaten agrees. "That's a very good point. Nobody's factoring in the hunger aspect of revolution. If we are heading towards food shortages, that's a natural consequence when folks have nothing left to lose."

## The Question of Freedom

Danny raises a question many Westerners are asking: Is anywhere safe? The West appears to be in decline, but everywhere else seems to have its own problems.

Minoshi's answer centers on a distinction most Westerners miss. "One of the things I've learned is that economies basically thrive when they enjoy a certain degree of economic freedom. The story we're told in the West is that we have freedom—the word freedom is just thrown around. Most of the time, people reference freedom with respect to political freedom, which is to say an ability to vote. If there's anything that the last four or five years should have taught anybody, it's that political freedom does not exist. It doesn't fucking matter who you vote for. Orange man, white man, pink man—you get the same outcome. It's the same highway. You just get a different car traveling down it."

The real freedom is economic. "What matters is economic freedom. There are countries that provide relatively more economic freedom than others while not necessarily providing political freedom. Singapore is a perfect example. There's no political freedom, but there's relative economic freedom. Even Hong Kong."

Latin America presents an interesting case study. "Broadly speaking, Latin America is well-positioned—that's going to do quite well to some degree. It's because nobody trusts their government there. The conversations I have with Argentine partners—whenever there's some particular legislature, the immediate response is, 'How do we get around this?' Because they've been doing it for 80 years. There's an internal resilience built into the populace because they've just been dealing with it for generations."

The contrast with the West is stark. "When I contrast that to people in the West, they're like, 'Oh, but but but what if, you know, how do I have to report this or I have to do this or I have to do that.' The mindset is one of slavery for Westerners because they've never had to stand up and go, 'Hang on a second. I'm going to do what I want to do.'"

McFaten offers a more pragmatic perspective. "Latin America is fine as long as you can speak Spanish or Portuguese. Argentina's a great place. But my Spanish ain't too hot."

His advice is straightforward: "Money doesn't buy love, but my god, it sure makes things a lot easier. You live where you're happy, where your family is, where your community is, where you want to put roots down. Concentrate on making money. Get yourself in front of the right sector, the right theme, and you get a big tailwind behind you."

That tailwind, in his view, is energy. "Be it coal, be it oil and gas exploration, production, be it shipping, tankers, be it oil services. That's what we concentrate on—making money and just living in places where maybe the tax rates are a bit higher, so you got a little bit more money. You complain and winge about taxes, but just earn some more money."

## Staying the Course

Despite their bearish outlook on the S&P, McFaten and Minoshi remain fully invested—just not in the indices. "We're still fully invested in particularly the energy space. There's no reason for us to, even though we're somewhat bearish on the S&P, there's no reason for us to not be fully invested in the things we like: shipping stocks, energy stocks, agricultural stocks, miners, and so on."

Their message to investors: understand the volatility. "It's entirely possible that we can have $75 oil in the next few months, right? But that's not the story. That's your traders with leverage moving in and out of futures contracts. That's not going to be the end story. The end story is much more likely $100, $200-plus oil."

Position sizing and risk management matter, but so does conviction. "People will see some wild swing in something and then they automatically go, 'Oh, I'm wrong or whatever. I'm getting out.' They let their emotions drive their decision-making. That's a big mistake."

The big trends they're betting on—energy nationalism, resource scarcity, the unwinding of AI speculation—play out over years, not weeks. "We've been overweight energy-related sectors. We've done very, very well—probably doubled the performance of the S&P 500. But it's still been rather frustrating because we really haven't got liftoff yet. All the things we're looking at are just coming off the bottom. We think we've got a massive, decade-long tailwind behind us."

In a market making all-time highs on the strength of AI dreams and passive flows, Brad McFaten and Chris Minoshi are betting on a different future—one where the lights stay on, the tractors keep running, and the resources beneath the earth's surface become the most valuable assets on the planet. Whether they're right won't be known for years. But as McFaten says, the bond market already knows. And it's telling a very different story than the stock market would have you believe.
