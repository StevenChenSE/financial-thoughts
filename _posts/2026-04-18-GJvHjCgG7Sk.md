**The market that should make sense doesn't—and the one that does, nobody's listening to**

It's April 2026, and the S&P 500 is making new all-time highs. To most investors, this sounds like confirmation that everything is fine. To Brad McFaten, it sounds like a warning.

"I've been baffled," McFaten admits during a recent episode of Capital. "You go back a few years—this sort of thing would have happened and you would have been on your knees. But it doesn't seem to want to go down."

The conventional explanation would point to Federal Reserve printing, dollar erosion, or simple confidence in American markets. But McFaten isn't buying it. "By that same token, you should also see the energy stocks move in tandem as well. Although they are at decent levels, they're not outperforming the general S&P. And beyond reasonable doubt, they should be."

## A False Rally Built on Thin Air

The disconnect between market prices and underlying reality has McFaten drawing uncomfortable parallels to history. "I've seen this before, Danny. I saw this back in—when was it? 2002. That's almost 25 years ago, back when we had the bounce from the 9/11 smackdown. The NASDAQ rose very significantly from September 11th right through until about March 2002. But that was then. And the downside there from March, April 2002 right through to October 2002 was huge. Not many folks talk about that, but that was nasty."

"I fear the same sort of thing we're staring down that barrel now," McFaten continues. "It's a false rally. I wouldn't believe in it."

Co-host Chris Minto offers additional perspective on why this rally has such strange legs. "What's that saying—that the market can remain irrational longer than you can stay solvent? I mean, to some degree, when you go back and think about the dot-coms, there were points in time where you'd go, 'What's going on? This doesn't make sense.' And yet the market continued higher."

The answer, Minto argues, lies in how capital is now allocated. "We've got capital largely now being run by passive managers. Most of this stuff is just algo-driven, and it's very much trend-following. So it's like one of those super tankers—difficult to turn around because they just sort of move very slowly."

When there's nowhere else to hide, even a bad-looking market can look relatively attractive. "If you're running—I don't know—$10 billion, where do you look?" Minto asks. "Europe looks like a mess. The GCC countries—you wouldn't necessarily want to be investing there right now. Europe, of course, we've known what's happening there. They've had problems with respect to the conflict with Russia for some time, and prior to that, they've had all manner of socialist problems, which the bureaucrats in Brussels just keep foisting on top of them—higher taxes, more regulation, yada yada—all of these things which are long-term poor for economics. So the North American market, on that basis, looks less bad than many other ones."

## The Bond Market Tells a Different Story

While equities ignore reality, the bond market is sending signals that shouldn't be ignored. "The bond market is always the smarter of the two," Minto notes. "If we look at the bond market, that does look problematic. Bonds are rolling over across the board. In fact, the only bond market in the world that's actually posting positive returns is China."

For McFaten, the bond market's message is unambiguous: "The market that makes all the sense in the world is the bond market. That we can understand. And that suggests that once the bond yields want to go way, way higher—"

The US 10-year yield has been consolidating for nearly two years, compressing into a range that McFaten sees as a coiled spring. "That should be the yield should be 5% already on the US 10-year. That's where it probably wouldn't make sense. But in terms of the price action, this is getting ready to go. Break out of some trading ranges, make up for lost time in a hurry. We're about to see a massive move in the bond market, bond yields to the upside."

## Private Credit's Warning Signs

Beyond public markets, the private equity and private credit markets are showing cracks that mainstream investors haven't begun to price in. "Private equity has now been gating," Minto reports. "There's over 20 different funds that have all gated now. And they won't give investors their capital back. The two are tied at the hip."

This matters because of what those funds were financing. "A lot of those private credit transactions were things in data centers," Minto explains. "I saw a report two days ago—something like 83% of all planned data center investments were on hold. When you translate that all the way back into this whole AI bubble, the front end of that train is these investments. And if they're all being halted, if they're saying, 'Well, we're concerned this doesn't make any sense. Maybe we don't have the energy to actually power these things. We're going to pull these data centers in'—then you can't power the damn things. So there's all of these consequences to the actions that have been taken, not just with this war but gosh, 10, 15 years ago with the lack of capital expenditure into the energy space."

The absurdity of AI valuations has reached dot-com-era heights. "You've got a company called Allbirds," Minto recalls. "What were they before they became an AI company? A shoe company. A shoe company. They ceased operations on shoes because they were literally broke. They did a $50 million convertible financing, switched the name to an AI stock, rebranded themselves as an AI company, and the shares quadrupled."

"It actually did way better than a quadruple at the high," McFaten adds. "It did almost a 10x from the bottom to the peak."

These aren't isolated anecdotes. They're symptoms of a bubble that's running on momentum and narrative rather than fundamentals.

## Energy: The Story Nobody Wants to Hear

If the S&P's behavior defies explanation, the energy sector's underperformance is equally baffling—with oil at $90-plus per barrel, the stocks that should benefit most are lagging. "Even when we look at energy stocks, which should be doing exceptionally well, they've done okay, but just—look at Exxon. They've done okay, but they're not really taking off. No one really believes this is long-term sustainable."

The great rotation McFaten and Minto have been predicting is happening, but slowly. "We've been talking about this great rotation into what we call the halo stocks—your typical miners and energy stocks outperforming relative to your IT stocks and your Magnificent 7 stocks. It's been happening, but at a very, very slow pace. Everyone just doesn't want to give up on this whole AI thing, the Magnificent 7 thing, the whole growth stock thing."

Yet the fundamentals for energy are compelling. Consider capacity utilization: "Drill ships sitting about 90% capacity. You know, 95% would be considered full capacity. It's essentially full capacity now. There's just not the facilities, the infrastructure, the resources that there once was to absorb all this increase in capex coming down the line."

ExxonMobil is investing $22 billion in West Africa. Shell and BP are doing the same. Who absorbs all that capital? "Exxon doesn't go and drill," McFaten points out. "Exxon doesn't do subsea service work. Exxon doesn't have OSVs. Exxon doesn't have FPSOs—not many, anyway. And that's where the money goes."

The energy services sector (tracked by ETFs like XES) sits near all-time highs, while the producers (XOP) have pulled back. "You bring up XES—look at a daily chart. It's not far from making new highs. Now look at XOP. It's popped up, but on a daily basis, it's pulled back. One we do hear people talking about bullish commentary on energy stocks—but that's limited to Exxon and Chevron, maybe Shell or Total. Very few are talking about the smaller-cap energy stocks. And no one's talking about oil and gas services."

McFaten's thesis is straightforward: "Pick the sector. That's a sector that's going to rocket. And it's rocketing. And no one's talking about it."

## The Middle East's Delayed Reckoning

The conflict in the Middle East is being treated by markets as a temporary inconvenience. McFaten disagrees strenuously. "No one's really believing that what's happening in the Middle East will be a game-changing event. A few months from now—pick a time—but not too far from now, this will all be over with, back to normal, business as usual. That's what the market is suggesting. But I think otherwise. I think it's a rather game-changing event."

The reason for optimism is that most Western countries have inventory to burn through. "All the petrol, diesel, jet fuel we're receiving is from tankers that had left the Straits of Hormuz or had passed through before the war broke out," McFaten explains. "That's inventory we've been surviving on up until now. That starts running out towards the end of April—which is gosh, getting very, very close. So we're probably going to see rationing of diesel particularly here in Australia within the next month."

This is just the beginning. The fertilizer supply chain is even more vulnerable. "You need sulfur to add to phosphate to create super phosphate—the typical grade most farmers would use. And 50% of the world's sulfur comes through the Straits of Hormuz."

Farmers won't plant crops they can't fertilize. Diesel shortages compound the problem. "That doesn't show up until 12 months later. Higher food prices and shortages of food is going to only show up towards the end of the year. Now, higher food prices—that's inflation. Combine that with energy prices still above $100 a barrel 12 months from now—that's a toxic thing. Interest rates much, much higher. That's not a good outcome for highly valued AI stocks."

Minto adds the geopolitical dimension: "Recall when the Arab Spring was—2011. When people start getting hungry, that's when the pitchforks come out. That's when revolutions start happening. And if we are heading towards food shortages, that's a natural consequence. And none of that's being factored in."

## Energy Nationalism: The Theme They Called Years Ago

Before the conflict, before the energy crisis, McFaten and Minto were writing about "energy nationalism" in their Capitalist Exploit Insider newsletter. Today, it looks prescient.

"When the whole ESG was the big thing," Minto recalls, "we said, 'Look, five years from now, the big thing will be energy nationalism.' And here we are."

Australia exemplifies the problem. "They had eight refineries at one stage. Now they're down to two. Now down to one, because one essentially blew up the last few days. We're only producing about 10-15% of the oil we produced 20 years ago. So we've been increasingly relying on oil coming from imported sources—South Korea, Singapore, some from Malaysia, and also China, which has now stopped."

The solution is obvious: develop domestic resources. "There's plenty of oil here. We could be self-sufficient. But now you're starting to get different states going, 'Hey, we've got all this oil here. We need to be starting to develop it.' And that's happening across multiple countries. More and more emphasis will be placed on developing oil and gas assets outside of the Straits of Hormuz."

There's also a refining problem that complicates the picture. "The US is a net energy exporter—except it exports the wet fuels and a lot of light sweet crude. But it's heavy crude that's used for the production of diesel, and diesel is what really makes the industrial economy operate. So the US imports Saudi oil and exports the light stuff to Asia. It's not as simple as people think."

## The Honest Answer on Geography

Host Danny posed a question that many Western investors are asking: with the West seemingly in decline, where do you go?

Minto's answer is unexpected but considered. "Broadly speaking, Latin America is well-positioned. It's because nobody trusts their government there. So when the government chooses to do something, the following from the populace to whatever ridiculous rules they might choose to put into place largely doesn't happen."

He contrasts this with Western attitudes: "The immediate response is, 'How do we get around this?' Because they've been doing it for 80 years. There's an internal resilience built into the populace. When I contrast that to people in the West, they're like, 'Oh, but what if I have to report this or do that.' The mindset is one of slavery for Westerners because they've never had to sort of stand up and go, 'Hang on a second. I'm going to do what I want to do.'"

McFaten offers a different perspective: "Rather than concentrating on where you want to live, concentrate on making money. It makes things a lot easier."

His key insight: "I can think of one sector where you don't have to worry about where you are geographically because it's a global sector. It's energy. Be it coal, be it oil and gas exploration, production, be it shipping, tankers, be it oil and gas services. That's what we concentrate on."

## Volatility Is Part of the Game

Both hosts caution against emotional reactions to short-term moves. "Markets don't go in a straight line," McFaten notes. "We had a run from $54 oil to over $100 in spot. It's entirely possible that we can have $75 oil in the next few months. But that's not the story. That's traders with leverage moving in and out of futures contracts. The end story is much more likely $100, $200-plus oil."

"You might wake up in two weeks and oil is trading at $75," Minto adds. "That doesn't mean you're wrong. It just means that's the volatility. People see wild swings and automatically go, 'Oh, I'm wrong, I'm getting out.' They let their emotions drive their decision-making. That's a big mistake."

The energy thesis isn't a trade. It's a structural shift that McFaten believes will play out over a decade. "We've been overweight energy-related sectors. We've done very, very well—probably doubled the performance of the S&P 500. But it's been rather frustrating because we still really haven't got liftoff. All the things we're looking at are just coming off the bottom. We think we've got a massive, decade-long tailwind behind us."

Former energy analyst Jeff Curry put it simply in one of his last public appearances: Ten years from now, Exxon will be the biggest stock in the world. Video will be a fraction of what it was. Complete reversal of what we've seen in the last 20 years.

"That's what no one expects," McFaten says. "We're out there as weirdos."

But in a market making record highs while the fundamentals deteriorate, being a contrarian might be the only rational position.
