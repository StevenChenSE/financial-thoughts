## Despite Iran Blockade and Oil Surge, Analyst Sees Buying Opportunities Amid Economic Strength

---

The announcement came on April 13th: President Trump declared a naval blockade of all vessels entering and leaving Iranian ports, warning that any Iranian fast attack ships in the region would be eliminated. The message was clear—the United States would not allow Iran to export oil to the world. Yet for Ed Yardeni, president of Yardeni Research and the analyst who coined the term "bond vigilantes" back in 1983, the dramatic escalation changed little about his fundamental market thesis.

"The markets have been remarkably stable in the face of all that," Yardeni observed during a recent appearance on the financial news program. "I think investors are concluding that this too shall pass, and they're looking beyond it."

This measured response from equity markets despite escalating Middle East tensions exemplifies what Yardeni sees as the defining characteristic of the current environment: unprecedented economic resilience. Having appeared on the program several times previously—with prior interviews linked for viewers seeking context—Yardeni has maintained his conviction that the S&P 500 had already bottomed on March 30th, a call that proved prescient as the following week delivered some of the index's largest single-week gains in recent memory.

**The Anatomy of an Oil Shock**

With oil prices hovering near $100 per barrel for WTI crude and geopolitical uncertainty lingering, Yardeni has developed a framework for understanding how oil supply shocks transmit through the economy—not as rigid stages, but as scenarios dependent on price levels and persistence.

Below $125 per barrel represents the first scenario, where the economy can largely absorb the impact. "In that range, I think the economy can absorb it, can grow, the bond yield might stay around current levels, and the Fed probably wouldn't do anything," Yardeni explained. "They'd have more inflation even if they see some slowing, but it's not going to be enough to cause them to ease."

Between $125 and $150, the shock has persisted long enough that growth fears begin to emerge alongside inflation concerns. Above $150, real demand destruction sets in, forcing the Federal Reserve to pivot dovish to support the economy.

"We're much less energy intensive now than we were during the stagflationary 1970s, where we got hit by two oil shocks that spread very quickly to a broader inflation problem," Yardeni noted, drawing a crucial distinction between then and now. "The economy is much more services-oriented, much more digital, less manufacturing overall. I think the economy is resilient enough—and that's been my theme since the beginning of what I call the roaring 2020s: don't underestimate the resilience of the economy."

**Earnings: The Bulls' Foundation**

Yardeni's $7,700 S&P 500 year-end target rests on solid earnings fundamentals. His base case assumes $310 per share in S&P 500 earnings this year, rising to $350 next year. What has surprised him, however, is that industry analysts have become even more optimistic than his own forecasts.

"Right now they're looking for $320 this year, and they're not looking for $350 next year—they're looking for $370," Yardeni said. "I don't know what's going on, but analysts the past several weeks have gotten more bullish on earnings in the face of the war. They know there's a war going on, but at the end of the day, they get a lot of their guidance from the companies, and the companies reported really great fourth-quarter earnings. It looks like first-quarter earnings are going to be up at least 12 percent."

The forward-looking estimates for the remaining quarters show double-digit increases expected once again. "Sometimes they tend to be too pessimistic about the latest quarter we're in," Yardeni added, suggesting the actual numbers could exceed these already-robust projections.

**Small Business Confidence vs. Corporate Earnings**

The NFIB small business optimism index has been declining since 2025, exacerbated by the Iran conflict. Yet Yardeni sees a disconnect between the dour sentiment among small businesses and the optimism emanating from corporate earnings calls.

"Optimism is a kind of ambiguous idea," he cautioned. "Are you optimistic or pessimistic about your specific business or the business environment? I'm a small entrepreneur. I've got my own business. If you ask me about things, I might say, 'Well, I used to be more optimistic, now I'm kind of leaning towards pessimistic—I don't like what I'm seeing geopolitically.' But how's your business? Oh, it's great, and I'm actually hiring some people."

This underscores why Yardeni pays more attention to the NFIB's labor market indicators—hiring plans and actual employment decisions—than to the headline optimism reading.

**The Inflation Question**

The March CPI rose 3.3 percent, the highest reading since May 2024, driven by a 21 percent surge in gasoline and oil prices on a month-over-month basis. The crucial question is whether higher oil prices translate into broader consumer goods inflation.

"We can go back to 2022 when oil prices soared when Russia invaded Ukraine, and there was certainly some pass-through, but there was also supply chain disruption, so it's not a clear analogy to the current situation," Yardeni observed. "Today, instead of supply chain disruptions, we've had the tariff effect, and that maybe is about to wear off. We have yet to see that it actually wears off."

Yardeni credits Trump's tariffs with preventing inflation from falling to the Fed's 2 percent target, keeping it stalled around 3 percent instead. "Trump's tariffs didn't raise inflation, but they kept it from coming down to 2 percent. I think we would have been down to 2 percent by now. The tariffs had a big impact on durable goods prices—they had been negative on a year-over-year basis, and now they're positive."

His base case relies heavily on productivity gains continuing to offset inflationary pressures. "In the roaring 2020s scenario, productivity just continues to get better and better, and productivity is a very powerful force in keeping inflation down."

**Bond Vigilantes: Awake in Japan, Sleeping in America**

While the two-year Treasury note yield has risen about 50 basis points in a single month—a move Yardeni attributes to tariff concerns rather than oil—longer-duration bonds have remained remarkably calm. This asymmetry raises the question of whether bond vigilantes have returned to punish fiscal profligacy.

"Two-year notes are not bonds, and the bond vigilantes seem to be actually kind of asleep at the switch," Yardeni said. "If you want to see where bond vigilantes are working hard to push yields up, it's clearly in Japan. It's been in the UK. But here in the US, the bond market's been real quiet."

Yardeni sees 4.25 to 4.75 percent as the likely range for the 10-year yield this year, with a possible move toward 4.75 percent representing "a tremendous buying opportunity." The previous encounter with bond vigilante pressure came in 2023 when yields jumped from 4 percent to 5 percent in just three months.

"I got concerned," Yardeni admitted. "But then Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen essentially announced that she was going to issue more in bills and not increase the issuance of bonds. She basically told the bond vigilantes, 'Okay, I get it. I'm not going to stress you out by issuing more bonds. I'm going into the T-bill market.' So the bond vigilantes are not the only players in the bond market. There's the Treasury, there's the Fed, foreign investors, domestic investors—there's a whole bunch of players, and it doesn't always add up in a way where the bond vigilantes call the shots."

When pressed on whether bonds or equities were the better investment for the year, Yardeni didn't hesitate: "Equities."

**The Gold Puzzle**

Gold has been something of an anomaly. Despite war, inflation concerns, ballooning deficits, and increased defense spending demands, the precious metal declined rather than rallying. Yardeni's explanation: Turkey.

"The war put a lot of downward pressure on the currencies of some emerging markets, particularly Turkey," he explained. "And apparently Turkey sold a lot of gold in an effort to prop up the country's currency. So that's one explanation."

Despite this setback, Yardeni maintains a constructive long-term view on gold. "Nothing goes straight up. It's actually much better if it doesn't go straight up. If it consolidates, if there's profit-taking, you get rid of the leverage in the trading, and then you find that lo and behold, there's still buyers. I think Chinese investors are still likely to be buyers because they've gotten killed in their investments in real estate. They've gotten whipsawed in the Chinese stock market. There's natural buyers in India—they've always been buyers of gold and silver."

Gold's appeal extends beyond fear-driven demand. "I think people who didn't have any gold in their portfolio now have put maybe 5 to 20 percent in gold, and maybe that's as an alternative to bonds. There certainly seems to be lots of demand for bonds—U.S. bonds, anyway."

**Rethinking Tech Overweight**

In December of last year, Yardeni ended a 15-year overweight position in big technology stocks, a significant tactical shift. At the time, technology and communication services represented 46 percent of the S&P 500's market capitalization.

"I just felt kind of uncomfortable telling people to overweight two sectors that already represented 46 percent of the S&P 500," Yardeni recalled. "If you just market-weighted them, you were at 46 percent. To tell people to overweight them just didn't make sense—that two sectors would continue to gobble up market cap weight and earnings share."

The call proved prescient until the war started, as the market began broadening out to the "S&P 493"—the index excluding the dominant technology names. When the Magnificent 7 stocks declined from a forward P/E of 32 to around 25, Yardeni reversed course.

"We said, 'You know what, they're cheap. They're great companies. Let's go back to market weight.' And that's worked out pretty well for us."

**The Global Trade Returns**

Yardeni also ended his overweight on U.S. equities relative to international markets. That call proved excellent as U.S. markets had their worst start to a year relative to global markets in decades. Yet with Asia now dependent on oil flowing through the Strait of Hormuz—and the United States far more energy-independent—the "U.S. versus world" trade seemed ripe for reversal.

"I was thinking about that, but before I could do anything about it, the markets decided to go right back into the go-global trade," Yardeni admitted. "Korea had an amazing V-shaped recovery. Taiwan—these are emerging markets, though they really should be developed markets—it's been no, the go-global trade is kind of back in fashion."

Rather than chase the rotation, Yardeni prefers to maintain longer-term convictions. "I don't do trading calls very well. Every now and then I have a good one, but I like to limit them to when I have some conviction about it. Other than that, I try to have kind of long-term convictions on things."

**Three Scenarios for the Decade**

Yardeni assigns subjective probabilities to his outlook: 60 percent for the "roaring 2020s" base case, 20 percent for an even better "meltup" scenario, and 20 percent for downside risks—down from the 35 percent recession probability he had assigned before the Iran conflict.

"I really kicked myself because it should have been obvious to all of us when the president was moving all that firepower into the Middle East," Yardeni said of his failure to anticipate the conflict. "He wasn't doing it just for show. It was show and tell, and if the show doesn't do it, then show and bomb, I guess."

The historical analogies he considers are instructive. "The 1920s would be everything just works out, continues to work out. The 1970s is that we get a bigger oil shock that's more persistent that actually forces the Fed to go vulgar—raise interest rates to levels that cause a recession to keep the energy shock from broadening out into the rest of inflation. An analogy I have yet to really talk about that crosses my mind is the 1930s, which was geopolitically just an uglier and uglier decade all around. I don't think it's going to be the 1930s. I don't think it's going to be the 1970s stagflationary environment."

**Looking Ahead**

With geopolitical tensions persisting but markets showing remarkable staying power, Yardeni's framework emphasizes looking through short-term noise toward underlying economic fundamentals. The producer price index releasing later in the week would offer another data point on whether oil prices are beginning to pass through to broader inflation.

"Clearly the energy components are going to be up a lot, and transportation inflation will also be up a lot," Yardeni anticipated. "But I think we probably aren't yet seeing a pass-through. Companies learn how to pass through costs so quickly—in 2022 they could probably do it again. And maybe that'll be the shocker: how quickly inflation does pass through and create at least a short-term problem."

For investors seeking to navigate these crosscurrents, Yardeni's research platform at yardenni.com offers daily updates on macroeconomic and geopolitical developments and their market implications. The thesis remains intact: the economy's resilience will continue to absorb shocks, earnings will remain buoyant, and the roaring 2020s will prove their skeptics wrong—though not without periodic bouts of volatility that historically have represented buying opportunities rather than reasons for retreat.

As Yardeni himself noted, drawing a parallel to World War II: "A few months after Pearl Harbor, the U.S. got its act together and took out a big chunk of the Japanese navy at Midway, and the market bottomed at that point—even though there was still a lot of war left. The market is a discounting mechanism. I think in the current situation, the market anticipates that one way or the other, the oil will get out, the commodities that the rest of the world needs will get out."
