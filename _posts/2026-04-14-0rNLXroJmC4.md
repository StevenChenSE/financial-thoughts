*Dr. Ed Yardi, president of Yardani Research, joins the program to discuss the US naval blockade of Iranian ports, oil market dynamics, and his bullish outlook for equities in the remainder of 2025.*

---

On April 13th, President Trump announced a naval blockade of all vessels entering and exiting Iranian ports, warning that any Iranian fast attack ships in the area would be eliminated. The declaration sent ripples through global energy markets, yet financial markets have shown remarkable stability in the face of escalating geopolitical tensions.

Dr. Ed Yardi, president of Yardani Research, recently spoke with the program to provide his assessment of the rapidly evolving situation and its implications for investors.

## Markets Are Looking Beyond the Crisis

Despite the heightened tensions in the Persian Gulf, Yardi maintains his constructive view on equities—a position he staked out weeks earlier when he called a bottom for the S&P 500 on March 30th.

"The market is a discounting mechanism," Yardi explained. "I think in the current situation, the market anticipates that one way or the other, the oil will get out. The commodities that the rest of the world needs will get out."

The market's resilience mirrors its behavior during previous geopolitical shocks, including Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine. "A lot of angst about whether there'd be a food shortage because grain couldn't get through the Black Sea—and somehow or other, everything kind of unfolded in a way that brought these commodity prices down," Yardi noted. "The world economy and financial markets have learned to live with this horrible war that Russia inflicted on Ukraine."

He draws a parallel to World War II: "A few months after Pearl Harbor, the US got its act together and took out a big chunk of the Japanese navy at Midway, and the market bottomed at that point. There was still a lot of war left, and yet the market did bottom early on."

Yardi acknowledged the difficulty of investing based on presidential rhetoric. "You can't really invest based on what the president says day by day. It's a good way to lose your investment mind because things change so fast. He's speaking to a lot of audiences at the same time. He's clearly trying to convince the Iranians that he'll blast them to smithereens if they don't cooperate. And then when the markets don't cooperate with what he's doing, he tones it down, and then the market rallies on that."

## S&P 500 Target: $7,700

Yardi remains committed to his year-end S&P 500 target of 7,700, based on earnings of $310 per share for 2025 and $350 per share for 2026. What has surprised him is that industry analysts have grown *more* bullish despite the conflict.

"They're looking not for $310—they're looking for $320 this year, and they're not looking for $350 next year. They're looking for $370," Yardi observed. "I was actually thinking of raising my earnings estimate before the war. Then because of the war, I held back and kept them where they are. Meanwhile, I'm watching the analysts who maybe didn't get the memo about the war having a negative impact on the US economy and earnings. And so far, there's not much evidence that that's actually the case."

Fourth-quarter earnings came in strong, and first-quarter earnings are expected to show at least 12% growth. More importantly, analysts are forecasting double-digit earnings increases for the second, third, and fourth quarters of 2025.

## A Three-Stage Oil Shock Framework

Yardi outlined a framework describing how oil supply shocks move through bond yield curves and the broader economy:

**Stage 1 ($100-$125 per barrel):** Inflation fears dominate, but the Fed remains cautious. "The economy can absorb it. The bond yield might stay around current levels. The Fed probably wouldn't do anything in that kind of scenario," Yardi said.

**Stage 2 ($125-$150):** The shock persists long enough that growth fears begin to emerge alongside inflation concerns.

**Stage 3 ($150+):** Real demand destruction occurs, forcing the Fed to shift to a dovish stance.

"We're currently in Stage 1, which is just not even where we're at currently," Yardi clarified. "I think the US economy can actually handle $100-a-barrel oil. We are much less energy-intensive now than we were during the stagflationary 1970s, where we got hit by two oil shocks that spread very quickly to a broader inflation problem."

The modern economy is "much more services, much more digital, less manufacturing," he added. "I think the economy is resilient enough—and that has been my theme since the beginning of what I call the roaring 2020s: don't underestimate the resilience of the economy."

## Recession Odds Cut to 20%

Yardi lowered his recession probability estimate from 35% to 20% following the April 7th market recovery. "It was pretty clear that Trump wants to get out of there," he said. "Doesn't mean there won't be some lingering forces, enforcing a blockade. But I don't think it's going to be the kind of firepower we have there now."

His base case assigns 60% probability to the "roaring 2020s" scenario, with 20% allocated to a potential melt-up and 20% to downside risks—down from the 35% he previously assigned to recession risks.

"If things go badly again, I won't hesitate to say it's getting worse than I thought," Yardi acknowledged. "We're looking at the fog of war. I don't feel comfortable being a military strategist trying to predict how this all plays out, because even military strategists don't really anticipate how things go."

Historical analogies inform his thinking: "The 1920s would be everything just works out and continues to work out. The 1970s is that we get a big shock, a more persistent oil shock that forces the Fed to go Volcker and raise interest rates to levels that cause a recession. The 1930s was geopolitically just an uglier and uglier decade all around."

Yardi doesn't anticipate either the 1970s or 1930s scenario playing out. "But that scenario I would put in the 20% bucket of everything that could go wrong."

## Inflation: More Persistent Than Expected?

The March CPI report showed prices rising 3.3% year-over-year—the highest reading since May 2024—driven by a 21% surge in gasoline and oil on a month-to-month basis.

Yardi sees Trump's tariffs as a primary culprit for keeping inflation elevated. "Trump's tariffs didn't raise inflation, but they kept it from coming down to 2%. I think we would have been down to 2% by now. Where Trump's tariffs had a big impact was on durable goods prices. Those prices had been negative on a year-over-year basis. Now they're positive."

He's somewhat surprised that bond yields haven't risen more given the inflation picture. "I think inflation is going to be a little stickier than the markets are thinking. I'm kind of surprised that the bond yield hasn't gone up more, but I think it still could."

That said, he remains open-minded. "In the roaring 2020s scenario, productivity just continues to get better and better, and productivity is a very powerful force in keeping inflation down."

The NFIB small business optimism index has declined since 2025, exacerbated by the Iran conflict, but Yardi doesn't put too much weight on this indicator. "Optimism is a kind of ambiguous idea. Are you optimistic or pessimistic about your specific business or the business environment?" he asked. "I pay more attention to their labor market indicators—are they hiring or not?—and that's a little bit more useful."

## Bond Vigilantes: Sleeping at the Switch

The two-year Treasury yield has risen approximately 50 basis points in a single month, yet longer-term yields remain contained. Yardi, who coined the term "bond vigilantes" in 1983, doesn't see them active in the US market.

"They are in the two-year notes, but two-year notes are not bonds. The bond vigilantes seem to be actually kind of asleep at the switch," he said. "If you want to see where bond vigilantes are working hard to push yields up, it's clearly in Japan. It's been in the UK. But here in the US, the bond market's been real quiet."

He continues to expect yields in the 4.25% to 4.75% range but views any move toward 4.75% as "a tremendous buying opportunity."

"There's still a safe-haven bid for bonds. People are buying bonds because in a volatile world, if they just hold them for 10 years, they know exactly what they're going to get."

Yardi also notes that the world has become wealthier, creating natural rebalancing flows. "As people make a lot of money in the stock markets around the world, they start to get nervous and say, maybe I should take some of my profits and put it into bonds, maybe even into gold."

Recent Treasury auctions for two, five, and seven-year notes have drawn weaker demand, forcing yields higher than expected—a contrast to March's record-breaking 30-year auction. Yardi doesn't see this as a structural concern. "The bond vigilantes are not the only players in the bond market. There's the Treasury, there's the Fed, foreign investors, domestic investors. There's a whole bunch of players, and it doesn't always add up in a way where the bond vigilantes call the shots."

## Gold's Mysterious Correction

Gold has struggled despite the Iran conflict and rising inflation—a puzzle Yardi attributes to Turkey's currency pressures.

"The war put a lot of downward pressure on the currencies of some emerging markets, particularly Turkey," he explained. "And apparently, Turkey sold a lot of gold in an effort to prop up the country's currency."

Despite the near-term weakness, Yardi maintains a bullish long-term view. "Nothing goes straight up. It's actually much better if it doesn't go straight up. If it consolidates, if there's profit-taking, you get rid of the leverage in the trading, and then you find that lo and behold, there's still buyers."

He sees natural demand from Chinese investors, who "have gotten killed in their investments in real estate" and "whipsawe[d] in the Chinese stock market," as well as Indian buyers, who have "always been buyers of gold and silver."

"I think the trend is still higher for gold," Yardi said. "If my base case scenario of getting to 10,000 on the S&P 500 by the end of the decade works out, I think the price of gold could get to $10,000 on rebalancing—people making so much money in the stock market that they want to rebalance into gold and into bonds."

He estimates that investors who previously held no gold have now allocated 5% to 20% of their portfolios to the metal—potentially as a bond alternative.

## Rotating Out of Tech Overweight

In December 2024, Yardi ended a 15-year overweight position in large-cap technology and rotated toward international equities. This call proved prescient as the S&P 500 experienced its worst start to a year relative to global markets in years.

"I felt kind of uncomfortable telling people to overweight two sectors that already represented 46% of the S&P 500," Yardi said. "If you just market-weighted them, you were at 46%. To tell people to overweight them just didn't make sense—two sectors continuing to gobble up market cap weight and earnings share."

When the Magnificent 7 stocks declined from a forward P/E of 32 to approximately 25, he moved back to market weight, a decision that "worked out pretty well."

The Iran conflict has accelerated a market broadening, with the S&P 493 outperforming the mega-cap technology names. Meanwhile, the "go global" trade has staged a comeback, with Korea experiencing an "amazing V-shaped recovery" and Taiwan also rebounding sharply.

"I'm going to stay with the go global trade," Yardi said. "I don't do trading calls very well. Every now and then I have a good one, but I like to limit them to when I have some conviction about it. Other than that, I try to have long-term convictions on things."

## The Week Ahead

Looking to the week of April 14th, Yardi highlighted the Producer Price Index report as the key data release.

"That'll be another kind of indicator of the extent to which the war in March affected inflation in March in the PPI," he said. "Clearly, the energy components are going to be up a lot, and transportation inflation will also be up a lot. But I think we probably aren't yet seeing a pass-through. Companies learn how to pass through price increases so quickly—in 2022, they could probably do it again. Maybe that'll be the shocker: how quickly inflation does pass through and create at least a short-term problem."

## Bottom Line: Equities Over Bonds

When asked to choose between an overweight in bonds or equities for the remainder of 2025, Yardi didn't hesitate: "Equities."
