*The renowned market strategist discusses the Iran blockade, oil price thresholds, and why he remains bullish on equities despite rising geopolitical tensions*

---

On April 13th, financial markets faced a significant new development as President Trump announced a naval blockade of the Strait of Hormuz, warning that any Iranian fast attack ships in the area would be eliminated. The move effectively restricted oil exports from one of the world's most critical shipping channels. Joining CNBC to cut through the headlines and assess what this means for investors was Dr. Ed Yardeni, president of Yardeni Research and the economist who coined the term "bond vigilantes."

Despite the dramatic escalation, Dr. Yardeni remained remarkably composed—and bullish.

## The Market's Resilience: Learning to Look Beyond the Headlines

The S&P 500 had just experienced one of its strongest single-week rallies in recent memory, a remarkable rebound that followed what Dr. Yardeni characterized as an important contrarian signal.

"March 30th—it was a Monday—the market took a big dive, and then on Tuesday it rebounded dramatically when there was talk about the president looking for an exit strategy," Dr. Yardeni explained. "Sure enough, Wednesday night he gave a speech in which he said it could last another two to three weeks and the US won. If he had to declare victory and walk away, that was okay with him. And he's basically repeated that again recently."

This pattern—that when markets don't cooperate with presidential posturing, the rhetoric tones down and markets rally—has become a consistent feature of the current environment, according to Yardeni. More significantly, he sees investors increasingly discounting geopolitical noise altogether.

"You can't really invest based on what the president says day by day. It's a good way to lose your investment mind because things change so fast," he said. "He's speaking to a lot of audiences at the same time. He's clearly trying to convince the Iranians that he'll blast them to smithereens if they don't cooperate, and then when the markets don't cooperate with what he's doing, he tones it down."

The result? "I think investors are concluding that this too shall pass, and they're looking beyond it. And I agree with that view."

To underscore his point, Dr. Yardeni invoked one of the most dramatic periods in American history: "Geopolitical crises have always been buying opportunities. A few months after Pearl Harbor, the US got its act together and took out a big chunk of the Japanese navy at Midway. The market bottomed at that point—there was still a lot of war left in World War II, and yet the market did bottom early on."

## A Three-Stage Framework for Oil Price Shocks

With oil prices climbing toward $100 per barrel WTI, Dr. Yardeni outlined a framework he developed to assess how different oil price levels might impact the economy and markets:

**Stage One ($100–$125):** The current zone. "The economy can absorb it, can grow. The bond yield might stay around current levels. The Fed probably wouldn't do anything in that kind of scenario because they'd have more inflation, but even if they see some slowing, it's not going to be enough to cause them to ease."

**Stage Two ($125–$150):** The shock persists long enough that growth fears begin to emerge.

**Stage Three ($150+):** Real demand destruction sets in, and the Fed turns dovish.

"I don't think it's a framework—it's really descriptive of how the bond market is responding to oil prices," Dr. Yardeni cautioned. "It's kind of hypothetical. I don't have an econometric model. It's really just a way of conveying how I think the economy will respond to different price levels. It should have been called scenario one, two, three."

At current levels, Yardeni believes the US economy can handle elevated oil prices far better than it could during the stagflationary 1970s. "We are much less energy-intensive now than we were during the stagflationary 1970s, where we got hit by two oil shocks that spread very quickly to a broader inflation problem. The economy is much more services, more digital, less manufacturing. All in all, I think the economy is resilient enough, which has been my theme since the beginning of what I call the roaring 2020s—don't underestimate the resilience of the economy."

## The Bull Case Remains Intact: S&P 7,700

Dr. Yardeni maintains his year-end S&P 500 target of 7,700, based on earnings of $310 per share this year and $350 next year. What's striking is that he's now more optimistic than his own forecast—industry analysts have become even more bullish, projecting $320 this year and $370 next year.

"I'm amazed to see that industry analysts are even more optimistic than I thought I was," Dr. Yardeni noted. "The analysts read the headlines the way we all do. They know there's a war going on, but at the end of the day, they get a lot of their guidance from the companies. And the companies reported really great fourth-quarter earnings. It looks like first-quarter earnings are going to be up at least 12%, and for the second, third, and fourth quarters, analysts are looking for double-digit increases again. So they're very upbeat."

He added with a touch of irony: "I was actually thinking of raising my earnings estimate before the war. Then because of the war, I held back and kept them where they are. Meanwhile, I'm watching the analysts who maybe didn't get the memo about the war having a negative impact on the US economy. And so far, there's not much evidence that that's actually the case."

## Recession Odds: From 35% to 20%

In late March, with markets swooning and geopolitical tensions mounting, Dr. Yardeni's Denny.com subscribers received a notable update: he lowered his recession probability estimate from 35% to 20%.

The shift came after Trump's signals that he was seeking an exit from the conflict. "When people get extremely bearish, policy makers get the message. The policy response that developed was this ceasefire we kind of moved toward. Trump announcing that he was aiming for two to three more weeks—it was pretty clear that he wants to get out of there."

He acknowledged, however, that the escalation to an actual blockade was somewhat surprising in hindsight. "I really—when people asked me what they were, I wrote down... I mean, I'm kicking myself because it should have been obvious to all of us when the president was moving all that firepower into the Middle East. He wasn't doing it just for show."

## The Bull-Bear Spectrum: Where Bears Go Wrong

For investors sitting on the bearish end of the spectrum, Dr. Yardeni identified the key assumption they're making that he disagrees with: an analogy to the 1970s.

"If you want to take historical analogies, the 1920s would be everything just works out and continues to work out. The 1970s is that we get a big oil shock that's more persistent that actually forces the Fed to go Volcker and raise interest rates to levels that cause a recession to keep the energy shock from broadening out into the rest of inflation. And then an analogy I have yet to really talk about that crosses my mind is the 1930s, which was geopolitically just an uglier and uglier decade all around."

"I don't think it's going to be the 1930s. I don't think it's going to be the stagflationary 1970s environment. That scenario I would put in the 20% bucket of everything that could go wrong."

His base case, which he assigns a 60% subjective probability, remains the "roaring 2020s"—continued economic growth and market appreciation. Another 20% goes to an even better "meltup" scenario, leaving 20% for adverse outcomes.

## Inflation: The 3.3% Surprise and What Comes Next

The March CPI report, released last week, showed prices rising 3.3% year-over-year—the highest reading since May 2024—driven by a staggering 21% month-over-month surge in gasoline and oil prices. The question is whether this feeds through to broader consumer prices.

"That's a good question, and I don't know the answer," Dr. Yardeni admitted. "We can go back to 2022 when oil prices soared when Russia invaded Ukraine. There was certainly some pass-through, but there was also supply chain disruption, so it's not a clear analogy."

"Today, instead of supply chain disruptions, we've had the tariff effect, and that maybe is about to wear off. We have yet to see that. But we don't have the inflationary background that we had in 2022. So maybe this will be a more modest, shorter-lived inflation spike."

He's staying flexible on this front. "I think there's too much consensus that 'this too shall pass,' and I am somewhat concerned it may take longer. Then again, in the roaring 2020s scenario, productivity just continues to get better and better, and productivity is a very powerful force in keeping inflation down."

Looking ahead to the week's data, Dr. Yardeni flagged the Producer Price Index as particularly important. "The PPI will be another indicator of the extent to which the war in March affected inflation. Clearly, the energy components are going to be up a lot, and transportation inflation will also be up a lot. But I think we probably aren't yet seeing a pass-through. Companies learn how to pass through price increases so quickly—in 2022, they could probably do it again. Maybe that'll be the shocker: how quickly inflation does pass through and create at least a short-term problem."

## The Bond Market: Vigilantes Sleeping, Except in Japan

The two-year Treasury yield has risen about 50 basis points in a single month, but the long end of the curve has remained remarkably calm. For Dr. Yardeni—the man who coined "bond vigilantes" in 1983—this dichotomy is instructive.

"They are in the two-year notes, but two-year notes are not bonds," he clarified. "And the bond vigilantes seem to be actually kind of asleep at the switch. If you want to see where bond vigilantes are working hard to push yields up, it's clearly in Japan. It's been in the UK. But here in the US, the bond market's been real quiet."

He's maintained a 4.25% to 4.75% target for 10-year yields and still believes they could reach 4.75%. "But I would view that as a tremendous buying opportunity. I don't know if we'll get an opportunity the way we did in 2023 to buy them at 5%."

The last time bond vigilantes truly made their presence felt in the US was late 2023, when yields spiked from 4% to 5% in just three months. "I got concerned. But then just as I was getting too concerned, the bond market started coming down rather sharply. At 5%, people started talking about 6% and a debt crisis. I said I'll worry about a debt crisis when the bond market worries about it."

The turning point came from an unexpected source: Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen. "She basically told the bond vigilantes, 'Okay, I get it. I'm not going to stress you out by issuing more bonds. I'm going into the T-bill market.' So the bond vigilantes are not the only players in the bond market. There's the Treasury, there's the Fed, foreign investors, domestic investors. There's a whole bunch of players, and it doesn't always add up in a way where the bond vigilantes call the shots."

## Gold's Mysterious Weakness and the Case for 10,000

Despite geopolitical tensions, inflation concerns, and expanding deficits, gold hasn't staged its anticipated rally. Dr. Yardeni had an explanation—and it came from an unexpected quarter.

"I was kind of scratching my head, wondering: you have the war, you have inflation as a result, you have bigger deficits as a result, more pressure to increase defense spending, and gold goes down. Apparently, the story is that the war put a lot of downward pressure on the currencies of some emerging markets, particularly Turkey. And apparently, Turkey sold a lot of gold in an effort to prop up the country's currency."

He viewed the consolidation positively: "Nothing goes straight up. It's actually much better if it doesn't go straight up. If it consolidates, if there's profit-taking, you get rid of the leverage in the trading, and then lo and behold, there are still buyers. I think Chinese investors are still likely to be buyers because they've gotten killed in their investments in real estate. They've gotten whipsawed in the Chinese stock market. There's natural buyers in India. We've seen around the world people buying ETFs like GLD."

As for where gold is headed, Yardeni offered a bold projection: "If my base case scenario of getting to 10,000 on the S&P 500 by the end of the decade works out, I think the price of gold could get to $10,000 on rebalancing—people making so much money in the stock market that they want to rebalance into gold and into bonds."

He also addressed whether gold is replacing bonds in investor portfolios: "People who didn't have any gold in their portfolio now have put maybe 5 to 20% in gold. Maybe that's as an alternative to bonds. But there certainly seems to be lots of demand for bonds—US bonds, anyway."

## Bonds vs. Equities: The Clear Answer

When pressed to choose between bonds and equities for the remainder of the year, Dr. Yardeni didn't hesitate: **equities**.

## Exiting Big Tech: A 15-Year Overweight Ends

In December of the previous year, Dr. Yardeni made a significant tactical shift, ending a 15-year overweight position in big technology stocks. He also shifted to underweight the Magnificent 7—at a time when their forward P/E ratio stood at 32.

"I just felt kind of uncomfortable telling people to overweight two sectors that already represented 46% of the market capitalization of the S&P 500," he explained. "If you just market-weighted them, you're at 46%. To tell people to overweight them just didn't make sense—that two sectors would continue to gobble up market cap weight and earnings share."

The call proved prescient, though not for the reason he expected. "That turned out to be a pretty good recommendation until the war started, because the market was broadening out to the S&P 493 and was going increasingly global. Actually, I was late on the go-global call. It was already happening last year, but it took me a while to change my mind."

When the Magnificent 7's valuations compressed to around 25 times forward earnings, Yardeni reversed course: "We said, 'You know what, they're cheap. They're great companies. Let's go back to market weight.' And that's worked out pretty well for us."

## The Return of the Go-Global Trade

His call to overweight US equities versus the rest of the world—known as the "stay home" trade—had been one of his most successful calls. But that's now shifting.

"I was thinking about changing that view, given that Asia depends on oil coming out of the Strait of Hormuz, whereas the West and the US particularly don't really need it," he acknowledged. "But before I could do anything about it, the markets decided to go right back into the go-global trade. Korea had an amazing V-shaped recovery. Taiwan—these are emerging markets, though they really should be developed markets. The go-global trade is kind of back in fashion."

He's staying with it rather than trying to time tactical shifts: "I don't do trading calls very well. Every now and then I have a good one, but I like to limit them to when I have some conviction about it. Other than that, I try to have long-term convictions on things."

## Small Business Pessimism vs. Corporate Optimism

The NFIB Small Business Optimism Index has been declining since 2025, exacerbated by the Iran conflict. Yet corporate earnings calls tell a different story. Is there a disconnect?

"Optimism is a kind of ambiguous idea," Dr. Yardeni observed. "Are you optimistic or pessimistic about your specific business or the business environment? I don't know if they're asking them about that distinction. Uncertainty is clearly going to weigh on confidence. But at the same time, I'm a small entrepreneur. I've got my own business. If you ask me about things, I might say, 'Well, I used to be more optimistic, now I'm kind of leaning towards pessimistic. I don't like what I'm seeing geopolitically.' But how's your business? Oh, it's great. And I'm actually hiring some people."

"That's why I don't pay that much attention to that particular index. I pay more attention to their labor market indicators—are they hiring or not? That's a little bit more useful in thinking about the labor market."

## The Bottom Line

For investors wondering how to navigate these turbulent times, Dr. Yardeni's prescription is straightforward: maintain conviction in the underlying resilience of the economy and corporate earnings, stay diversified with assets like gold that provide portfolio insurance, and resist the temptation to make wholesale changes based on daily geopolitical developments.

"Geopolitical crises have always been buying opportunities," he concluded. "The market is a discounting mechanism. I think in the current situation, the market anticipates that one way or the other, the oil will get out, the commodities that the rest of the world needs will get out."
