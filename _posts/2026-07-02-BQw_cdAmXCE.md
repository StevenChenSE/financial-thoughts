## Forget Geopolitics—Earnings Are the Only Thing That Matters

After 30 years of investment management experience, Chris Gallipo, head market strategist at Franklin Templeton, has developed a clear philosophy: the stock market doesn't care about geopolitics, politics, or media hype. It cares about earnings—and earnings alone.

"The stock market doesn't care about geopolitics. The stock market doesn't care about politics. The stock market cares about earnings," Gallipo explains. This fundamental principle, he argues, explains why markets recover so consistently from crisis events. When examining historical data going back to the Great Depression of 1929, every major geopolitical event—wars, terrorist attacks, financial crises, even presidential assassinations—has resulted in the S&P 500 gaining an average of 12% over the following 12 months with a 75% success rate.

The reason is straightforward: stocks follow earnings over time. When Q1 saw market concern about Middle East tensions, the market traded down 8-9%, only to rally over 20% once investors recognized Q1 earnings would be strong. "The market knows Q1 earnings are going to be good," Gallipo noted. "It rallies and doesn't look back."

## The Market Is Broader and Stronger Than You Think

Despite recent rotation from tech stocks into defensive sectors, Gallipo doesn't believe this represents a flight from risk. Instead, he sees a healthy correction in an otherwise robust market. The evidence is compelling: equal-weight S&P, midcap indices, Russell 2000, and Russell 1000 value are all hitting new all-time highs simultaneously.

"We've got new all-time high equal weight S&P, new all-time high midcap, new all-time high Russell 2000, new all-time high Russell 1000 value," Gallipo emphasized. "Markets broad, market strong."

The rotation he's observing reflects something more fundamental than fear: profit-taking in extended positions. Semiconductor stocks, which have seen parabolic moves, are experiencing necessary consolidation. "They need to take a break. Fundamentals are solid, but they need to take a break," Gallipo stated.

Meanwhile, the Mag 7 stocks that dominated from 2020 to 2024 are now lagging the broader market by 12%, creating potential opportunity. These stocks have "probably bombed out, sold, pulled out" and "probably should rally."

## Q2 Earnings Look Strong—But Know When to Take Profits

Looking ahead to Q2 earnings season, Gallipo expects continued strength. Q1 delivered 25% year-over-year earnings growth—double what consensus estimates predicted. Q2 estimates are tracking above 15% year-over-year, with banks set to report resilient consumers, improving loan books, and robust M&A pipelines.

Companies across sectors will likely emphasize AI's role in boosting productivity and profitability. Major firms including Walmart, Home Depot, and JP Morgan have already demonstrated measurable efficiency gains from AI implementation. Amazon and Google showed proof that capex spending is beginning to drive revenue acceleration.

However, Gallipo warns against assuming strong earnings guarantee stock price appreciation. The Broadcom example proves this point: despite reporting $22 billion in revenue (up 48% year-over-year) and AI semiconductor revenue jumping 143%, the stock fell after the report.

"The stock market is a discounting mechanism. It knows," Gallipo explained. When good news is already priced in, investors sell the news rather than buy it. "Dock up big in advance of the news, you get the good news, people take money off the table."

The same principle applies to semiconductor stocks, which Gallipo believes are overextended despite solid fundamentals. Micron recently reported "one of the strongest reports I'd ever seen" on every line item, yet investors should ask: "What's the next catalyst to make that stock move another 20% higher?"

## Don't Fight the Fed—But Don't Overreact to Data Points

The Federal Reserve's approach has evolved under new leadership. Rather than reacting to individual monthly data points, the Fed is adopting a trend-based strategy. Gallipo's forecast for core PCE inflation this year is 3.3%—matching the Fed's own guidance.

Two key indicators inform Gallipo's view of Fed policy: two-year Treasury yields and break-even inflation rates. Currently, two-year yields sit about 50 basis points above the effective federal funds rate, suggesting the market prices in potential rate hikes by December. However, break-even rates (the bond market's inflation expectations) tell a different story—they've collapsed from 5.5% in March to 1.5% today.

This divergence creates uncertainty. "This conflict—two-year note yield saying, 'Hey, you should raise rates.' And break-even rates saying, 'Hey, don't do anything'—is a little bit odd," Gallipo admitted. His expectation: the Fed does nothing in the near term, maintaining its current stance.

The recent jobs data supports this patient approach. JOLTS data and ISM reports show a strong labor market with readings north of 50. Gallipo expects the upcoming jobs report around 115,000 new positions—strong enough to maintain economic momentum without overheating.

## The US Economy Remains the World's Benchmark

Despite concerns about America's debt load and warnings from prominent investors like Jeff Gundlach about "bond vigilantes," Gallipo remains unconvinced by the bear case. Treasury auctions show no meaningful demand softness, and foreign investors aren't fleeing US assets despite geopolitical tensions.

"The European economy has never really grown. There's not a lot of earnings growth there," Gallipo noted, explaining why European stocks trade at lower multiples. The US combines the world's best management teams, innovation leadership, and an active Fed that has supported economic growth.

"I've heard the predicted demise of the US and the debasement of the dollar and no one's going to buy US assets my entire career. It has never happened," Gallipo stated.

## What Would Change This Outlook?

For Gallipo to shift toward a more defensive stance, two scenarios would trigger action. First, if the Fed begins raising rates quickly and signals a new rate hike cycle, that would "thwart aggregate demand, put downward pressure on GDP, and start to slow the economy." In that environment, the playbook shifts to growth stocks—ironically, the same defensive positioning that worked during the 2020-2024 period.

Second, if the S&P reached 8,000 with a multiple of 24 and analysts predicted peak EPS growth rate-of-change, valuation concerns would become legitimate. Currently, the S&P trades at a lower multiple than January 1st despite being up 10% in the first half of 2025—all driven by earnings expansion rather than multiple expansion.

"The entire move in 2025 is all earnings driven. All of it," Gallipo emphasized.

## Lessons From Three Decades of Investing

After literally making every mistake possible as an analyst and portfolio manager, Gallipo distills his experience into actionable advice:

- Block out noise, trend lines, and headlines
- Don't fall victim to doomsday narratives
- Focus on stock market valuation and earnings picture
- Understand your companies and catalysts
- Be objective and open-minded about changing your view

On the question of whether expensive stocks should be shorted, Gallipo offers clear guidance: "There's a big difference" between shorting and simply staying away. For those who do trade, "use stop-loss orders, good till canceled. That will protect your capital."

## The Bottom Line

The market isn't on the verge of collapse. Earnings remain strong, the economy is resilient, and the Fed has room to remain patient. Semiconductors may need consolidation after parabolic moves, while the Mag 7 laggards could surprise to the upside.

As for those predicting doom: "The data that the government gets and gives to us is useless. It's rearview mirror," Gallipo said. "Using macro sort of work to think you're going to be accurate in the stock market, to me, is a non-starter."

The real opportunity lies in staying focused on fundamentals, maintaining discipline during volatility, and having your shopping list ready for inevitable pullbacks. "It's a midterm year. We'll get some volatility this summer. Be patient."
