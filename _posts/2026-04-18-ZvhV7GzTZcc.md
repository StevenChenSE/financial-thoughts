## The April preliminary consumer sentiment index published by the University of Michigan has fallen to 47.6—the lowest level in the survey's 74-year history. But what's actually happening, and how concerned should we be?

The numbers are stark. For the first time since Joanne Shu began tracking consumer attitudes in her role as director of surveys of consumers at the University of Michigan, Americans are feeling worse about the economy than they did during the 2008 financial crisis, worse than during COVID-19, and roughly on par with the 1980s recession. Yet we're not in a financial crisis. This disconnect raises an urgent question: what exactly is weighing on consumer minds?

"Consumers have been loud and clear over the last few years that their number one factor weighing on them is the persistence of high prices," Shu explains. "And of course what happened in March and April—it's the war in Iran. Consumers are very concerned that the developments, those geopolitical developments, are not going to help on the inflation front. In fact, they believe it's going to make it considerably worse."

The timing matters. Consumer sentiment had been relatively low for more than a year, essentially treading water after reaching a trough at the height of the tariff developments from April's "liberation day" announcements. While sentiment improved somewhat afterward, those gains didn't hold. The latest shock from the Middle East conflict has pushed already-fragile attitudes to unprecedented lows.

---

## The Stock Market Puzzle

Here's what's puzzling analysts: while consumers express historic pessimism, the S&P 500 is rebounding toward new all-time highs. When stock prices rise, it typically signals that investors expect earnings and profits to improve—either through cost cuts or increased demand from consumers. So how do you reconcile optimistic markets with pessimistic shoppers?

Shu offers a nuanced answer that reveals a growing divide in American economic experience. "What's going on with consumers and analysts is not really quite the same thing with stock markets," she says. "That's actually something that some consumers do pay attention to. When we look at consumers with large stock portfolios, over the last few years, their sentiment has improved from troughs. They recovered very quickly from the liberation day tariffs."

The divergence becomes sharp when you look at wealth distribution. "People at the bottom of the wealth distribution, they are the ones who are really pulling sentiment down because they're just really weighed down by these high prices."

Research on the wealth effect supports this interpretation. While wealthier individuals with substantial portfolios may feel secure enough to spend freely, lower-income households don't benefit from rising stock prices at all. "We do know that wealthier people generate a disproportionate share of spending," Shu notes, "and that's one of the reasons why the weakening consumer might be a little bit harder to see in the aggregate data."

The pattern repeats in housing markets, which are closely tied to consumer confidence. Consumers without substantial assets simply aren't experiencing the economy the same way as those with portfolios and property.

## Why This Reading Is Different—And Why the Comparison May Be Misleading

The instinctive reaction to record-low sentiment is to ask: how does this compare to previous crises? But Shu cautions against simple historical comparisons. "The world has changed quite a bit over that period," she says. "The fundamental questions that are part of the index related to personal finances, business conditions, and buying conditions for durable goods both in the present as well as the future—those questions have not changed during this entire period."

What has changed is the methodology. The survey began as face-to-face interviews in the 1940s, moved to landlines, then cell phones, and now relies on web-based interviews as response rates have shifted with changing cultural norms. "We periodically needed to refresh our methodology in accordance with changing technology," Shu explains.

More significantly, the pandemic disrupted many traditional economic relationships. "With the pandemic, we had a lot of traditional economic relationships kind of fall apart," Shu notes. "The inverted yield curve leading indicator for recession—that did not work after the pandemic. The SAM rule indicated a recession which did not come to pass. And there are many others."

Beyond economics, Americans' relationship with institutions has fundamentally shifted. "We know that mental health particularly of younger people has deteriorated since the pandemic," Shu says. "We know that trust in institutions, views of the government, the increased polarization—these are things that have deteriorated about how Americans see the world around them that's not really specific to the economy."

The last time sentiment was nearly this low was June 2022, at the height of post-pandemic inflation. "Back then, even though consumers felt terrible about the economy and they were frustrated with inflation, they had strong incomes to support that," Shu explains. "In more recent years, they've had strong asset values and currently have strong asset values. However, we no longer have that incredibly strong labor market that will support robust spending despite negative feelings about the economy."

The difference today: consumers are being "stretched on both" sides—facing high prices while also experiencing weaker income growth.

## The Curious Case of Persistent Price Complaints

One of the most striking charts shows a clear divergence: year-ahead inflation expectations have been declining, while the percentage of consumers citing high prices as weighing down their personal finances has continued rising. This seems contradictory. If inflation is cooling, why do consumers still feel so pinched?

Shu provides historical context. "Historically speaking, when you go back to the 60s, 70s, and 80s, that red line moves pretty much in step with inflation expectations and realized inflation. It went up when inflation is high. It comes down when inflation slows."

That relationship has now broken. "After the peak of inflation in 2022, you see inflation expectations come down. They rise again with the tariff announcements and they've come down. The red line in contrast—it never came down from that 2022 peak. It has stayed up throughout this entire post-pandemic period."

Even though consumers recognize that inflation has slowed and that the worst-case tariff scenarios didn't materialize, the pain of elevated prices hasn't faded from their lived experience. Both complaints about high prices and inflation expectations surged again after the Iran conflict began, suggesting these concerns are now deeply embedded in consumer psychology.

## Will Consumers Front-Load Purchases?

One might expect that if consumers believe prices will rise, they'd rush to buy big-ticket items now—contributing to current economic growth while potentially exacerbating future inflation. But Shu's data suggests this isn't happening at scale.

"What's really interesting is that even though short-run inflation expectations went up substantially after the start of the Iran conflict, long-run inflation expectations only nudged up just a little bit," she notes. The survey specifically asks about buying conditions for durable goods and tracks whether consumers say "you should buy now to avoid price increases in the future."

"We're only seeing a little bit of that. It's not—we're not seeing any sort of surge like we are with short-run inflation expectations."

The reason? Consumers don't feel confident enough in their incomes to make major purchases on speculation. "They don't feel confident in their incomes to go out on a limb and make a major purchase now," Shu says. "They may think: if I don't need it right now, maybe I wait a year. By then, I might believe the conflict will be over and maybe inflation slows down again."

This suggests consumers are bracing for short-term pain while hoping for longer-term relief—a pattern that could moderate both spending and inflation pressures in the near term.

---

## How Fast Does Sentiment Respond to Shocks?

The April data was gathered during the Iran conflict, contrasting with previous reports that captured sentiment beforehand. This provides a natural experiment in measuring how quickly consumer psychology responds to geopolitical events.

"In terms of sentiment and expectations for the future, that changed pretty much immediately," Shu reports. "We saw a pretty sharp break in sentiment as well as inflation expectations immediately after the start of the hostilities in Iran, starting March 1st, and it continued to climb."

The first and biggest change was in gas price expectations—consistent with the immediate geopolitical risk. "Gas price expectations doubled, tripled in those immediate days after the start of the conflict," Shu says. "But consumers aren't necessarily confusing that with prices in general, right? So inflation expectations did not triple."

This nuance matters. Consumers recognize that gas prices are distinct from overall inflation, though gas remains a significant factor in how households view their overall financial situation.

## Does This Match What Experts Are Saying?

The IMF's World Economic Outlook offers an "adverse scenario" that assumes further disruption leading to higher energy prices, inflation expectations, and tighter financial conditions—with growth falling to 2% and inflation rising to 5.4%.

Shu finds this "highly consistent with what we're seeing from consumers. Consumers are broadly expecting a slowdown in the US economy. They're expecting inflation to be higher, at least in the short run. Consumers feel pretty sure that the economy is going to weaken in the year ahead."

However, long-run expectations haven't moved as dramatically. "I think consumers are still reserving judgment on what that's going to look like," she says. "That makes sense given how much uncertainty there is on how long it's going to take for this conflict to resolve, and more specifically, how long it's going to take for the Strait of Hormuz to open up and for the supply disruptions coming from this war to unwind."

This aligns with concerns from financial leaders. Citadel CEO Ken Griffin recently stated that if the Strait of Hormuz remains shut down for six to twelve months, "the world's going to end up in a recession. There's no way to avoid that."

Shu doesn't disagree outright. "A long-run supply disruption is going to be challenging for the world economy. The longer that supply chains are disrupted, the bigger the impact on the rest of the economy and the harder it's going to be to dig ourselves out of that."

The critical question is pass-through: how much will higher fuel costs translate into broader consumer-facing price increases? "Suppliers, producers can only eat these higher fuel costs to an extent," Shu notes. "They're already passing some of them through to consumers, and consumers don't feel confident about their income. So they don't really feel equipped to absorb all these price hikes."

## The Social Media Amplification Effect

The interview takes an intriguing turn when the host raises a provocative question: to what extent is social media responsible for historically low sentiment?

Shu has collaborated with communication researchers to understand how news consumption shapes economic attitudes. "When we began the survey in the 50s and 60s, everyone got their news from the same place," she explains. "It was the daily paper, the same radio stations, the same TV nightly news. Everyone got their news at the same time, and you only got it a couple times a day."

Cable news in the 1990s began creating some polarization, with consumer sentiment showing stronger reactions to economic news than the actual shocks might warrant. But the real shift came with algorithmic newsfeeds in the late 2010s.

"What the communications folks tell me is that it's actually really about the algorithmic newsfeed," Shu says. "It was no longer social media just serving up what your friends liked, what your friends are posting in chronological order. Now the algorithm is predicting what will generate the most engagement based on what everyone else is engaging in—and that might have nothing to do with what you would choose to post. And that really polarizes what people are seeing. And it also means that everything that they're seeing is emotionally triggering, because that's what generates more clicks."

This creates a self-reinforcing cycle. "I think we are in an era of diminished expectations," Shu concludes. "I think it's hard to imagine a situation where consumers feel really vibrant about the economy. That's why I think it's not necessarily useful to compare the level of sentiment today versus previous troughs 15, 20, 30 years ago. The world is different. The people are different. The generations are different. But the trends—upward trends still mean an improvement, downward trends still mean a deterioration—and it's very clear with this Iran conflict consumers think things have worsened."

## The Reality Behind the Credit Data

Some might point to credit indicators as evidence that household finances aren't as bad as sentiment suggests. Credit card delinquency rates, for instance, appear lower than in 2022 or pre-2008 levels.

But Shu cautions against misreading these numbers. Recent research from the Philadelphia Fed reveals a critical insight: "When you see an improvement in credit indicators, it's actually confined to people in higher income areas. And we're not seeing any improvement at all in lower and middle-income families. They're just being shut out. So they're not even in the data to begin with."

This means traditional credit metrics may be masking the true financial stress of lower-income households who have simply lost access to credit rather than improving their situations.

## Labor Market Worries Simmer Below the Surface

While high prices dominate current concerns, consumers are increasingly anxious about employment—and these fears have been building for over a year.

"The share of consumers who expect unemployment to rise in the year ahead is over two-thirds," Shu reports. "And it was about half that at the beginning of 2025. It's very clear that consumers believe that labor markets are much weaker now than they were at the beginning of 2025."

When asked about the probability of losing a job in the next five years—whether for themselves or loved ones—"that's fairly elevated right now," Shu notes. "It was actually a little bit higher at the end of 2025. It has come down just a touch, but it's still near the upper end of its range."

The nature of labor anxiety is also shifting. "Last year they were worried more about tariffs. This year they're more worried about AI." The anecdote about a Harvard Medical School student worrying about job displacement to AI may be extreme, but it reflects a broader anxiety spreading even among traditionally secure professional categories.

---

## A Rare Consensus Across Divides

Perhaps most striking about the latest sentiment readings is their universality. "Demographics across age, income, and political party all posted setbacks in sentiment," the April report noted.

What's remarkable is the agreement across political affiliations. "What was really interesting about these recent trends after the start of the Middle East conflict is that everybody agreed—there was a consensus," Shu emphasizes. "Even people who generally feel stronger about the economy felt worse about it after the conflict began. Across the political spectrum, that's really important as well. This isn't just partisan flag-waving by people at one end of the political spectrum. Independents, Republicans, and Democrats alike all see a worsening in the economic outlook."

This cross-party consensus suggests the concerns are grounded in shared economic reality rather than political identity.

---

## What Would Turn Things Around?

The path to recovery, according to Shu, depends heavily on developments in the Middle East.

"Even if there is a longer-term ceasefire, if that ceasefire is not accompanied with a reopening of the Strait and unconstrained shipping, we're still going to see elevated gas prices," she warns. "A long duration of elevated gas prices will be more likely to pass on to overall inflation than a short-run disruption to energy prices."

Conversely: "If these supply disruptions go away quickly, I see consumers recovering, particularly if they start feeling confident that these spikes in gas prices are spikes and not permanent shifts up and won't necessarily translate to other prices they pay."

The critical variable is how much gas price increases translate into broader consumer price pressures. "Right now they are bracing for an increase in inflation," Shu observes. "The question is: is that actually going to happen? The worst-case scenario they were fearing back last April with the liberation day tariffs—that didn't end up coming to pass, and consumers pretty much immediately started updating their views of the economy. Another year, a different shock—but is it a long-term or a short-term shock? That's what I'm watching."

---

For now, American consumers remain in uncharted territory—unified in their pessimism across demographics, bracing for potential pain from geopolitical disruptions while hoping for relief that remains uncertain. The survey's 74-year history provides context, but the current moment reflects a confluence of economic pressures, institutional distrust, and information environments that make simple historical comparisons misleading. What the data clearly shows is that for millions of Americans, the lived experience of persistent high prices and weakening job markets tells a very different story than headline stock indices might suggest.
