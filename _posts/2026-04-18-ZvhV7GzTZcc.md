The April preliminary consumer sentiment index published by the University of Michigan stands at 47.6—the lowest level in the survey's 74-year history. The reading raises critical questions: What is driving this historic pessimism? How should investors, economists, and policymakers interpret these numbers? And what do they portend for the American economy?

Joanne Shu, Director of the Surveys of Consumers at the University of Michigan, recently joined a financial program to unpack the data and its implications. Her team is responsible for assembling the surveys and indices that economists and investors cite as essential barometers of economic health.

## The Context of Historic Low Sentiment

The current reading is not an anomaly but the continuation of a prolonged period of consumer pessimism. According to Shu, consumer sentiment has been relatively depressed for more than a year.

"Sentiment has pretty much been treading water for the past year," Shu explained. "It reached a trough at the height of the tariff developments from last April, Liberation Day. It improved somewhat thereafter, but those improvements didn't really stick."

The reasons are not mysterious. Consumers have consistently cited one primary concern weighing on their outlook: the persistence of high prices.

"And of course what happened in March and April—it's the war in Iran," Shu continued. "Consumers are very concerned that these geopolitical developments are not going to help on the inflation front. In fact, they believe it's going to make it considerably worse."

## The Stock Market Paradox

The timing of this grim sentiment reading is striking given that stock markets are rebounding, with the S&P 500 approaching new all-time highs. This creates an apparent paradox. Stocks rise when analysts and investors expect earnings or profits to improve—either through cost-cutting or increased demand. If consumers are feeling weak, why are market participants feeling more optimistic?

Shu points out that the two groups are not experiencing the same economic reality.

"When we look at consumers with large stock portfolios, over the last few years, their sentiment has improved from troughs. They recovered very quickly from the Liberation Day tariffs," she said. "At the same time, people at the bottom of the wealth distribution—they are the ones who are really pulling sentiment down because they're just really weighed down by these high prices."

The divergence reflects a broader pattern in post-pandemic economic data. While higher-income households with substantial investment portfolios have seen their wealth grow, lower-income Americans are struggling with the same price pressures that emerged during the inflation surge of 2021-2022.

"Consumers aren't nearly so optimistic about the anticipated productivity gains from AI," Shu added. "They are seeing quite a bit of weakness in the economy right now, which we're seeing with weaker labor markets. It is absolutely the case that labor markets are nowhere as dynamic or as robust as they had been in the immediate post-pandemic period. And really, consumers are paying more attention to kitchen table issues than anything else. On those two fronts—prices and incomes—they tell us that they are struggling."

## Why This Differs from Previous Troughs

The current reading is worse than the 2008 financial crisis, worse than COVID-19, worse than the dot-com bubble, and roughly on par with the 1980s recession. This raises an obvious question: How can sentiment be this low when the economy is not in crisis?

The University of Michigan has been measuring consumer sentiment since 1946, and the fundamental questions related to personal finances, business conditions, and buying conditions for durable goods—both present and future—have remained stable throughout that period. However, the methodology has evolved.

"We were originally a face-to-face survey," Shu explained. "Interviewers were knocking on doors in the '40s, '50s, and '60s. We eventually became a landline survey, and as people stopped using landlines, we switched to cell phones. Now people don't really answer cell phone calls from unknown callers, and we switched to interviewing via web. So periodically we've needed to refresh our methodology in accordance with changing technology and changing cultural norms."

Beyond methodology, the post-pandemic economic landscape has challenged traditional relationships between indicators. The inverted yield curve—a reliable leading indicator for recession—did not function as expected after 2020. The Sahm Rule indicated a recession that never materialized.

"And there are many others," Shu noted. "A lot of things have changed since the pandemic on the consumer front. We know that mental health, particularly of younger people, has deteriorated since the pandemic. We know that trust in institutions, views of the government, the increased polarization—these are things that have deteriorated about how Americans see the world around them. That's not really specific to the economy."

The second-lowest reading in the survey's history came in June 2022, at the height of post-pandemic inflation. Since then, while inflation has softened, it remains above the Federal Reserve's target. More significantly, labor markets have weakened considerably.

"Back after the pandemic, people were feeling stretched on the expenditure side, but labor markets were incredibly strong, so they weren't feeling stretched on the income side," Shu said. "Now they're being stretched on both."

## Sentiment and Spending: How Strong Is the Connection?

The University of Michigan sentiment index is among the most influential measures of consumer confidence, regularly cited by economists, analysts, and fund managers in their decision-making. But how well does sentiment actually predict consumer spending?

Shu acknowledges the data show "some red flags with respect to consumer spending." In the post-pandemic period, sentiment was low while spending remained relatively strong. At that time, consumers felt frustrated about inflation but had strong incomes to support their purchasing.

"In more recent years, they've had strong asset values and currently have strong asset values," she said. "However, we no longer have that incredibly strong labor market that will support robust spending despite negative feelings about the economy."

The current situation differs from earlier periods of low sentiment. Elevated delinquencies, increased credit usage, and very low savings rates paint a precarious picture.

"Consumers are just highlighting too many points of weakness," Shu warned. "The one bright spot is strength in asset values, but that only benefits consumers who hold substantial holdings. If you don't have large portfolios allocated to higher-valued assets, you don't really benefit from a rising stock market."

Research on the wealth effect confirms its existence but also its limitations. The relationship between portfolio values and spending is not dollar-for-dollar.

"Consumers are going to be spending dependent on their expectations for the future," Shu explained. "Do they think these asset values will remain high? Regardless, it's unambiguously the case that if you have a large portfolio—whether stocks or your house—you feel more secure in the ability to take risks, and those risks might include spending a little bit more than you're comfortable with."

Wealthier individuals generate a disproportionate share of total spending, which may explain why weakness appears muted in aggregate data even as underlying conditions deteriorate.

## The Curious Divergence: Inflation Expectations vs. Price Complaints

A striking chart from the discussion shows year-ahead inflation expectations declining while the percentage of consumers citing high prices as dragging down their personal finances continues to rise. This divergence demands explanation.

Historically, these two measures moved in lockstep. When inflation was high, consumers blamed high prices. When inflation slowed, complaints diminished. That relationship has fundamentally changed.

"After the 2022 peak, you see inflation expectations come down," Shu said. "They rose again with the tariff announcements and have since come down. But the red line—consumers telling us that high prices are weighing down their personal finances—it never came down from that 2022 peak. It has stayed up throughout this entire post-pandemic period."

Even though consumers are "fully aware that inflation has slowed" and that the worst-case scenarios they feared did not materialize, the pain of high prices persists in their consciousness. Both inflation expectations and price complaints surged immediately after the Iran conflict began.

The question then becomes: If consumers expect prices to rise, wouldn't they accelerate purchases to buy that refrigerator or television now, actually contributing to economic growth?

"That's a great question," Shu responded. "Not only would that contribute to economic growth, it would also contribute to higher inflation in the long run. It becomes both a positive factor as well as a negative factor for the economy."

What the data show is instructive. While short-run inflation expectations rose substantially after the Iran conflict began, long-run inflation expectations nudged up only slightly. Consumers appear to be making a distinction.

"There may be people who fear inflation right now but may believe that when it comes to big-ticket items, if I don't need it right now, maybe I wait a year," Shu explained. "By then, I might believe the conflict will be over and inflation will slow down again, and that's the right time."

The survey includes specific questions about buying conditions for durable goods and whether consumers believe they should purchase now to avoid future price increases. The data show only minimal evidence of front-loading purchases.

"At this time, consumers are bracing for pain from the Iran conflict, but they're not necessarily expecting that pain to persist into the long run—by which I mean five years or more down the road," Shu said.

## The Speed of Geopolitical Shock Transmission

The April report was published during the Iran conflict, contrasting with previous reports gathered before or at the start of the war. This timing provides insight into how quickly geopolitical shocks affect household psychology.

"In terms of sentiment and expectations for the future, that changed pretty much immediately," Shu said. "We saw a pretty sharp break in sentiment as well as inflation expectations immediately after the start of hostilities, starting March 1st."

The most dramatic change appeared in gas price expectations, which doubled or tripled in the immediate days following the conflict's onset—consistent with the primary risk consumers associate with Middle Eastern instability.

"Consumers aren't necessarily confusing that with prices in general," Shu noted. "Inflation expectations did not triple. Consumers are fully aware that gas prices aren't everything, but it is still going to be a big part of how they view their personal finances. A lot of that red line—consumers blaming high prices for dragging down their personal finances—a lot of that is gas prices."

This finding aligns with recent commentary from major economic institutions. The IMF's World Economic Outlook includes an adverse scenario assuming "further disruption leading to higher energy prices and inflation expectations and tighter financial conditions throughout the year," with growth falling to 2% and inflation rising to 5.4%.

"That's highly consistent with what we're seeing from consumers," Shu confirmed. "Consumers are broadly expecting a slowdown in the US economy. They're expecting inflation to be higher, at least in the short run. Consumers feel pretty sure that the economy is going to weaken in the year ahead. Again, long-run expectations didn't move nearly as much. Consumers are still reserving judgment on what that's going to look like."

## What Would Turn Things Around?

The answer depends largely on events in the Middle East.

"Even if there is a longer-term ceasefire, if that ceasefire is not accompanied by a reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and unconstrained shipping, we're still going to see elevated gas prices," Shu said. "A long duration of elevated gas prices will be more likely to pass on to overall inflation than a short-run disruption to energy prices."

The longer supply disruptions persist, the more negative consumer sentiment will remain. However, if disruptions resolve quickly, Shu sees potential for recovery.

"Particularly if consumers start feeling confident that these spikes in gas prices are spikes and not permanent shifts up—and won't necessarily translate to other prices they pay," she added.

The implications of prolonged disruption are severe. Ken Griffin, CEO of Citadel, recently commented: "Let's assume it's shut down for the next 6 to 12 months. The world's going to end up in a recession. There's no way to avoid that."

Shu does not dismiss this assessment.

"I think a long-run supply disruption is going to be challenging for the world economy," she said. "The longer that supply chains are disrupted, the bigger the impact on the rest of the economy and the harder it's going to be to dig ourselves out of that. We know that with the oil industry, anything that is shut down because things can't ship—it can't just be turned on like a light switch. It takes weeks, if not longer, to get back up and running."

The critical question for consumer spending is how much pass-through to other consumer-facing prices will occur.

"Suppliers, producers can only eat these higher fuel costs to an extent. They're already passing some of them through to consumers, and consumers don't feel confident about their income. So they don't really feel equipped to absorb all these higher price hikes," Shu explained. "We are at risk for a kind of a non-virtuous cycle in which constraints in supply chains can lead to constraints for consumers and for the economy as a whole."

## The Social Media Factor

The discussion touched on a more structural question: To what extent has social media and the internet contributed to lower consumer sentiment compared to previous decades?

Shu has collaborated with professors in the Department of Communication to research how consumers form economic opinions and what news sources influence them.

"Views of a lot of things have become more negative, and social media is part of that," she said. "When we began the survey in the '50s and '60s, everyone got their news from the same place—the daily paper, the same radio stations, the same TV nightly news. Everyone got their news at the same time, a couple times a day."

The onset of 24-hour cable news in the 1990s introduced some polarization in consumer views. The reaction to inflation during the Great Recession was disproportionate relative to the actual economic shocks of the 1970s and 1980s.

"But it's not just social media—social media has been around for 20 years now," Shu noted. "What the communications folks tell me is that it's actually really about the algorithmic newsfeed. That was in the late 2010s, where it was no longer social media serving up what your friends liked in chronological order. Now the algorithm predicts what will generate the most engagement, and that might have nothing to do with what you would choose or what your friends are posting. It becomes really polarizing in what people are seeing. And it also means that everything they're seeing is emotionally triggering because that's what generates more clicks."

The implications are significant.

"I think we are in an era of diminished expectations," Shu concluded. "It's hard to imagine a situation where consumers feel really vibrant about the economy. That's why I think it's not necessarily useful to compare the level of sentiment today versus previous troughs we might have seen 15, 20, 30 years ago. The world is different. The people are different. The generations are different. But the trends—upward trends still mean an improvement, downward trends still mean a deterioration—and it's very clear with this Iran conflict, consumers think things have worsened."

## The Credit Data Question

The host raised a final point about credit indicators. Delinquency rates on credit cards have been rising, and usage of credit cards and revolving plans is increasing—not at 2008 or 2022 levels, but trending upward. Is this evidence of deteriorating financial positions?

Recent research from the Philadelphia Fed suggests caution in interpreting these data. The research points out that improvements in credit indicators may not signal healthy household finances.

"This is an intersection of both what consumers are doing as well as what lenders are doing," Shu explained. "Lenders have the ability to cut off credit to people they think are higher risk. And that's precisely what these researchers believe is happening in recent quarters—that when you see an improvement, it's actually confined to people in higher income areas. We're not seeing any improvement at all among lower and middle income families. They're just being shut out. They're not even in the data to begin with."

This underscores the importance of disaggregating data rather than relying on national aggregates.

"Different consumers are experiencing different things," Shu said. "People with large stock portfolios—they have been looking very different in our data than people with smaller or no portfolios at all."

## Labor Market Fears

The April report showed demographic setbacks across age, income, and political party. While much of the discussion focused on inflation and prices, labor market concerns deserve attention.

"Consumers haven't seen changes in labor market sentiment since the war," Shu said. "But it's unambiguously the case that over the past year or so, year and a half, consumers see that the labor markets have deteriorated substantially. The share of consumers who expect unemployment to rise in the year ahead is over two-thirds—and it was about half that at the beginning of 2025."

When asked about the probability that they or a loved one will lose a job in the next five years, consumers' responses remain elevated.

"Last year they were worried more about tariffs. This year they're more worried about AI," Shu noted. "Unambiguously speaking, consumers believe that labor markets have weakened, but they don't think it's gotten differentially worse after the Iran conflict began."

## The Unusual Consensus

Perhaps most notably, the recent deterioration in sentiment was not confined to any single demographic or political group.

"What was really interesting about these recent trends after the start of the Middle East conflict is that everybody agreed—there was a consensus," Shu said. "Even people who generally feel stronger about the economy felt worse about it after the conflict began. Across the political spectrum, that's really important. This deterioration isn't just partisan flag-waving by people at one end of the political spectrum. Independents, Republicans, and Democrats alike all see a worsening in the economic outlook."

## Looking Ahead

What developments will determine whether sentiment improves or deteriorates further?

"The big thing is how much gas prices pass through to the prices of other things," Shu said. "That's the number one thing that is going to be on consumers' minds and will influence consumer behavior. Right now they are bracing for an increase in inflation. Is that actually going to happen?"

The previous year offers a relevant precedent.

"The worst-case scenario they were fearing back last April with the Liberation Day tariffs didn't end up coming to pass, and consumers pretty much immediately started updating their views of the economy," Shu noted. "Another year, a different shock—but is it a long-term or a short-term shock? That's what I'm watching."

The answer to that question will determine whether American consumers return to cautious optimism or sink deeper into historic pessimism.
