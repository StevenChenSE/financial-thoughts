The global landscape is undergoing a profound restructuring. According to George Friedman, Chairman of Geopolitical Futures, the catalyst for this shift was the war in Ukraine, which signaled the definitive end of the Cold War and set in motion a cascade of changes in Europe, U.S.-China relations, and the Middle East.

### The End of an Era: Russia’s Decline

Friedman argues that Russia has already lost the war in Ukraine by failing to win it. "Russia lost the war by not winning it," he states. After four years, Russia's inability to conquer a much smaller neighbor demonstrates its diminished status. "It's not even a regional power. It is a declining power that is no longer the pivot of the relationship with the United States or with the rest of the world."

The war's objective, Friedman explains, was to rebuild the Soviet Union by first retaking Ukraine as a buffer zone. Russia's failure reveals fundamental weaknesses, both in its rigid, centralized military command structure and its inability to adapt to new technologies. "Satellite warfare changed the nature of warfare," he notes, explaining that American and European satellites provided Ukraine with real-time intelligence, making it impossible for Russia to mass troops secretly. This, combined with drone technology, created a battlefield for which Russia was unprepared.

The conflict now appears locked in a grim stalemate. "So long as both the Ukrainian and the Russian political system remain intact, neither one is inclined to give in," Friedman observes. He sees little room for further escalation, as Russia has "thrown everything they had into it." The war's ultimate resolution may depend on internal Russian politics—whether a point comes where the Russian public or elite tell President Vladimir Putin, "you failed, you will go."

### The Fractured Dream of "Europe"

A major geopolitical takeaway from the Ukraine conflict, Friedman asserts, is the fragmentation of the concept of "Europe." He challenges the notion of Europe as a single entity. "We don't have a relationship with Europe. There's no such country as Europe. There's Britain, Germany, France, Poland, all very different."

The European Union, while an economic coalition, lacks the unified military and political will to act as a true nation-state. "The idea of a nation with a unified economy... but no unified military means they cannot act as a nation," Friedman says. The end of the Cold War and the diminished Russian threat have removed the external pressure that once fostered European unity.

The fundamental obstacle to a "United States of Europe" is history itself. "Your history is a very long and bad one," Friedman, who was born in Hungary, points out. "You are not a nation. You're a continent of nations and you have bad histories." This internal competition and distrust prevent Europe from becoming a cohesive great power, despite its collective economic might.

### NATO's Existential Question

This European fragmentation directly challenges NATO's future. "The mission of NATO was to keep the Soviets from invading and taking Europe. That mission was accomplished," Friedman states. With Russia no longer seen as a credible threat to Western Europe, the alliance must find a new purpose.

While alliances rarely formally disband, their relevance wanes. Friedman suggests NATO may become a forum for discussion rather than decisive action, with member states making individual decisions on engagements. He cites the Middle East as an example: "It was made by the British, the French and so on and so forth... I doubt if the other European countries are going to go to Turkey's aid" if it becomes involved.

### The Middle East: A Nuclear Nightmare and a Military Quagmire

Turning to the ongoing conflict in the Middle East, Friedman identifies the core U.S. fear: "The threat of a nuclear 9/11." The concern is not merely a nuclear-armed Iran, but the potential for a nuclear device to be used in a covert attack by ideological Islamist groups. "The idea of a nuclear Iran is not just an idea of a nuclear Iran. It's what you could do not with missiles but with covert actions with a nuclear weapon."

However, the military challenge is immense. The real power in Iran lies with the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), which is deeply embedded in the country's economy and security apparatus. Airstrikes alone are unlikely to topple this regime. "No war has ever been won simply from the air," Friedman warns.

The immediate strategic problem is securing the Strait of Hormuz, a critical chokepoint for global oil shipments that is vulnerable to Iranian drones. Clearing it would be a major undertaking, as drones can be launched from hundreds of miles away. The prospect of a ground invasion is daunting. "Iran is a huge country... The amount of force that would have to be used on a ground warfare would be enormous and it would be a multi-year war," creating a "fundamental crisis in the United States."

### The New Global Order

The world that emerges from these intertwined crises, Friedman concludes, will look fundamentally different from the post-Cold War era. Russia is sidelined. Europe remains a continent of competing nations rather than a unified power. The U.S.-China relationship moves to center stage, while the Middle East presents a persistent and complex security dilemma.

The old frameworks are breaking down. The task for nations and alliances is to confront this "stark reality" and adapt to a world where the certainties of the past half-century have evaporated. As Friedman bluntly advises Europe, the first step is to "stop making believe." The same could be said for the entire international community navigating this new, uncertain order.
