## Louis Gave on Market Concentration, the Death of Bonds, and Portfolio Defense Strategies

The old adage about tequila shots applies perfectly to modern financial markets: you never know you have had too much until you actually have. In a recent conversation with Thoughtful Money host Adam Tugert, GaveKal CEO and founding partner Louis Gave used this analogy to frame the current state of global equities. The market party is in full swing, driven by an unprecedented surge in artificial intelligence and semiconductor investments, but the underlying mathematics and macroeconomic conditions suggest a reckoning may be closer than many realize.

Gave, known for his on-the-ground perspective across international markets, argues that while the AI revolution is genuinely transformative, the capital expenditure cycle is becoming dangerously stretched. With semiconductor stocks now representing 18% of the S&P 500 and nearly a third of major Asian indices, market breadth has narrowed significantly. Strip out names like TSMC, Samsung, and SK Hynix, and the performance picture changes dramatically. The fear of missing out (FOMO) has fueled a "picks and shovels" trade that mirrors the broadband boom of the late 1990s, raising serious questions about sustainability once the honeymoon phase ends.

This discussion moves beyond sector rotation to examine the broader macroeconomic landscape, the structural failure of traditional portfolio models, and the geopolitical risks lurking in Asian capital flows. By applying a disciplined four-quadrant framework, Gave outlines how investors can navigate the current inflationary boom, hedge against a potential AI bust, and position portfolios for the inevitable hangover.

### The Semiconductor Super-Cycle and Stretched AI Economics

The current market rally is heavily concentrated in tech hardware and semiconductors. Gave notes that if you own semiconductor stocks, you have had a monster year; if you own software, healthcare, or consumer discretionary, you have likely had a miserable one. This divergence is not just a US phenomenon but a global trend, with North Asia outperforming due to heavy weighting in chipmakers, while European and Hong Kong markets lag.

The core issue lies in the sheer scale of expected AI capital expenditure. According to McKinsey, the industry is projected to spend $6.7 trillion between now and 2030. To justify this level of capex, the AI sector would need to generate roughly $2 trillion in annual revenue every single year. For context, the entire global advertising industry generates about $1 trillion annually. Furthermore, compute infrastructure depreciates rapidly; replacing data center chips costs roughly two-thirds of building them initially. Gave draws parallels to the late-1990s broadband boom, where $900 billion (in today's dollars) was poured into dark fiber and "picks and shovels" companies like Nortel Networks and Lucent, many of which eventually went bust. The fundamental challenge is determining whether we are in the early stages of a productive cycle or the late stages of an overbuilt one.

### Financials as the Economic Pulse

Rather than relying solely on equity indices, Gave uses financial sector performance as a leading indicator of economic health. Banks are the pulsating hearts of the financial system; when they thrive, money is moving and the economy is generally robust. When they struggle, it serves as an early warning signal.

Internationally, financials are telling a compelling story. Japanese, Korean, Chinese, and Canadian banks have delivered monster returns, driven by yield curve steepening, fiscal stimulus, and resilient domestic activity. In contrast, US banks have traded sideways despite strong macroeconomic data. This global divergence gives Gave more comfort in international markets, where the financial underbelly appears healthier. However, it also underscores how reliant the US market has become on a narrow set of AI-driven mega-caps, leaving little room for broad-based economic confirmation.

### The Four Economic Quadrants and the Inflationary Boom

Gave evaluates asset allocation through four macroeconomic scenarios: deflationary boom, deflationary bust, inflationary boom, and inflationary bust. A deflationary boom is the natural state of capitalism, driven by efficiency gains. A deflationary bust occurs when excessive capex and bank overextension lead to a credit crunch. An inflationary bust is typically triggered by energy price shocks. Currently, however, the global economy is firmly in an inflationary boom.

This environment is sustained by massive fiscal deficits, with the US running a budget deficit of roughly 7% of GDP during a boom, alongside a "tsunami" of private AI capex. Oil prices are effectively capped between $65 and $100, with China acting as the price setter by buying aggressively at the lower bound and stepping back at the upper bound. Given this backdrop, a deflationary bust is the least likely scenario. Consequently, Gave advocates building portfolios around value stocks, financials, metals, and energy, while avoiding assets that thrive only in deflationary environments.

### The Death of Bonds and Regulatory Capital Traps

The traditional 60/40 portfolio model is officially dead. Gave argues that bonds have been a dead asset class for five years, a reality cemented by financial repression and creeping capital controls. Regulatory frameworks increasingly force institutional capital, such as pension funds and life insurance policies, into domestic government bonds. In France, for example, life insurance products are tax-advantaged but face tightening equity allocation limits, effectively funneling savings into French and German bonds.

Japan's monetary trajectory offers a preview for the West. After years of zero and negative interest rates, Japan now features short rates around 1%, inflation near 3.5%, and a yield curve steepening by 300 basis points. The Federal Reserve has missed its 2% inflation target for 64 consecutive months, suggesting that higher inflation is no longer a bug but a feature designed to manage sovereign debt. Until bond yields spike to 6% or 7% on the 10-year, bonds will remain poor hedges and inferior stores of value.

### The "Godzilla" Risk: Asian Capital Repatriation

One of the most significant tail risks to Western markets is the potential repatriation of Asian capital. Korea recently implemented tax incentives allowing investors to sell foreign assets and repatriate funds without capital gains tax, fueling a massive domestic market rally. Japan holds approximately $3.5 trillion in US assets, representing roughly 10% of US GDP. If Japanese leadership follows Korea's playbook or directs the Government Pension Investment Fund (GPIF) to repatriate capital for domestic re-industrialization and defense spending, the impact could be severe.

Gave contrasts this potential shock with Mike Green's "giant mindless robot" analogy, which describes the slow, steady flow of passive capital into US indices. A Japanese-led repatriation wave would be "Godzilla": fast, furious, and structurally disruptive. The GPIF's movements often set the tone for smaller pension funds, creating a herd effect that could trigger a sharp leg down in global bond markets as treasuries, French OATs, and German bunds are sold to buy back JGBs. Gave notes that hedging this risk is currently cheap due to low FX volatility, making out-of-the-money yen calls an effective form of portfolio "fire insurance."

### China’s Divergent Reality and the Software vs. Hardware AI Debate

China presents a complex, bifurcated economic picture. On the supply side, a decade of industrial investment has produced world-class companies in transportation, energy storage, telecommunications, and robotics. With the cheapest cost of capital, electricity, and labor, Chinese exports are thriving. However, domestic consumption remains depressed due to a real estate bust, regulatory interference in banking, and crushed consumer confidence. While major cities like Shanghai and Shenzhen show signs of real estate stabilization, the broader economy still lacks a consumer recovery.

Despite weak GDP growth, Chinese equity markets (Shanghai and Shenzhen) have performed well, mirroring the post-2008 US dynamic where monetary easing and liquidity support markets even as the real economy stagnates. Additionally, the emergence of competitive AI models like DeepSeek challenges the US hyperscaler thesis. While American firms are engaged in a hardware arms race to reach AGI, Chinese developers are proving that software efficiency and algorithmic optimization can deliver competitive performance without matching the $6.7 trillion capex trajectory. If hyperscalers realize they can achieve similar results with 20-30% less spending, the AI investment cycle could cool rapidly.

## Summary

Louis Gave’s analysis underscores that while the AI revolution is real, the market's current pricing reflects extreme concentration, stretched valuations, and FOMO-driven momentum. The macroeconomic environment remains firmly in an inflationary boom, supported by fiscal deficits and contained energy prices, making a deflationary bust unlikely in the near term. However, the structural failure of bonds, the regulatory trapping of capital, and the looming risk of Asian capital repatriation present significant vulnerabilities to traditional Western-centric portfolios.

For investors, the path forward requires discipline over momentum. Gave recommends abandoning the 60/40 model, rotating into value stocks, financials, metals, and energy, and exploring less crowded opportunities in Latin America and international markets. Hedging tail risks, particularly through cheap yen calls, provides essential downside protection against a potential AI correction or a sudden shift in Asian capital flows. As the tequila analogy reminds us, the hangover is inevitable; the goal is to position portfolios so that when the party ends, you are prepared to walk away with your capital intact.
