## Warsh's Inaugural FOMC Meeting Signals Hawkish Pivot as Rate Hike Expectations Surge

Kevin Warsh officially took the reins as Federal Reserve Chairman in a historic first day that sent shockwaves through financial markets. The Federal Open Market Committee held interest rates steady at 3.5% to 3.75%, but the real story emerged from Warsh's stark departure from his predecessor's communication approach—and the stark disagreement among his colleagues about what comes next.

"This is Kevin Warsh's first day as Fed chair," the host noted. "We'll be going over what happened today at the FOMC, why certain things were said, what could be next."

Warsh wasted no time implementing his vision for the Federal Reserve. The most significant change: eliminating forward guidance from official statements entirely. Reporters pressed him on how markets should interpret future policy without this traditional tool. Warsh's response was characteristically blunt—the Fed would rely less on dot plots and more on data-dependent decision-making.

The announcement triggered immediate market turbulence. The S&P 500 dropped 1.2%, the NASDAQ fell in tandem, gold slipped 2.3%, and Bitcoin declined 2%. But the most dramatic movement came in the bond market: the 2-year yield "rocketed higher," rising 15 basis points as traders priced in an accelerated timeline for rate increases.

## The Dot Plot Reveals a Divided Committee

Behind the scenes, Warsh faces a committee that may not share his vision. Nine of the 19 FOMC members indicated they believe interest rates should increase before year-end, with some projecting multiple hikes. Notably, Warsh himself did not vote on this projection, creating a stark contrast between his stated philosophy and his colleagues' preferences.

"The question for your listeners is what happens to the 10-year and 30-year?" explained Komal Sri Kumar, President of Sri Kumar Global Strategies. "10 and 30 did not move very much today. But if you have more confusion on the 2-year side and if inflation is not controlled before the end of the year, expect the 10-year and 30-year to move up as well."

Sri Kumar raised concerns about Warsh's ability to manage a restive committee without traditional communication tools. "If he's not going to give forward guidance, if he's not going to speak regularly, he cannot stop the other members of the Federal Open Markets Committee from talking," he warned. "They are going to talk to the press, and they are going to say different things in public."

The analyst emphasized that Warsh is not the committee members' supervisor—he's merely the chairman of the board. This structural reality may undermine his authority to implement unified messaging.

## A Monetarist at the Helm

Warsh's appointment represents a dramatic philosophical shift from Jerome Powell, who famously suggested the Fed needed to "unlearn monetarism." Warsh has explicitly embraced monetarist principles, which prioritize money supply control and strict inflation targeting.

His background reveals a consistent hawkish streak. Serving on the Board of Governors from 2006 to 2011, Warsh was known as an "inflationary hawk" who opposed balance sheet expansion even during the depths of the 2008 financial crisis. He departed in 2011, with speculation that his resignation represented silent protest against the Fed's quantitative easing programs.

However, Sri Kumar cautioned against taking Warsh's pre-appointment positions at face value. "There is going to be a difference between what Warsh said before he became chairman and what he is going to do when he has become chairman," he observed.

In a Wall Street Journal op-ed written when he was under consideration for the chairmanship, Warsh advocated for interest rate cuts—directly contradicting his earlier hawkish stance. "Campaigning can make strange bedfellows," Sri Kumar noted wryly.

## Balance Sheet Expansion Continues Unchecked

Despite his historical concerns about Fed balance sheet growth, Warsh faces a complicated reality. The Federal Reserve's balance sheet has been climbing in recent months, and quantitative tightening appears unlikely to resume anytime soon.

"The Fed did not want to face that again in the opening months of a new chairman," Sri Kumar explained, referencing the September 2019 cash crunch and March 2023 regional banking crisis. "They decided to postpone it."

The FOMC meeting made no mention of balance sheet tightening, suggesting the Fed prioritizes financial stability over aggressive QT in the near term.

## Markets Price In Aggressive Rate Hikes

The CME FedWatch tool revealed a dramatic shift in market expectations following Warsh's press conference. The probability of a 25 basis point hike by the July 29th meeting surged. By September, markets now assign greater than 50% odds of at least one 25 basis point increase, with some projections pointing to a potential 50 basis point move.

"The probability has shifted overwhelmingly in favor of at least one hike, only 25% chance it will stay," the host noted. "By December, that probability has dropped to only 13% that the Fed funds rate will stay and overwhelmingly in favor that there will be at least one, perhaps even three 25 basis point hikes by then."

Sri Kumar agreed with these aggressive market expectations. "I have been saying on your show as well as in my writings even before the Iran war that the Fed should be increasing the interest rate to keep inflation under control," he stated.

The ongoing geopolitical uncertainty, including the Iran conflict now exceeding 100 days, adds additional complexity to inflation projections. "If there is an attack on Iranian territory again by US forces, I would expect that as a result of that oil price would go up yet again," Sri Kumar warned.

## Historical Parallels and Political Undercurrents

The analysis drew uncomfortable historical comparisons. "This to me reminds me of the situation with President Richard Nixon in the 1970s when he wanted Fed Chairman Arthur Burns to cut interest rates even though inflation was going up," Sri Kumar observed.

The parallel highlights persistent tensions between presidential pressure and Fed independence. Despite President Trump's apparent preference for lower rates—potentially influenced by his real estate background—"the president has wanted interest rates to be lower, no matter what the interest rate was," Sri Kumar noted.

Sri Kumar also suggested the previous Fed under Powell may have cut rates toward the end of 2024 with political motivations, potentially favoring a Harris presidency over Trump's return. "The cuts in interest rates that were done toward the end of 2024 were not justified," he argued. "They were not required because Powell said at that time, 'Our employment is a major threat. Inflation has been completely controlled.' It turned out to be just the opposite."

## What Comes Next

Kevin Warsh's first day as Fed Chair established a clear pattern: unconventional communication, hawkish inflation concerns, and a committee with its own ideas. Whether he can maintain control and guide policy effectively remains to be seen.

"If you're really a monetarist, you know that [lower rates are] not the case, and that's not the ultimate solution to the problems that we have," Sri Kumar concluded.

For markets, the message is clear: prepare for higher rates ahead. The era of easy money may be over, and Warsh shows no signs of reversing course.
