*An analysis of the unfolding crisis in the Strait of Hormuz and its seismic implications for global energy markets.*

---

In a recent interview with Investing News, Adam Rosenwag, managing partner at Gary and Rosenwag, provided a stark assessment of the ongoing blockade in the Strait of Hormuz, framing it as potentially "the largest shock the oil industry has ever experienced."

The conversation, occurring against the volatile backdrop of Middle Eastern conflict, moved beyond immediate headlines to examine the structural vulnerabilities of global energy markets and the shifting investment landscape.

## An Unprecedented Disruption

Rosenwag was clear that his analysis wasn't alarmist, but data-driven. While long-term oil fundamentals depend on supply and demand, the current situation presents "very, very acute, very impactful short-term issues."

The scale is staggering. An estimated 20 million barrels of oil pass through the Strait of Hormuz daily. While some has been rerouted via Saudi Arabia's pipeline, Rosenwag estimates **10 to 15 million barrels per day are currently impacted**. By mid-March, this could represent a cumulative disruption of roughly **230 million barrels**.

"This is by far the largest shock that the oil market has ever dealt with," he stated, noting it dwarfs the 1970s OPEC embargoes and the COVID-era price swings.

The international response has been swift but likely insufficient. The International Energy Agency (IEA) has coordinated a release of **400 million barrels from strategic petroleum reserves (SPR)** over 100 days. However, Rosenwag highlighted a critical bottleneck: flow rates. "It's unlikely that even if you were willing to release all the SPR... you still wouldn't be able to get it much up above 4 million barrels a day. So that leaves a huge hole."

The physical impacts are beginning to ripple out. Refiners are being forced to slow operations, China has halted refined product exports, and Rosenwag shared an anecdote of vessels struggling to find bunkering fuel in Singapore. "You're already starting to see physical shortages here."

## A Catalyst for Renewed Focus

Perhaps the most significant long-term consequence of the crisis, according to Rosenwag, is psychological. It casts "a bit of a lens back on the energy industry."

He reminded viewers that oil entered this crisis as "arguably the most out-of-favor major asset class." Speculative positioning was profoundly bearish just before the conflict began. "The idea that you can just take the oil market for granted could be behind us."

This presents a potential investment paradox. While spot crude prices have surged, the longer-dated futures curve has risen only modestly, signaling traders expect a resolution. Rosenwag believes this may be a mistake.

"The stocks are up... but they're up like 10 or 15%... they're not up 50%." He argues that if long-term supply/demand fundamentals are as tight as he believes, **oil could move "well in excess of $75" long-term**, and equities have not yet fully priced this in. "The risk here is that you look back and you didn't buy now."

## The Great Rotation: From Gold to Oil

Rosenwag detailed a strategic portfolio rotation his firm has undertaken, moving from precious metals back into energy.

"We had been essentially carrying a very full precious metal equity portfolio," he explained. After oil's strong post-COVID and post-Ukraine rally, relative valuation swung decisively in gold's favor by late 2023. They increased gold exposure to a "full position" of 25%.

However, in October of last year, they began taking profits on gold, rotating into what they saw as "really undervalued... oil and natural gas." A further decisive move came in January 2024, after a powerful silver rally—a classic historical signal of a nearing peak in precious metals momentum.

"We actually cut [gold] all the way down to 10 or 11%, which is a half position for us," he said.

This shift was rooted in a comparative question: "Will gold go from 5,000 to 10,000 before oil goes from 50 to 100? And our answer was no." While maintaining a long-term bullish view on gold (citing a target with "five figures in it"), Rosenwag believes the **near-to-medium-term opportunity is superior in energy**.

## The Road Ahead for Gold and Inflation

Rosenwag outlined a historical pattern for commodity cycles: gold leads early, industrial commodities like oil dominate the middle, and gold has a final "blowoff" rally at the end. He believes we are entering that middle phase.

This could mean a period of weakness for gold. Historically, in the mid-1970s, gold pulled back **35-40% before making new highs**. A similar correction is "entirely possible," especially given high levels of "fast money" in gold ETFs that could quickly reverse.

A key risk for gold, he noted, is inflation stemming from the energy crisis. Disrupted shipping flows impact far more than crude—fertilizers, coal, and global supply chains are already feeling the strain.

"If the situation in Iran continues... I would expect inflation numbers to move up a lot," Rosenwag warned. This could stifle anticipated rate cuts and even prompt hikes—a scenario no gold investor is pricing in. "I see the recipe for a lower gold price in the near and medium term."

## Beyond Oil: The Ripple Effects

The crisis is highlighting vulnerabilities in overlooked markets. Rosenwag specifically pointed to **fertilizers** as a critical area to watch. A significant portion of global phosphate and urea supplies transit the Strait of Hormuz. Disruptions could affect crop yields and bid up grain prices later in the year.

## Conclusion: A Wake-Up Call

The blockade of the Strait of Hormuz is more than a geopolitical event; it is a stress test for a global energy system that has been under-invested in and taken for granted. As Adam Rosenwag concludes, the immediate physical oil shock is historic, but the lasting impact may be a fundamental reassessment of risk and value across the entire real asset complex. For investors, the message is clear: the era of ignoring energy security is over.
