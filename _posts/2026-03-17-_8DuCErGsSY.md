*An interview with Adam Rosenwag, Managing Partner at Gary and Rosenwag, on the unfolding crisis in the Middle East and its seismic impact on global energy markets.*

In a recent interview with Investing News, Adam Rosenwag provided a stark assessment of the oil market disruption stemming from the closure of the Strait of Hormuz. Against a backdrop of escalating Middle East tensions, Rosenwag argued that the current crisis has the potential to rank as "the largest shock that the oil market has ever dealt with."

## An Unprecedented Supply Shock

The immediate issue is one of sheer scale. An estimated 20 million barrels of oil per day typically transit the Strait of Hormuz, a vital global choke point. While some rerouting has occurred, Rosenwag estimates that "on the order of 10 to 15 million barrels a day" is currently being impacted.

"Just on a barrels per day basis, this is by far the largest shock that the oil market has ever dealt with," he stated, noting it surpasses the 1970s OPEC embargoes and the COVID-era volatility. "This is the biggest imbalance that we've seen in global oil markets."

The International Energy Agency (IEA) has coordinated a release of 400 million barrels from strategic petroleum reserves (SPRs) over 100 days. However, Rosenwag highlighted a critical bottleneck: flow rate. "It's unlikely that even if you were willing to release all the SPR... you still wouldn't be able to get it much up above 4 million barrels a day. So that leaves a huge hole day in and day out."

The physical impacts are beginning to surface. Refiners are being forced to slow operations as they deplete existing crude slates, and Rosenwag shared an anecdote about vessels struggling to find bunkering fuel in Singapore just a week into the crisis. "You're already starting to see physical shortages here," he said.

## A Catalyst for Renewed Investor Focus

Perhaps one of the most striking aspects of the situation, according to Rosenwag, is that it erupted when investor sentiment toward oil was at a near-record low.

"Oil had been the forgotten commodity for the last 3 years," he observed. "The Friday before the US and Israel launched their attacks on Iran, speculative positioning amongst traders... was at a near-record high and on a net long basis was at a near-record low."

He believes the crisis may have permanently altered market psychology. "I think some damage has been done here in the sense that I think people are now looking at the space again and the idea that you can just take the oil market for granted could be behind us."

## Investment Implications: Rotating from Gold to Energy

Rosenwag detailed a significant portfolio rotation his firm executed, moving capital from precious metals into energy starting in October of last year. This was driven by a relative valuation call.

"We said, look, we still like the gold fundamentals... But the question we had to ask ourselves as we came into this year, oil at 50 bucks and gold at 5,000, was will gold go from 5,000 to 10,000 before oil goes from 50 to 100? And our answer was no."

While maintaining a long-term bullish view on gold ("our long-term price target for gold still probably has five figures in it"), he sees a better near-term opportunity in energy. He notes that while spot crude prices spiked, the longer-dated futures curve has remained relatively subdued, signaling trader expectations for a resolution.

"I can tell you in a second why I think even if the situation does resolve itself, that might be a poor strategy... I think the risk here is that you look back and you didn't buy now."

## The Broader Commodity Ripple Effect

The disruption extends far beyond crude oil. Rosenwag pointed to several critical secondary impacts:

*   **Fertilizers:** A significant amount of phosphate and urea transits the Strait. Disruptions could affect crop yields later this year, potentially putting a bid under grain prices.
*   **Shipping & Coal:** Vessels shipping coal are already experiencing bunkering difficulties, creating logistical snarls.
*   **Inflation:** "When energy prices rise, it works its way into pricing across the whole system," Rosenwag warned. He suggested the supply chain impact could be as large as that seen during COVID, with potential implications for interest rate policy.

## Outlook for Gold and Silver

Addressing why gold hasn't surged more dramatically as a traditional safe haven during the crisis, Rosenwag was pragmatic. "Gold investors are never happy," he quipped, noting it's still up significantly from its lows weeks ago.

However, he sees potential for a consolidation or pullback in the precious metals complex in the coming years, drawing parallels to historical patterns in the 1970s and 2000s.

"In the 1970s, gold pulled back by like about 35 or 40% before going on to huge new highs... Is something like that possible? I mean, yeah, certainly it's possible." A catalyst could be shifting expectations around interest rates if the oil crisis fuels persistent inflation. "So I see the recipe for a lower gold price in the near and medium term. Again, longer term... prices are probably higher 10 years from now."

## Conclusion: A Wake-Up Call for Real Assets

The interview painted a picture of a market at an inflection point. The Strait of Hormuz crisis has acted as a violent revealer of underlying fragility in global energy supply chains, exacerbated by years of underinvestment.

For investors, Rosenwag's analysis suggests the event is less a short-term trading opportunity and more a fundamental wake-up call. The extreme bearish positioning in oil has been unwound, but the longer-term rerating of energy equities, which he believes still price in only $70-$75 oil, may have just begun. In a world where the flow of real commodities can be abruptly severed, the value of owning the companies that produce them has been thrown into sharp, urgent relief.
