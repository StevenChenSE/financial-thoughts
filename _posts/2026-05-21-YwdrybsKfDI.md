## The Fed's Diminishing Grip on Interest Rates

The US 10-year Treasury yield has surged to 4.69%, breaking decisively above a four-year resistance level that investors had been coiling beneath. This isn't a minor fluctuation—it's a signal that the Federal Reserve is losing control of long-term interest rates, according to veteran credit expert and resource investor Rick Roll.

Speaking on Capital Cosm (May 19th, 2026), Roll drew a clear distinction between the Fed's effectiveness at managing short-term rates versus its ability to influence the long end of the yield curve. While the central bank maintains pressure to lower rates, bond investors around the world are demanding more compensation for both inflation risk and the deteriorating purchasing power of the dollar.

> "People need to consider that higher interest rates normally have a negative impact on equity pricing for two reasons. That increases company's cost of capital—at least debt capital—which could reduce earnings per share at the same time that it competes with common equity dividends in terms of sources of income."

The implications for equity markets are straightforward: as borrowing costs rise, companies face margin compression, and stocks must compete with increasingly attractive bond yields. This repricing is happening not just in America—Japanese 10 and 30-year yields are spiking, and European bonds are experiencing their own version of a crash.

### Who Wins and Who Loses

The impact of rising rates isn't uniform across the economy. Roll offered a candid assessment of winners and losers:

**Winners:**

- Savers who traditionally relied on CDs and money market accounts
- Income-focused investors who can now access meaningful yields

**Losers:**

- Consumers who finance lifestyles through credit
- Borrowers facing higher debt service costs
- Investors with large equity allocations competing against bond income

The artificial suppression of interest rates over decades represented what Roll called "an income transfer from savers to spenders." Now that transfer is reversing, but the adjustment is far from painless.

## The Strait of Hormuz Crisis: More Than Just a Conflict

### How 50% of Export Oil Became Hostage

Beyond interest rate concerns lies an even more pressing crisis: approximately 50-60% of the world's export oil supplies flow through the Strait of Hormuz, and this critical chokepoint has been effectively inoperable for nearly three months following the start of the Iran conflict.

While North America has achieved self-sufficiency in oil and gas production, the global market remains deeply exposed. Tankers laden with roughly 200 cargoes are sitting north of the strait, unable or unwilling to transit waters where they risk becoming targets. The result is a massive supply disruption that the current $104/barrel price barely reflects.

### Physical Shortages: From Anticipation to Reality

What makes this situation particularly dangerous is the difference between inventory and available supply. Jeff Curry, former head of commodities trading at Goldman Sachs, appeared on the program to deliver a stark warning:

> "Most of that [global storage] is for pipe fill. It's system fill. It's working inventory. You cannot touch it. We're at least 2 to 300 million barrels before we hit tank bottoms. In some products like diesel and distillates, you're there. Motor oils, you're there. Gasoline in the US in July, you're going to be there. When that happens, the deficit turns into a shortage and you got problems."

This distinction between a supply deficit and an actual shortage is critical. When inventories represent working capital necessary to keep systems operational, they're not available to absorb shocks. Markets are currently pricing energy "in anticipation of shortage rather than as a reflection of shortage"—but that reflection is coming.

**Current shortage indicators:**

- Diesel and distillate inventories already depleted
- Jet fuel availability declining, forcing revised aircraft schedules
- Refined product prices artificially suppressed by hope rather than supply
- North American self-sufficiency provides a buffer, but not immunity

### The Price Reality Nobody Wants to Acknowledge

Roll estimates that 30-40% premiums are already being paid at spot markets for material already south of the Strait—essentially black market pricing for scarce products. When the physical shortage fully manifests, he expects Brent or WTI crude to reach significantly higher levels than today's $104.

The paradox is that consumers remain relatively calm because experts keep dismissing the crisis. Roll observed:

> "The comment from Wall Street is that the increase in oil price acts as a tax and is much more likely to cause recessionary pressures than inflation pressures. Which is to say the increase in the oil price likely will be reflected in reduced demand elsewhere in the economy."

But this conventional wisdom assumes functioning markets and adequate substitutes—both questionable assumptions when physical supply constraints override price signals.

## The Interest Rate and Metals Connection

### A Century of Correlation

The interview featured a fascinating long-term analysis showing the relationship between 10-year Treasury yields and silver prices stretching back to 1913:

- **1940s-1980s:** Rising interest rate environment → Rising silver prices
- **1980s-2020:** Declining interest rate environment → Silver moved sideways for 40+ years
- **2020-2026:** Rates broke out of downtrend → Silver broke above its $50 all-time high

This historical pattern suggests that the bond market's repricing may have profound implications for precious metals.

### Competing Theories on the Relationship

Roll offered his interpretation while acknowledging uncertainty:

**Bullish metals theory:**

- Rising yields reflect inflationary expectations
- Investors hedge inflation by buying hard assets
- Silver may outperform gold in inflationary environments

**Bearish metals theory:**

- Higher interest rates increase the relative attractiveness of bonds
- Dollar strength typically correlates inversely with gold and silver
- Money rotates from commodities into yield instruments

> "Increased interest usually decreases demand for gold. Increased interest attracts debt instruments, yield instruments relative to other instruments. Increased US interest rates relative to other countries' interest rates also increases the attractiveness of the US dollar, and given that gold and silver are denominated in dollars, usually strength in the dollar correlates inversely with strength in gold and silver."

The tension between these forces creates uncertainty, but the historical correlation is too strong to ignore.

## The Credit Contagion Threat

### Three Factors Converging

Roll's most sobering assessment concerned the possibility of a credit crisis. He's increasing liquidity not based on a specific base case, but on the convergence of multiple concerning factors:

1. **Higher oil prices** creating economic shock
2. **Rising interest rates** amplifying credit stress
3. **Decreased economic activity** from both forces combined

> "I think there is an increasing possibility of a credit contagion. I don't think it's a probability yet. I think it's a possibility, but it's a possibility where the price that we pay is high. Circa 2008."

### Private Credit's Hidden Vulnerabilities

Roll identified structural problems accumulated over 25 years of benign credit conditions. The private credit boom that began roughly a decade ago attracted what he calls "tourists"—investors and fund managers without deep credit experience who drove prices to unsustainably tight levels.

> "Non-investment grade project loans that I thought should have been priced at 15% were beginning to be priced at 11 and 12 percent. And I think that was a consequence of tourists in the market. When that happened, I just got out of the way."

The reckoning hasn't occurred yet, but the combination of higher rates and tighter credit conditions creates the kindling for problems.

### Junk Bonds: The Tipping Point to Watch

If private credit represents one concern, the high-yield bond market represents another:

> "If we begin to see real cracks in the junk market, particularly if we see disintermediation out of high yield ETFs, then we have real problems."

This is the scenario that would signal the transition from possibility to probability.

## Banking System: Better Than 2008, But Not Safe

### Structural Improvements Since the Last Crisis

Roll acknowledged that bank balance sheets are in significantly better shape than during the 2008 financial crisis. Tier one capital relative to deposits is stronger, credit discipline has improved at many institutions, and the equity-to-deposit ratios are healthier.

### Derivatives: The Elephant in the Room

But one shadow looms large:

> "There's a big difference between the nominal value of derivatives and the actual value of derivatives. But that difference only occurs if both sides pay. And the idea that a big bank can manage a $60 trillion derivative book on $500 billion in equity—I don't like that idea."

The entire derivatives edifice depends on confidence and the assumption that all parties will meet their obligations. The moment that assumption breaks down, the gap between nominal and actual value becomes catastrophic.

### Three Specific Banking Concerns

**Consumer Credit Cards:**
> "A lot of banks can't help themselves. They're so greedy going after 25% annual interest rates that they're willing to lend money to people who have to borrow to fund their lifestyle, which is a really stupid way to run a bank."

**Commercial Real Estate:**
> "There are office towers in major American cities that are 25% or 30% vacant. That means those buildings aren't covering their operating expense, never mind their mortgage. The banking industry has been able—nod nod, wink wink—with the acceptance of some of the auditors and the regulators to extend and pretend those loans. And I think that game's coming to a complete halt."

**Duration Mismatch:**
> "If you're loaning long, particularly loaning long fixed rate, and you're funding that by borrowing short at a floating rate, a rise in interest rates like the one we have now absolutely annihilates your balance sheet. It's what did in Silicon Valley Bank. It's what did in First Republic Bank. It's what did in the savings and loan industry."

## Oil Services: A Structural Opportunity

Despite the broader economic concerns, Roll remains constructive on oil field services. His thesis predates the Hormuz crisis:

> "The oil industry had underinvested in sustaining capital to the tune of about a billion dollars a day. We're going to have to play catch-up there, and the oil service companies are perfectly in place to do that."

The conflict accelerated timelines significantly. Both US and Canadian rig counts are gapping higher to capture elevated prices, and the industry is seeing renewed emphasis on sustaining capital and new project development globally.

> "I think the oil field services companies are going to have a pretty good five or six year run as a consequence of that."

## Metals and Mining: Headwinds and Hidden Risks

### Why Metals Are Selling Off Despite Supply Constraints

The apparent contradiction of falling metals prices in an environment of supply disruption reflects two dominant forces:

1. **Dollar strength** making dollar-denominated metals more expensive for foreign buyers
2. **Interest rate competition** pulling capital from commodities into bonds

### Resource Nationalism Rising

When commodity prices rise, governments see opportunity. Roll pointed to increasing resource nationalism globally:

- Higher royalty rates in West African countries
- Requirements for gold mining companies to sell production to central banks
- Restrictions on currency repatriation
- "Social theft" disguised as legitimate policy

### The Capital Cost Inflation Problem

Even before current energy price spikes, new mine construction costs were increasing at 8-10% annually. Now, higher energy costs will compound the problem. Building new copper mines—which will be necessary given electrification demands—requires capital that's becoming increasingly expensive.

### The High-Grading Trap

Roll revealed a practice that inflates short-term profitability at the expense of long-term sustainability:

> "Companies during periods of low commodity prices produce their higher grade material and they don't always reflect the fact that they produce that high-grade material relative to their total resource grade correctly in the income statement. In subsequent quarters, they move from higher grade to lower grade material, their production costs necessarily go up."

Now that commodity prices are strong and can "hide sin," this high-grading problem will manifest as unexplained cost increases.

## Personal Strategy: Liquidity and Patience

### Why Roll Is Raising Cash

Rather than making a specific directional bet, Roll is increasing liquidity based on asymmetric risk:

> "Even though I don't think that there's a probability—a better than break even chance of it occurring—the penalty that one would pay for ignoring it is so high that I personally have increased my liquidity. Positions that I've sold haven't been reinvested. I feel differently now."

His approach draws directly from experience:

> "Come 2008, I was very liquid and I was very ready. The consequence of that is that the single best investment year of my life was 2009."

### What Would Change His Mind?

Roll outlined conditions that would prompt him to deploy capital:

1. **Substantially lower equity prices** creating value opportunities
2. **Resolution of the Gulf conflict** removing the energy supply shock
3. **Credit market stabilization** reducing contagion risk
4. **Attractive prices on specific assets** regardless of market direction

> "Money is made on the delta between price and value. So to the extent that the prices fell and the value stayed the same, I'd likely be a player."

### You Can Stay Liquid Forever—If You're Wealthy Enough

A provocative observation from Roll:

> "You actually can stay liquid forever if you're relatively wealthy. I could stay wealthy forever. I mean, I could stay liquid forever. It's no fun."

He draws parallels to Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger, who maintain high liquidity when opportunity is constrained—not because they're bearish, but because they haven't found assets trading below intrinsic value.

### Perversely, a Crash Would Attract Him

> "The thing that would cause me to reduce my liquidity in a hurry would be a crash. Because in a crash, I've learned that when everybody else runs for the hills, it's my time to run to assets. If you saw something like 2008 where broad equity markets had a 50% decline and junk debt markets had a 65% decline, you'd see me buying with both hands."

## Key Takeaways

1. **The Fed has lost control of long-term rates.** Rising 10-year yields reflect investor demands for inflation compensation and currency risk—forces the central bank cannot directly address.

2. **Physical energy shortages are imminent.** Inventory levels reflect working capital, not surplus. When tank bottoms are hit, expect deficits to transform into shortages with prices potentially 30-40% higher than current levels.

3. **Credit contagion is a possibility, not a probability—but the cost of being wrong is catastrophic.** The convergence of higher oil prices, rising rates, and slowing growth creates conditions for systemic stress.

4. **Banking system risks are elevated but different from 2008.** Derivatives exposure, commercial real estate losses, and duration mismatch pose specific threats, though capital ratios are stronger.

5. **The long-term thesis for resource investing remains intact despite near-term volatility.** Sustaining capital underinvestment, growing demand for electrification metals, and accelerating energy transition spending support the sector—but patience and selectivity are essential.

> "Don't curl up in fetal condition in the corner and quit. We have some challenges to get through. We're going to get through them. The back side of this is going to be better than the front side. Invest in yourself. Invest for the long term. Don't get overcome by gloom. Don't quit."
