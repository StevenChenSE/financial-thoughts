### Macroeconomist Philip Pilkington explains why surging refining spreads, Middle Eastern refinery damage, and fertilizer shortages are setting up severe food inflation and broad economic fallout.

The global energy market is exhibiting severe stress signals beneath the surface of headline crude prices. While benchmark oil futures fluctuate based on political announcements and algorithmic trading, the prices of refined products—most notably diesel—tell a far more alarming story. 

In a recent discussion on *Capital Cosm* with host Danny, macroeconomist Philip Pilkington—author of *The Collapse of Global Liberalism* and co-host of the *Multipolarity* podcast—outlined the cascading risks emerging from a worsening diesel shortage, the vulnerability of agricultural supply chains, and the underlying fragility of modern market pricing.

## The Divergence Between Crude and Refined Products

Financial markets often treat crude oil benchmarks like West Texas Intermediate (WTI) as the definitive gauge of energy conditions. However, Pilkington argues that headline crude prices have become increasingly distorted by algorithmic trading, public statements, and policy interventions. 

Instead, the true state of the energy system is reflected in the **3:2:1 crack spread**—the margin refiners earn by converting three barrels of crude into two barrels of gasoline and one barrel of diesel.

```
+------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                      The 3:2:1 Crack Spread                      |
|                                                                  |
|   3 Barrels of Crude Oil  -->  2 Barrels of Gasoline             |

```

With refining crack spreads surging to historic highs—such as the New York Harbor ULSD futures hitting record levels—the cost of usable fuel at the pump is disconnecting from paper crude prices. This widening gap signals that the physical refining and distribution network is operating under extreme duress.

## Refinery Destruction and Geopolitical Chokepoints

A core driver of the current supply crunch is structural damage to global refining capacity. While media attention frequently centers on Ukrainian drone strikes against Russian oil facilities, Pilkington highlights a critical operational difference:

* **Russian Facilities:** Ukrainian attacks using lightweight, rudimentary drones often result in damage that can be repaired within days, constrained by low payload capacities and heavy Russian air defense.
* **Middle Eastern Facilities:** In contrast, advanced ballistic missile strikes on Middle Eastern infrastructure (involving Iran and the Houthis) have caused catastrophic, long-term damage with little effective defense, taking crucial refining capacity offline permanently or for extended periods.

This loss of refining capacity, combined with a 10% to 20% contraction in effective global product supplies, places immense strain on remaining facilities worldwide.

## The Myth of Complete Independence

Although the United States is classified as a net energy exporter on paper, aggregate figures obscure critical product-level imbalances:

1. **Crude Quality Mismatch:** The U.S. primarily produces light, sweet crude, which is optimal for gasoline production but poorly suited for diesel yields.
2. **Dependence on Heavy Sour Crude:** U.S. refiners rely heavily on heavy sour crude imported from Canada to produce middle distillates like diesel.
3. **Trade Friction Risks:** Trade disputes or proposed export restrictions could trigger retaliatory cutoffs from key partners, further limiting domestic diesel feedstock.
4. **Strategic Reserve Depletion:** Continued drawdowns of the Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) are pushing inventories toward operational floors, where physical extraction from salt caverns risks structural degradation and contamination.

## Agriculture, Fertilizer, and Food Inflation

The most urgent secondary impact of the diesel squeeze is bearing down on agriculture. Diesel powers tractors, harvesters, and freight logistics, while natural gas remains the primary input for nitrogen-based fertilizers.

* **Crops Left in Fields:** High diesel costs and regional fuel shortages make harvesting economically unviable for some producers, leading to unharvested yields.
* **Fertilizer Deficits:** Upstream energy disruptions have sharply curtailed fertilizer availability, reducing crop yields globally.
* **Food Supply Shocks:** Unlike previous inflation waves driven primarily by supply redirection, the current environment involves an outright reduction in physical inputs, raising the risk of sharp food inflation and shortages heading into next year.

## Market Distortions and Macro Risks

Financial markets have been slow to price in the broader inflationary effects of these physical bottlenecks. Much of the equity market remains focused on capital expenditure around artificial intelligence, while sovereign bond yields have lagged the real rates necessary to account for sustained double-digit inflation risks.

As quarterly corporate earnings and physical delivery constraints force markets to reconcile with real-world energy deficits, the pressure on global supply chains is expected to mount.

## Summary

The global economy is facing severe structural pressure driven by refinery destruction, diesel supply shortages, and heavy-crude constraints. Because refined distillates underpin logistics, agriculture, and fertilizer production, the ongoing energy bottleneck poses a direct threat to food production and price stability across major Western economies.
