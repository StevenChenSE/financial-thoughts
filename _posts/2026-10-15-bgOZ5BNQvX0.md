## Introduction

On Wednesday, October 14th, the financial markets presented a complex picture of divergence. While headline indices remained near highs, underlying weakness emerged across key sectors. South Korea’s Kospi index fell for a sixth straight session, signaling potential trouble for the global chip trade. Simultaneously, defensive sectors like utilities and consumer staples began to rally, indicating a shift in risk appetite.

Joining the discussion was Cameron Dawson, Chief Investment Officer of New Edge Wealth and the Institutional Investor Research and Intelligence (Raia) Intel’s CIO of the Year for 2024. Dawson provided critical insights into the widening credit spreads, the implications of hot CPI data, and the sustainability of the AI-driven bull market.

## The Divergence: Breadth and the "Have and Have-Nots"

A central theme of the discussion was the dramatic lack of market breadth. Despite the S&P 500 trading just roughly 2% below its all-time highs, nearly 40% of the index was down 20% from its peaks. This "K-shaped" market performance suggests a severe deterioration in overall market health.

Dawson highlighted that this divergence is unsustainable. The strength seen in defensives—utilities, staples, and healthcare—is not a sign of healthy economic expansion but rather a "run for the hills" indicator. These sectors, which had been oversold and lagging for much of the year, began to outperform as investors sought safety amidst rising yields and credit concerns.

## Credit Spreads and the "Cascade" Effect

One of the most warning signs Dawson pointed to was the widening of credit spreads. For much of the year, high-yield spreads had been quiet, with only Triple C spreads (the lowest rung of credit) showing weakness. However, this weakness has now spilled over into broader high-yield markets.

> "We're seeing these cascades start to happen now... Triple C spreads, as I mentioned, high yield spreads are starting to widen out."

This cascade metaphor describes a "monster climbing up the capital stack," starting at the lowest quality credits and moving upward. When credit spreads widen, investors demand more compensation for risk, leading to tighter financial conditions. This tightening typically precedes equity market volatility and lower forward returns.

## The Semiconductor Warning: Price Leads Data

The sell-off in South Korean chipmakers, particularly SK Hynix, was identified as a leading indicator for the broader AI trade. Dawson emphasized a core principle of his investment philosophy: **"Price leads data."**

While earnings estimates for semiconductor companies were still rising, the price action told a different story. The July correction had established a "distributive top," and the subsequent failure to reclaim highs suggested weakness.

Key metrics discussed included EBIT margins for the semiconductor index:
*   **2025:** 16%
*   **2026:** 42%
*   **2027 Estimate:** 49%

Dawson questioned the sustainability of these margins, noting that 90% gross margins on manufacturing businesses are "supernormal profits" that rarely last forever. The market may be pricing in a peak in these margins before the data confirms it.

## The Fed’s Dilemma: Hot CPI and the Yield Curve

The release of CPI data showed headline consumer prices rising 3.7% in September, driven significantly by a 9.1% jump in gasoline. Core CPI remained elevated at 2.6%. This data complicated the Federal Reserve’s path, with prediction markets pricing in a higher probability of an October hike.

Dawson argued that the Fed might be "behind the curve." The 2-year Treasury yield had previously been pricing in three more hikes over the next two years. Although the 2-year yield softened slightly post-CPI, the broader bond market was still signaling tightening financial conditions.

### Is the Bond Market Doing the Fed’s Job?

The discussion touched on Christopher Waller’s assertion that the Fed does not control the 10-year yield, which is determined by global supply and demand. Dawson countered that while long-end yields are influenced by factors like fiscal policy and term premium, the Fed’s credibility and policy expectations directly impact long-term rates.

If the Fed fails to address inflation, term premiums and inflation break-evens could rise, forcing higher yields. This tightening of financial conditions acts as a de facto policy tool, slowing economic activity even without explicit rate hikes.

## The Sustainability of AI Capex

The AI trade relies heavily on hyperscaler capital expenditure (capex). Dawson noted that hyperscaler capex grew from 0% expected growth to nearly 100% actual growth this year. The critical question for 2027 is whether the estimated 30% growth forecast can be sustained.

The demand cycle flows from:
1.  **End Users:** Companies like Anthropic and OpenAI.
2.  **Cloud Providers:** Hyperscalers (Google, Microsoft, Amazon) providing infrastructure.
3.  **Chip Makers:** Beneficiaries like Nvidia and SK Hynix.

Dawson warned that while hyperscalers have strong balance sheets (e.g., Google has net negative debt), they rely on the capital markets to fund the next generation of AI labs. If AI startups struggle to raise capital, the entire chain—from cloud demand to chip orders—could face a slowdown.

## Conclusion: What’s Next?

Cameron Dawson’s outlook suggests a cautious approach. The mantra that "as long as earnings estimates are making new all-time highs, the market can make new highs" is showing cracks. With financials and semiconductors showing weakness, and credit spreads widening, the market may roll over before earnings do.

Investors should monitor:
*   **PPI Data:** As it feeds into PCE, which the Fed watches.
*   **Credit Spreads:** Continued widening is a red flag for equities.
*   **Defensive Leadership:** A rotation into utilities and staples is a harbinger of broader weakness.
*   **Fed Policy:** Whether the Fed will tighten financial conditions significantly to combat sticky inflation, potentially hurting household net worth which is increasingly tied to equity markets.

The market is currently a "have and have-nots" environment. As Dawson noted, "Nothing gold can stay," and the supernormal profits of the semiconductor sector may be facing their first true test of durability.
