## The Magnificent Seven Are No Longer the Winners—Here's Who's Winning Now

The market is undergoing a significant rotation, and the investment landscape looks different than it did just months ago. On Thursday, July 2nd, stocks sold off with tech leading the charge downward. The Nasdaq dropped 1.5%, the S&P fell about 64 basis points, while gold climbed 1% and Bitcoin surged 2.5%. This divergence between stocks and precious metals signals a potential shift in investor sentiment—a rotation that could define the second half of 2024.

Sam Roman, portfolio manager at Hedgeye Asset Management, joined the show to break down what's really happening in the markets, which tech plays look promising, and what the latest employment numbers mean for the economy and your portfolio.

**Understanding the Current Market Action**

"What you're seeing is a bit of an unwind," Roman explains. "Positioning-wise, a lot of people were very overweight semiconductors and related stocks related to the AI theme. I think the last couple of days you're starting to see that unwind and this is kind of accelerating today."

The beneficiaries of this rotation are clear: healthcare stocks, consumer staples, and financials are all up. Meanwhile, money is moving out of tech into everything else. Roman characterizes these market rotations as happening "very quickly, short, and very painful"—exactly what investors are experiencing right now.

**The Surprising Truth About the Magnificent Seven**

One of the most significant revelations from the interview: the Magnificent Seven has been the losers within tech year-to-date. The winners by a long stretch have been semiconductor stocks, memory stocks, and companies like Micron, SanDisk, and Applied Materials that are critical components of the data center build-out.

"Most of them are down year to date," Roman notes. "The Mag 7 stocks have shifted from being the innovators to perhaps the lower growth aspect of the tech sector."

This represents a fundamental shift in how the market views big tech. The hyperscalers—some of the Mag 7 companies—were spending massive amounts of money to fund the AI build-out, but the market decided to sell the spenders and buy the beneficiaries of that spend: semiconductors and memory stocks.

**Meta's Strategic Crisis and AI Existential Risk**

The discussion took a deep dive into Meta, where Roman sees significant challenges ahead. Meta announced a move into cloud computing, which potentially decreases demand for semiconductors—but Roman thinks the Meta news is "more of an excuse than a real reason for the big sell-off."

More concerning is Meta's strategic direction. The company started as the number one disruptor of social media, then rebranded into the metaverse, pulled the plug when losing too much money, and now CEO Mark Zuckerberg is "in search of another unicorn to chase after, which is AI."

"This guy hasn't innovated anything since the original Facebook," Roman observes. "He's bought everything since then—Instagram, WhatsApp—and now he's latching onto building an LLM and building out Meta compute to compete with the behemoths in the space."

The real existential risk Roman identifies: AI itself. If people spend more time interacting with AI agents on their phones—booking flights, travel, restaurants, and organizing their days—that's "less time on social media." Engagement drives Meta's advertising business, and losing that at the margin represents a significant problem.

Roman also highlights how Meta lacks the three critical elements of successful tech companies: control of compute, control of the app layer/user interface, and control of distribution. "Meta has compute but not as much as the other guys, and it's really mostly for internal use. They control the UI in terms of the apps, but the underlying platform is owned by other companies. Distribution—which is where the apps are based off of, like phones and devices—is also owned by other companies. So it's not a good spot to be in relative to the other competitors."

**Semiconductor Dependency and the CapEx Boom**

The semiconductor stocks that have outperformed are now dependent on the Mag 7, since they're the biggest customers. When the Korean Kospi index plunged 7.89%—the number one performing index in the world as of June—amid a shock triggered by Meta, sell orders poured in around chip stocks globally.

Yet Roman remains bullish on the underlying theme. "This is a capex boom, so capex drives the spending in those critical components. This is going to be a long-term boom, but the stocks have clearly gone up a lot. What you're seeing right now is a little bit of digestion. I don't think the capex is going to slow down anytime soon, this year or next year."

He expects hyperscalers to "all going to really reiterate their capex intentions for 2027" when announcing earnings later in July.

**The Jobs Numbers and Fed Policy Implications**

The employment picture adds another layer of complexity. June nonfarm payrolls increased by only 57,000—much lower than the expected 115,000 jobs that Wall Street consensus had forecasted.

Roman notes that this data has "become less valuable" over the years, suggesting other indicators like initial claims and ADP data might provide better real-time information. However, even if the labor market does cool further, he doesn't think it changes the underlying growth themes driving the economy.

"Reshoring is a massive investment theme affecting lots of industries within the economy," Roman explains. "With regards to AI and the data center build-out, I don't think a slowing of the jobs market really would affect that per se, because that's sort of spending that's going towards multiple years worth of investments in revenue expectations."

Hedgeye is forecasting bond yields have peaked and expects inflation to cool through the rest of the year. This would bring down bond yields, which Roman sees as "going to be a positive for the underlying economy because those high yields going back earlier this year were really, really problematic for anybody wanting to take out any credit." The firm is particularly positive on yield-sensitive, rate-sensitive industries like housing and financials.

**Where to Put Your Money: The Investment Framework**

Roman outlined his three-category investment framework:

1. **Companies with structural competitive advantages**: "Nothing is permanent. Moats are not permanent things. Winners from 10-15 years ago have no right to continue to be winners the next 10 years. They have to sustain that moat over time, and competition and disruption are forces that sometimes are not in these companies' control."

2. **S-curves and disruption**: Investing in multi-year investment cycles. AI is currently the biggest one, but throughout history, there have been numerous investment cycles "massive in scale that involved multiple industries."

3. **Special situations**: Companies going through transformation—new management teams, breakups, acquisitions, or idiosyncratic geopolitical events. "Those changes can be sometimes really lucrative from an investment perspective because the market never knows how to really value those assets separately within a large company."

**Apple: More Than Just a Device Company**

On Apple, Roman sees beyond the iPhone criticism. "The watch and AirPods, as separate categories, are probably in the top five or 10 largest consumer electronics categories in the world. The way I think about Apple is the ecosystem—the iOS ecosystem."

More importantly, Apple should be viewed as a platform company rather than a device company. "When you buy a phone or an Apple device, you are also buying a whole set of services attached to that. Those services are much higher margin than the hardware and they're growing at a faster rate than the hardware business."

Looking ahead, Roman expects "to see in the next 3 years with Apple, we're going to get really surprised by the pace of innovation by this company, not just in iPhones, but also with AI-related software features that are going to be part of what's going to drive a massive upgrade cycle in iPhones and other devices." Beyond that, he believes "Apple is going to be a serious player in home robotics."

**Alphabet and the AI Search Transformation**

Google's integration of AI-generated content into search results represents both an opportunity and a challenge. The DOJ ruling in Google's favor last August "really allowed Google to sort of push forward with their big initiatives, which is Gemini and their own model to offset some of the risks in search."

The AI overview at the top of search results may cannibalize some ad revenues, but Roman argues "what they lose on those blue lines below that they've historically monetized, they're gaining on the AI overviews because you can still link to certain things that are in that AI overview if you want more information."

YouTube remains "such an incredible asset" because it's hard to disrupt—user-generated content has value that AI-created content currently cannot replicate. "Open AI tried to do this with Sora, and nobody talks about Sora anymore," Roman notes.

**Robotics: The Next Frontier**

On home robotics, Roman is optimistic but realistic about the timeline. "I think there's going to be a testing phase and just people's discomfort with having a robot in the house. That's going to take a while."

However, Apple is working on a desktop or kitchen-top device with a screen on a swivel that moves with you, interacts through voice, and connects with your iOS profile. "That becomes a robotic extension of what you can access by typing or talking on a handheld device."

**The Future of AI: Commoditization and Specialized Models**

Looking at AI development, Roman predicts the major LLMs will ultimately become commoditized as they leapfrog each other. "Anthropic was the best model for a while, then Open AI came out with their latest model and became as good or even slightly better. These companies are all going to continue to innovate and get better, but they're all going to be pretty close to each other."

The real opportunity Roman sees: SLMs (small language models) or specialized language models that are proprietary to individual companies. "If a JP Morgan builds a model within JP Morgan that just processes and looks at all the data—the billions of transaction data in payments or transfers of money from one place to another over multiple years—that's going to be specialized data proprietary to them. Building those models and the compute to power them is a really big driver of future CapEx spending and AI spending that goes beyond just these big companies."

**The Bottom Line: Nasdaq 100 Prediction**

On the prediction market trade about whether the Nasdaq 100 will finish positive this year, Roman is confident. "I think it does end up positive. It doesn't mean that we can't have a tough couple of months over the summer as earnings season approaches. There's a lot of positioning problems within semiconductors where everybody's very overweight this space, so you could see some correction in the month, a few weeks to a month. But I think over the course of the rest of the year, I think it'll do well because this capex spend trend and boom is going to continue into 2027."

**Key Takeaways**

The market rotation from tech into other sectors represents a meaningful shift in how investors should think about the tech space. The Magnificent Seven are no longer the clear winners—semiconductor and memory stocks have taken that crown. Meta faces genuine existential risk from AI disrupting its core advertising model. Meanwhile, the AI capex boom remains intact and likely extends through 2027.

For investors, the key is understanding that moats are temporary, S-curves create multi-year investment opportunities, and the most valuable AI applications going forward may be specialized models proprietary to individual enterprises rather than consumer-facing LLMs.

The near-term pain of tech selling pressure should be viewed as an opportunity rather than a reason to abandon the sector entirely. Those with longer time horizons can use these corrections to position themselves for continued strength as the AI build-out continues to drive capital expenditure across the hyperscalers and their semiconductor suppliers.
