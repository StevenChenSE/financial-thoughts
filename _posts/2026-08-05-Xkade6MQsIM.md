## How sovereign debt, geopolitical choke points, and intervention are reshaping markets and creating asymmetric investment opportunities

### Introduction
In an August 2026 episode of Capital Controls, host Danny is joined by Chris MacIntosh, publisher of the Capitalist Exploits Insider newsletter, to dissect a rapidly evolving macro landscape. The conversation spans emergency currency intervention, the mechanics of sovereign debt, the geopolitics of energy, three looming financial bubbles, and why gold equities may be dramatically mispriced. The overarching theme: rising debt, expanding conflicts, and supply-side destruction are creating a highly inflationary environment where traditional narratives obscure the underlying drivers of markets.

### Emergency Intervention in the Japanese Yen
The discussion opens with emergency intervention by the Bank of Japan and the US Treasury to support the Japanese yen. MacIntosh argues this is not an isolated FX issue, but a symptom of deeper stress in the global bond market.

Japan is the largest holder of debt securities, including massive positions in US Treasuries. As war-related spending and domestic liabilities rise, the US needs Japan to continue buying and holding Treasuries. A collapsing yen would pressure Japan to sell US debt to fund energy imports and stabilize its currency. To prevent that, the US is effectively helping to prop up the yen, even selling euro holdings to buy yen—an intervention more about preserving the Treasury market than a fundamental bet on the yen.

This creates complex second-, third-, and fourth-order effects: selling euros to defend the yen, distorting other FX markets, and masking the reality that the entire system is being held together to allow continued debt issuance.

### Why Bonds Matter: Financing Operations and War
Bonds are how governments finance operations—domestic spending, pensions, and, critically, military operations. MacIntosh stresses that modern wars are also financial operations: capital flows to corporate entities linked to the “deep state” via government contracts. The bond market is the funding mechanism behind this entire apparatus.

With roughly 51% of US revenues going toward interest on an existing $39.6 trillion debt load, every incremental rise in rates adds billions to the interest bill. As rates climb, the system approaches a point where nearly all tax revenue is consumed by interest, leaving no room for principal repayment or other spending. Historically, governments respond by monetizing debt—printing money to devalue the currency and, by extension, the real value of bonds.

### The Rising Cost of Servicing National Debt
The core problem is simple: debt service costs are becoming unsustainable. As rates rise and maturities roll, the government must either:

- Raise taxes significantly,
- Cut spending dramatically, or
- Monetize debt and accept inflation.

MacIntosh argues the last option is the most likely. This path devalues fiat currency and bonds while benefiting hard assets. The bond market’s stability is therefore not a technical detail; it is the linchpin of the current financial and geopolitical order.

### Petro-Dollar Hegemony and Geopolitical Tensions
A central geopolitical driver is the need to preserve the petrodollar and US dollar hegemony. MacIntosh explains that US interventions in the Middle East, including the conflict with Iran, are not solely ideological. They are attempts to maintain dollar-denominated oil trade and global dollar demand.

On the Strait of Hormuz, MacIntosh speculates that any tolling arrangement might be negotiated in US dollars. Even if the US does not directly collect the toll revenue, mandating dollar pricing would create structural demand for dollars and support currency dominance. For Iran, this may only delay the inevitable erosion of the petrodollar, but for the US, it is a critical stopgap.

### Debt vs. Collateral: The Drive for Resource Acquisition
Debt is always underpinned by collateral. When debt rises faster than collateral, the system becomes fragile. One historical solution is conquest: acquiring land, resources, and strategic assets to expand the collateral base.

MacIntosh argues that the drive to seize or control trillions of dollars in resources—particularly in Russia and contested regions—is partly about bolstering collateral for failing debt systems. Wars are expensive and require more debt, creating a feedback loop: take on more debt to seize assets that justify more debt.

### Geopolitical Choke Points: Suez, Hormuz, and Beyond
Control of choke points is another way to generate revenue and exert power. Examples include:

- The Suez Canal: Egypt has long collected tolls.
- The Strait of Hormuz: Now a focal point of military and diplomatic maneuvering.
- The Bosphorus in Turkey: A critical supply route, with geopolitical narratives masking strategic interests.
- The Strait of Gibraltar: Tied to tensions around Spain and Morocco, with US political moves influencing recognition and control narratives.

These conflicts are often framed in moral or ideological terms, but MacIntosh sees them as contests over logistics, revenue streams, and strategic leverage.

### Japan’s Energy Crisis and Treasury Holdings
Japan’s position is especially precarious. As a net energy importer with no domestic supply, Japan must sell assets to fund imports. Simultaneously, a falling yen and rising inflation make holding bonds unattractive for Japanese institutions.

This creates a dual pressure: Japan needs to sell Treasuries for energy, and domestic investors want to sell bonds amid inflation. The US cannot afford Japan, its largest creditor, to unload Treasuries while Washington issues more debt to fund wars. Hence, the intervention: the US effectively takes partial control of yen policy, buying yen and selling euros to stabilize Japan’s ability to hold Treasuries.

### Europe’s Role in Global Conflicts and Debt Levels
European nations face debt levels at or worse than the US, but without the privilege of reserve currency status. MacIntosh argues Europe is desperate to secure Russian assets to shore up its own collateral. The US supports Europe technologically and logistically—via satellite imagery, drones, and intelligence—even if it avoids large-scale troop deployments.

There is also a domestic political dimension: war narratives provide a “villain of the day” to justify cutting pensions, healthcare, and social spending. Propaganda rallies the public around external threats while elites reduce domestic liabilities.

### Why War is Always Inflationary
MacIntosh emphasizes that wars are inherently inflationary, not because of demand growth, but because of supply destruction and higher costs:

- Choke points are disrupted, raising shipping costs.
- Insurers avoid high-risk routes, forcing longer, more expensive paths.
- Refineries and infrastructure are damaged, constraining supply.
- Governments monetize debt to fund military operations.

This is not the demand-led inflation of the Asian Tigers or China’s rise. It is cost-push, supply-constrained inflation, compounded by massive debt monetization—a “powder keg” for prices.

### Gold vs. Oil: Navigating Commodity Volatility
Escalation often creates a short-term inverse relationship between oil and gold:

- Escalation → bullish oil, bearish gold.
- De-escalation → bearish oil, bullish gold.

MacIntosh advises investors not to overtrade this dynamic. Both assets can be volatile, with sharp drawdowns and resumption of trends. For long-term investors, the focus is on asset allocation and percentage positioning, not timing every move.

### The Significant Mispricing of Gold Equities
MacIntosh highlights gold equities as one of the most mispriced assets in the market:

- Gold miner free cash flow is roughly 10x higher than in 2020.
- Net debt has been cut in half.
- Many miners are cash-rich, low-debt, high-yielding, yet priced as if gold will collapse.

He describes this as a “priced for catastrophe” scenario: the market is assuming gold will fall, miners’ margins will evaporate, and companies will fail. Given the debt-driven inflationary environment, MacIntosh sees this as highly improbable.

He also notes Trump’s expected roundtable with mining executives, suggesting a political push to expand domestic resource production.

### Global Energy Markets and Refining Interdependence
The US narrative of being a net energy exporter is oversimplified. The US exports crude and some distillates, but much of its crude is shipped abroad for refining—particularly to the Middle East and Asia—then sent back as refined products.

Iran’s threats to attack Gulf energy infrastructure would directly impact the US. Additionally, a significant portion of US refined oil comes from Saudi crude processed in places like Louisiana. Disruptions to Middle Eastern refining capacity therefore have material domestic consequences.

MacIntosh argues the US strategy includes:

- Restricting global supplies to force business back into the North American hemisphere.
- Consolidating influence over Canada, Mexico, Brazil, and Argentina.
- Leveraging cheap, relatively uncontested Latin American resources and supply chains.

### Three Simultaneous Bubbles: Private Equity, AI, and Sovereign Debt
MacIntosh identifies three interconnected bubbles:

1. Private Equity / Private Credit
   - Many firms have “gated” redemptions, preventing investors from withdrawing.
   - Assets bought in the low-rate environment (e.g., 2021) are now being marked to market as debt matures and lenders demand higher coupons.
   - Sales at 20–40% below par could wipe out equity and cascade through pension funds that were sold these assets as “safe.”

2. AI “Make Seven” Bubble
   - Valuations are predicated on continued low rates and near-infinite growth assumptions.
   - Higher rates and slower monetization could expose overvaluation.

3. Sovereign Debt
   - The “big daddy” bubble: unsustainable debt levels, rising rates, and reliance on monetization.
   - All three bubbles are linked through feedback loops in financing, valuation, and confidence.

Looking at gold versus the S&P 500 over the long term, MacIntosh notes that recent volatility is small in historical perspective. Meanwhile, the S&P is increasingly concentrated in companies trading at extreme multiples—roughly 40% at around 10x sales.

Gold, by contrast, remains cheap relative to equities over the long run. The implication: as debt and inflation pressures mount, capital may increasingly rotate toward real assets.

### Gold-to-Oil Ratio and Miner Margins
MacIntosh introduces the gold-to-oil ratio as a proxy for gold miner margins:

- Gold is the output; oil is a major input cost.
- The GDX gold miner index is trading at an index level of about 1.5 relative to historical norms, compared to peaks near 7.7.

This suggests that, given current gold and oil prices, gold miners are undervalued. If gold rises and input costs remain manageable, margin expansion could be significant.

### Investment Strategy: Eliminating the Overvalued
MacIntosh outlines a simple, probabilistic approach:

- Eliminate overvalued, high-risk assets using standard metrics: P/E, price-to-sales, debt levels.
- What remains tends to be emerging markets and commodities—sectors with no compelling narrative, widely disliked, and therefore cheap.
- Build a diversified portfolio of “asymmetrical” opportunities where downside is limited and upside is substantial.

He emphasizes patience: decide your position size (e.g., 5–10% in gold equities), then wait. Trying to time triggers often leads to buying after moves have already occurred.

### Analyzing Trump’s Comments on Oil Company Profits
MacIntosh critiques recent comments by President Trump claiming oil companies are “making too much money” and should lower prices. He points out fundamental misunderstandings:

- Producers like ExxonMobil and Chevron do not set retail gas prices.
- Refiners and distributors determine pump prices, influenced by refining capacity, logistics, and geopolitics.
- Capping profits disincentivizes refining and production exactly when supply is constrained.

He sees this as either political pandering or potential groundwork for asset nationalization, but in either case, economically counterproductive.

### Supply Constraints and Disincentivizing Production
With refineries damaged in Russia and the Middle East, global refining capacity is under pressure. MacIntosh warns:

- Politicians demand higher production while attacking the profitability of the companies needed to deliver it.
- There is no “tap” to turn on for more oil; expanding production requires drilling, infrastructure, labor, and capital—none of which appear overnight.

This political myopia exacerbates supply constraints and inflationary pressures.
