## The Case for TSMC: Steady, High-Quality, and Underpriced

During a panel discussion at Consensus Miami, John Woo—president of Ava Labs and former portfolio manager at Kingdom Capital—delivered a straightforward message about Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company: it's historically been a low-volatility, steady, high-quality business trading at a very fair valuation. But here's the nuance that most investors miss.

"Relative to itself, it's probably more expensive because it sits in such an important place and they have the best at what they do," Woo explained. "But relative to other AI stocks, it's still very cheap, especially considering the growth rates."

This framing matters enormously. When investors compare TSMC to its own historical averages, it appears pricier than usual. But zoom out to the broader AI landscape—Nvidia, AMD, the speculative software plays—and TSMC looks like a bargain given its critical position in the semiconductor food chain.

### The Taiwan Discount: Location Risk or Opportunity?

Woo acknowledges there's a geographic risk premium baked into TSMC's valuation. "I think there's a little discount for the location," he noted, citing proximity to China as one factor. "But also, people are not aware of this company as much as they would if they were a US company. US companies just get naturally more publicity."

This media and analyst attention gap creates an opening for sophisticated investors. While American retail investors gravitate toward recognizable brand names, the actual scarcity value in AI infrastructure—where chips are fabricated—remains concentrated in Asia.

## Asia's Hidden Advantage: The Hardware Layer of AI

TSMC isn't alone. Woo points to a broader ecosystem of Asian companies dominating the physical layer of AI infrastructure:

- **Memory companies in Korea**: Samsung and SK Hynix control critical HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) supply
- **Tokyo Electron in Japan**: A leading supplier of semiconductor manufacturing equipment
- **Broader Taiwan**: An entire supply chain ecosystem that Western competitors struggle to replicate

"Asia is very good at the hardware and the electrical engineering layer of AI, which is really where a lot of the things that are in scarcity are," Woo observed. "There's a lot of good things to look at in Asian companies, and TSMC is probably at the very top of that list."

### The Work Culture Factor: Can America Catch Up?

Panelists raised a pointed question: can the United States actually achieve President Trump's stated goal of 50% domestic chip manufacturing? Woo's answer was frank.

"In Asia, I know a lot of engineers, they work three shifts," he said. "Whenever there's an earthquake, all the engineers don't go to their kids or their wives. They head straight back to their companies."

While American culture prizes entrepreneurship and risk-taking—"if you can blend those two things together, that's the ideal scenario"—the deep operational expertise embedded in Asian semiconductor ecosystems doesn't replicate overnight. Intel, Woo suggested, remains "very far away" from matching TSMC's foundry capabilities, despite recent government support and stock surges driven by CPU scarcity concerns tied to the agentic AI wave.

### Sustaining the Technological Edge

The critical thesis for TSMC investors rests on one question: how long can the company maintain its competitive moat?

Woo acknowledges the challenge in a technology landscape where "tech cycles when I started investing were longer, tech cycles and the economic cycles are far shorter."

> "The only edge—even in quant trading firms—these quant models have a half-life that gets shorter and shorter. This is why some of these firms have a gazillion PhDs, because new models have to come out. It's constant innovation."

Yet TSMC's position is different from pure software plays. Fabrication excellence requires decades of process refinement, massive capital expenditure, and institutional knowledge that new entrants cannot simply purchase or replicate through acquisition.

## Building Your AI Portfolio: Individual vs. Professional Strategies

The conversation turned to portfolio construction, with Woo distinguishing between two investor types:

### For Individual Investors

Woo sees this as a "super exciting time" reminiscent of the early internet era. His advice: identify the potential Amazon, Google, and Nvidia equivalents of the AI age—and hold them through volatility.

> "If you can find a portfolio, you're going to get some of them wrong. But if you identify Google, Netflix, and Amazon back then, just hold it and don't worry about the ups and downs."

The key is staying power. Individual investors with long time horizons can afford to ignore short-term turbulence that forces professional managers into difficult positioning decisions.

### For Professional Investors: Following the Bottlenecks

Professional portfolio managers have become "very good at identifying each bottleneck," Woo noted. This creates visible market spikes as capital rotates through successive points of scarcity:

- **Nvidia GPUs**: The initial AI boom
- **Networking infrastructure**: The next bottleneck
- **Photonics and optics**: Bandwidth constraints drive demand
- **Memory**: Micron, SK Hynix, and Samsung capture value

Micron serves as a perfect case study. "On P/E basis, it's still cheap," Woo explained. "But people don't know how long [the cycle] could last, yet the stock looks like it should be trading with no earnings, and yet it's trading less than 10 times next year's earnings."

The memory sector demonstrates how professional investors anticipate bottlenecks before they become obvious to the public market.

## The Memory Paradox: Will AI Models Disrupt RAM Companies?

A provocative question emerged: why invest in RAM companies when AI model innovation aims to reduce memory usage? When Google announced Turbo Trich from Gemini, Micron dropped 12-15% in a single session—before recovering and ultimately rising 50% higher.

Woo pushed back on both assumptions embedded in the question:

1. **Memory companies are innovating too**: They aren't standing still while their customers evolve
2. **Supply chain integration matters**: You can't simply swap one tech component into an existing stack without replacing the entire ecosystem

> "When you take one piece of tech out, doesn't mean you can just simply put another piece in because the next piece has to fit in with everything that already exists in that tech stack."

He pointed to a pattern反复: When engineers at his company discovered they could reduce third-party service provider costs, SAS stocks would drop—yet those incumbents still exist and thrive. "You cannot disintermediate code at the core consensus, the core blockchain, the deep tech. It's not being replicated."

The travel industry analogy lands clearly: every time Google was rumored to enter online travel, Expedia, Priceline, and Booking.com stocks would crash. "25 years later, they're still here and doing better. And somehow Google never was able to get into travel."

## Bitcoin's New Chapter: Beyond the Old Guard

Shifting to cryptocurrency, Woo discussed Bitcoin's surge past $81,000 and the broader implications for digital assets.

### The Bear Market Question

Tom Lee's framework holds that if Bitcoin closes above $76,000 for three consecutive months, the bear market is definitively over. At current valuations, this threshold has already been crossed.

Woo agrees with Lee's statistical rigor—and sees fundamental reasons for optimism:

**The Changing of the Guard**: "We saw a changing of the guard. The old school Bitcoin people were selling into MicroStrategy and the ETFs. I think that uncertainty has passed."

Many "OG whales" who entered during crypto's early phases have rotated out—not necessarily due to conviction loss, but because "there's a natural time for some of those people, especially given the liquidity was finally there too."

Woo draws an analogy to serial entrepreneurs: "Why don't you just go from 0 to 10? Because some people like 0 to 1, and some people like 1 to 10."

Crypto's maturation from novelty to established asset class mirrors this transition.

### The Wealth Channel Opportunity

Institutional adoption through registered investment advisors (RIAs) represents a multi-quarter catalyst. The process moves deliberately:

1. Regulatory clarity enables RIAs to recommend crypto
2. Education cascades through advisor networks
3. Advisors educated on the product then recommend to clients
4. End users gain sufficient understanding to act

"This process takes four, five, six months," Woo noted. "And it's now in May. So we're getting to the point where since the beginning of the year when these channels were allowed to start recommending it... the end user knows it enough."

The macro environment also supports crypto strength: calm conditions, reasonable liquidity, and continued hope for regulatory clarity (the "Clarity Act").

## Avalanche's Infrastructure Play: Powering Tokenized Real World Assets

The conversation pivoted to Avalanche's partnership with Broadridge Financial Solutions—a move Woo describes as "fantastic" and "very important."

### What Broadridge Brings

Broadridge is a "global technology leader that processes $8 trillion in tokenized assets every month." Their traditional role involves corporate actions, proxy votes, and the unglamorous but essential plumbing of securities ownership.

"They announced and we're very proud to say a deal where they're going to do the online token on-chain tokenized equities, and put that record on an Avalanche blockchain," Woo explained. Galaxy Equity will be the first public equity tokenized through this infrastructure.

### The Two-Layer Framework

Woo frames Avalanche's architecture through a two-layer lens:

| Layer | Components |
|-------|-----------|
| **Asset Layer** | Stablecoins, money market funds, treasuries, private credit, private equity, tokenized public equities |
| **Infrastructure Layer** | Corporate actions processing (Broadridge), compliance systems, custody solutions, wallet and off-ramp services |

> "Everyone wants tokenized real world assets, but they forget you need systems underneath that to make those things smooth."

This infrastructure layer represents the unsexy but critical work that enables institutional participation—the proxy voting systems, regulatory compliance frameworks, and settlement processes that make ownership claims legally enforceable.

### Value Capture in the Avalanche Ecosystem

On the question of where value ultimately accrues—DApps, layer ones, or infrastructure—Woo sees a distributed model:

- **DApps** (decentralized applications) must capture sufficient value to sustain operations
- **Layer One protocols** must design token economics that fund continued development
- **Infrastructure providers** (like Avalanche) capture value from enterprises spinning up bespoke chains

Avalanche's "network of networks" architecture enables enterprises to deploy private, permissioned chains that connect to the broader ecosystem when advantageous. "They capture value already just because they're in their own private chain," Woo noted. "We capture a little bit of value because they pay us to do that."

Straits X, a Singapore-based stablecoin provider building on Avalanche, exemplifies this dynamic. Connected to Grab Pay and Alipay, Straits X chose Avalanche precisely because of its technical capabilities—not through subsidies or artificial incentives.

> "It starts with the customer having to be able to create value for themselves. And they are already doing it. And then once they start connecting, the natural value will flow through to the rest of the Avalanche ecosystem."

## The Modern Investor's Dilemma: Quant Dominance and Retail Participation

Woo expressed ambivalence about how markets have evolved since his hedge fund days:

**The Good**: Individual investors now participate directly in markets that were once exclusively institutional domains. The democratization of finance is real.

**The Concerning**: "Stocks more than ever are highly correlated and move up and down together, sometimes nothing to do with the stock itself, just because of the weightings of the ETF."

When Palantir—hailed as an AI software winner—experiences a security scare, unrelated Palantir-holding ETFs sell off. When Micron drops on a Google memory efficiency announcement, entire semiconductor ETFs follow despite underlying business strength.

Woo's concern centers on valuation discipline. "In the long term, and this is why Warren Buffett has been great, the power of compounding and buying relatively fairly valued companies will win out."

The cautionary tale remains: Internet-era investors who loaded up on Pets.com never recovered. Those who held the 10-15 quality names—or bought an index—fared dramatically better.

## Key Takeaways

1. **TSMC trades at a "Taiwan discount" relative to US peers despite superior execution and critical AI infrastructure positioning.** Patient investors who recognize the company's quality and understand the work culture advantages over Western competitors may find compelling risk-adjusted returns.

2. **Asia dominates the physical layer of AI—the hardware and electrical engineering components where scarcity is most acute.** Beyond TSMC, companies like Samsung, SK Hynix, and Tokyo Electron represent underexamined opportunities for investors willing to dig into supply chain dynamics.

3. **Professional investors follow bottlenecks through the AI infrastructure stack—from GPUs to networking to memory.** This rotation creates exploitable patterns as successive points of constraint become obvious to institutional flows.

4. **Tech announcements don't automatically destroy incumbent businesses.** Supply chain integration, corporate systems, and the friction of replacing entire technology stacks mean that even technically superior solutions take years to displace established players.

5. **Institutional crypto adoption is a multi-quarter process, not an overnight event.** RIAs began educating clients on Bitcoin in early 2025; by mid-year, that education is translating into allocation decisions—creating a sustained tailwind beyond initial regulatory catalysts.

6. **The real money in tokenized assets flows through infrastructure, not just applications.** Companies building corporate action systems, compliance tools, and settlement infrastructure (like Avalanche's Broadridge partnership) capture essential value that enables the broader tokenization opportunity.
