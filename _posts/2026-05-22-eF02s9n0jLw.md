## The AI Hardware Opportunity Hidden in Plain Sight

TSMC remains one of the most undervalued stories in the AI revolution—despite being the backbone of virtually every advanced chip in existence. That's the core argument John Woo, president of Ava Labs and former Wall Street tech investor, made during a recent appearance at Consensus Miami alongside hosts David and Bonnie.

"TSMC is historically a low-volatility, very steady, high-quality company," Woo explained. "Relative to itself, it's probably more expensive because it sits in such an important place and they're the best at what they do. But relative to other AI stocks, it's still very cheap, especially considering the growth rates."

The location discount applied to Taiwan's semiconductor giant is real, Woo acknowledges. Proximity to China creates geopolitical risk that markets still price in, and US companies naturally receive more investor attention simply due to cultural familiarity. But this creates an opportunity: when comparing two equal companies, the one with less marketing buzz often trades at a discount despite equal or superior fundamentals.

## Asia's Edge in the AI Hardware Stack

Taiwan's TSMC sits at the apex of a broader Asian semiconductor ecosystem that often flies under Western investors' radar.

The region excels at what Woo calls "the hardware and the electrical engineering layer of AI"—precisely the components experiencing the most acute scarcity during the AI boom. Korea's memory manufacturers, Japan's Tokyo Electron, and Taiwan's broader supply chain all represent compelling exposure to AI infrastructure that doesn't require investors to pick specific AI application winners.

"TSMC is probably at the very top of that list," Woo said, "but there's a lot of good things to look at in Asia companies."

### The Work Ethic Question: East vs. West

When asked whether US government support for domestic chip manufacturing—evidenced by Intel's post-policy stock surge—could challenge Asian dominance, Woo offered a nuanced perspective that transcended simple nationalism.

Woo recounted conversations with engineers in Taiwan who work three-shift schedules, noting that during earthquakes, engineers reportedly leave their families to report immediately to their companies. He acknowledged Japan's cultural acceptance of extreme work dedication, including terminology for dying at one's desk as a noble outcome—though he expressed some skepticism about that framing.

Yet Woo resists treating this as a simple East-over-West equation. "You're not like Americans don't work hard either," he pushed back. "What Americans have is a slightly different culture. They have a very entrepreneurial culture. They're a little bit more risk-taking."

His ideal scenario: blending both work ethics. Early Ava Labs days proved this thesis, with a 10-person founding team of Cornell engineers—many Asian—combining hard work ethic with entrepreneurial boldness.

## Can TSMC Maintain Its Technological Edge?

The persistent bull case for TSMC rests on its technological moat. But in 2026, are moats eroding faster than ever?

Woo's assessment is sobering. "It's unfortunate that the tech cycles when I started investing—tech cycles were longer. Tech cycles and the economic cycles are far shorter now," he said. "The reality of the world we live in in technology today is that consistent and constant innovation can be an edge."

He draws an analogy to quantitative trading: "Even in quant trading firms, these quant models have a half-life that gets shorter and shorter. This is why some of these firms have a gazillion PhDs—because new models have to come out. It's constant innovation."

The implication is that TSMC's edge, while significant, requires relentless investment and execution rather than assuming current dominance will persist indefinitely.

## Building Your Portfolio for the AI Age

### For Individual Investors: Think Like the Early Internet Era

Woo distinguishes between professional and individual portfolio strategies, and his advice for retail investors is surprisingly simple: find the Amazons, Googles, and Nvidias of today and hold them.

"This is a super exciting time. It reminds me of the internet days," Woo said. "If you can find a portfolio—you're going to get some of them wrong—but if you can find the Amazon and the Google and I guess Nvidia of back in the day today, and as an individual, you just don't have to deal with the day-to-day volatility. If you can find a portfolio of potentially the best in various parts of the AI world going forward, you just sit with it and hold it for a while."

The internet era's lesson remains relevant: investors who bought pets.com never recovered, but those who identified even a few of the eventual winners—or simply bought an index—flourished.

### For Professional Investors: Following the Bottleneck Chain

Professional money managers have become adept at identifying sequential supply chain bottlenecks, Woo explains. The pattern is predictable: initial focus on Nvidia, then networking infrastructure, then photonics, then memory.

Micron represents a textbook case study. "Micron was supposed to be a cyclical slow memory business," Woo noted. "On PE basis it's still cheap, but people don't know how long it could last. The stock looks like it should be trading with no earnings, yet it's trading—I think—less than 10 times next year's earnings."

The stock's volatility illustrates another pattern Woo has observed: when Google announced Turbo Query (reducing memory requirements), Micron dropped 12% in a day—yet it subsequently surged 50% higher.

This reaction reveals a misunderstanding about how tech infrastructure actually evolves.

## Why Memory Companies Aren't as Vulnerable as They Look

The counterintuitive resilience of memory companies against efficiency innovations stems from two factors investors often overlook:

- **Memory companies innovate too.** The assumption that established players aren't advancing their technology ignores constant R&D investment and deep customer relationships.

- **You can't replace just one piece of a tech stack.** When introducing new components, they must integrate with everything already existing in that system. "You have to replace the whole tech stack if you want to replace one piece," Woo explained. "That's literally one of the things that the crypto industry is learning."

Woo referenced a consistent historical pattern: whenever Google announced travel ambitions, Expedia and Booking.com stocks would crater—yet 25 years later, those companies "are still here and doing better."

## The Bitcoin Transition: Old Guard Out, New Guard In

Bitcoin's trajectory past $76,000 (trading around $81,000 at the time of recording) signals a critical transition, in Woo's view. If Bitcoin sustains above $76,000 at month-end, traditional metrics would declare the bear market over.

Woo credits two structural shifts:

**The shareholder turnover has already occurred.** The previous cycle saw "old school Bitcoin people" selling into MicroStrategy and ETFs for liquidity. "We're seeing a changing of the guard," Woo explained. "The old guard that were the old school Bitcoin people were selling into MicroStrategy and the ETFs. I think that was stabilizing back earlier this year."

**The wealth channel education cycle is completing.** Institutional wealth management channels manage trillions in assets. When regulations allowed them to recommend crypto products, the process of educating individual relationship managers began—but that takes time. "You need those individual RAs to get educated," Woo said. "After they're educated on the product, then they recommend it to their clients. That process takes four, five, six months."

By May, that education cycle was reaching completion, with end-users finally understanding the products well enough to act.

### Crypto's Maturation: From Zero to One

Bitcoin is approaching 18 years old, and Woo sees a natural maturation occurring—not unlike serial entrepreneurs who build companies from zero to one, then hand them off to operators who scale from one to ten.

"Crypto is going into a new phase right now. It's no longer the new new thing," Woo observed. "It's the equivalent of asking why there are serial entrepreneurs who take a company from zero to one, hand it off to someone who goes from one to ten. Some people like zero to one, and some people like one to ten."

The liquidity available through ETFs and institutional products has accelerated this transition, as original believers finally had exits that earlier markets couldn't provide.

## The Institutional Finance Inflection Point

Ava Labs' partnership with Broadridge Financial Solutions represents precisely the kind of infrastructure development that will drive crypto's institutional future, Woo argues.

Broadridge processes $8 trillion in tokenized assets monthly and serves traditional finance by handling corporate actions, proxy voting, and the behind-the-scenes mechanics of equity ownership. Their partnership with Avalanche will move tokenized equities on-chain, starting with Galaxy equity.

"Broadridge is one of those partners and pieces that help the infrastructure layer of the future world of tokenized real world assets," Woo explained.

The vision is dual-layer: an **asset layer** encompassing stablecoins, tokenized money markets, treasuries, private credit, private equity, and public equity, sitting atop an **infrastructure layer** that handles compliance, corporate actions, custody, and all the unsexy-but-essential systems that make ownership work.

### Straits X and the Asian Stablecoin Opportunity

Singapore's Straits X represents another critical partnership, building Asia-oriented stablecoin infrastructure on Avalanche and connecting into payment giants like GrabPay and Alipay.

"We want to help businesses, and what we've done is create basically the components and the pieces," Woo said. "We think of it as there's an asset layer and then there's an infrastructure layer."

Avalanche's architecture supports this vision through its network-of-networks approach: traditional finance partners can run closed, private chains for internal operations while maintaining the ability to connect to Avalanche's broader ecosystem for liquidity and cross-chain functionality.

## Key Takeaways

1. **TSMC remains attractive despite its stature**—historically cheap relative to AI peers given its critical role and steady performance, with a modest location/geopolitical discount providing entry opportunity.

2. **Asia dominates the hardware layer of AI**, and TSMC is merely the most prominent example; Korean memory makers, Japanese equipment suppliers, and Taiwan's broader ecosystem all warrant investor attention.

3. **Constant innovation—not static moats—sustains competitive advantage** in modern tech markets; the same dynamic affecting quant trading models applies to semiconductor companies.

4. **Individual investors should think in decade-long horizons**, identifying potential category winners and holding through volatility, rather than attempting to trade around news events that often produce temporary overreactions.

5. **Bitcoin's structural transition is real**: old-cycle holders have largely rotated out, institutional wealth channels are completing their education cycle, and crypto is maturing from speculative novelty toward institutional infrastructure.
