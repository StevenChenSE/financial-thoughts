**March 23** – The Strait of Hormuz, a maritime chokepoint responsible for 20% of the world’s daily oil shipments, remains effectively closed. More than 70% of the oil that normally transits the strait goes to Asia, and at the current rate, Vietnam, Bangladesh, and the Philippines are estimated to run out of oil within three weeks. China has halted fuel exports to guard domestic inventories. But even if the strait reopens tomorrow, long-term damage has already been done to global energy supply chains.

This morning, the head of the International Energy Agency (IEA) warned that at least 40 energy assets across nine Middle Eastern countries have been severely or very severely damaged since the Iran war began on February 28. The IEA’s executive director called the crisis “equivalent to the two oil shocks of the 1970s and the 2022 gas crisis put together.” Shipping flow through the Strait of Hormuz is at roughly 5% of normal levels. The IEA has already released a record 400 million barrels from emergency stockpiles and signaled readiness for another release.

Oil prices swung violently today after President Donald Trump posted on Truth Social claiming “very productive and good conversations with Iran.” Iran swiftly denied any talks, with state media saying “no talks with the US have taken place.” Brent crude plunged from above $113 to below $100 in under an hour, erasing roughly $17 a barrel in one of the largest intraday swings on record. West Texas Intermediate (WTI) hovered around $89.

Equity markets rallied on the news: the S&P 500 and Dow Jones jumped sharply, while gold fell to its lowest level of 2026, dropping below $4,300 as rising yields and a strong dollar crushed the traditional safe-haven trade. Diesel futures are up 57% this month—the largest single-month spike on record—and historical correlations between diesel and CPI point to inflation north of 8% in the coming months.

The crisis extends beyond energy. The entire semiconductor industry is at risk due to helium supply disruptions. Helium is critical for transferring heat in semiconductor manufacturing. According to the U.S. Geological Survey, Qatar produces 30% of the world’s helium. Key semiconductor manufacturing countries rely heavily on this supply: in 2025, South Korea bought 55% of its helium from the Gulf Cooperation Council, while Taiwan bought 69%.

Is this shaping up to be one of the worst global supply chain disasters in history?

## A Conversation with Doomberg

To help unpack the situation, we spoke with Doomberg, head writer of the Doomberg Substack, on the geopolitical and market implications of the ongoing crisis.

**On Trump’s Truth Social Posts and Market Whiplash**

Over the weekend, Trump issued a stark ultimatum to Iran: “If Iran doesn't fully open without retreat, without threat, the Strait of Hormuz within 48 hours… the United States of America will hit and obliterate their various power plants.” Then, on Monday morning before markets opened, he posted about “productive” talks with Iran—a claim Iran denies.

“It's really hard to do analysis when you have such whiplash,” Doomberg noted. “If we take this morning's post, there's only two real possibilities: what the president said is true, or what the president said is not true.”

He outlined the evidence for each scenario. On one hand, Trump is the president, and markets reacted with hope. On the other, “Trump has posted many things that turned out to be not true in the past,” and the timing—just before market open—is a “recurring pattern.”

“If the post is true, count us amongst those who are happy that negotiations are ongoing and that the war might soon end,” Doomberg said. “And if it's not true, then we have big, big issues to ponder.”

**On Escalation and “Red Lines”**

Doomberg framed the conflict through a simple model: “A war is won when one side inflicts more pain than the other side can tolerate.” Trump’s threat to destroy Iran’s electricity grid was an attempt to inflict such pain. However, “it's pretty clear that Trump is not ready to tolerate significant stock market declines in order to prosecute this war… Trump does not want to see oil prices go higher and Trump does not want to see stock prices go lower.”

Iran, meanwhile, has threatened to retaliate against Arab oil and gas facilities and Israeli desalination plants if its power grid is attacked. Doomberg believes Iran has demonstrated its capability to follow through, citing an attack on Qatar’s Ras Laffan LNG facility just hours after Israel struck Iran’s South Pars gas field.

“The Middle East is a collection of very fragile light bulbs that are easy to break,” he said. “The question is, is that pain that the West is willing to tolerate in order to win the war?”

**On a Potential “Trap” for the U.S. in Cuba and Taiwan**

Doomberg raised a provocative theory linking Cuba, Taiwan, and the broader geopolitical chessboard. Currently, the U.S. is enforcing an embargo on Cuba, and Russia has sent tankers to break it. “Will Trump allow these Russian tankers to reach Cuba?” he asked. “It's a replay of the Cuban missile crisis without the missiles.”

He suggested this could be a test: if the U.S. stops the tankers, it sets a precedent for China to impose its own embargo on Taiwan while the U.S. is distracted in the Middle East. China recently offered to collaborate with Taiwan on energy security—an offer Taiwan rejected—and has since increased naval exercises around the island.

“Taiwan is in a very precarious position,” Doomberg explained. “They rely on imports for essentially 100% of their hydrocarbons… If China's navy surrounds Taiwan and says, ‘No LNG tankers should come here,’… we've seen that the mere threat of hostilities against cargo ships in the Strait of Hormuz has led to the effective closure of the strait.”

**On How the War Ends—and Why the Strait May Stay Closed**

Asked how long the conflict could last, Doomberg was pessimistic. “I honestly think the war is going to go on for a very long time,” he said. “Not enough weight is being given to what the other side is saying and what they're doing… The Iranians will open the strait when the Iranians decide to open the strait.”

He argued that militarily reopening the strait is nearly impossible as long as Iran can launch drones and missiles at shipping. “All it takes is a few drones fired from great distance and the threat of more drones… If you're an owner of an oil tanker… are you going to run that gauntlet?”

The only ways to reopen the strait, in his view, are to change Iran’s regime or come to terms with it. Regime change could backfire, leaving a fractured state unable to secure the strait. And Iran’s current terms—including the U.S. abandoning its Middle Eastern military bases—are “non-starters.”

“We seem to be in this vice, this impossible vice, where the world needs that strait to open, Trump needs that strait to open, but the pathway to doing it is impossible.”

**On Damaged Infrastructure and Market Complacency**

The physical damage to energy infrastructure is severe and lasting. Doomberg agreed with the IEA’s assessment, noting that the Ras Laffan facility alone supplies 20% of global LNG, and even a partial shutdown will take months to repair.

Despite this, he expressed surprise at the relative calm in oil markets. “I'm shocked at the complacency of the market… Why Brent is only sitting at $100 as we're talking is a real mystery.”

He attributed WTI’s lower price to U.S. energy independence and SPR releases, but warned that the global system is under extreme stress. “This is profoundly consequential.”

## The Bottom Line

The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has triggered a multi-faceted crisis affecting energy markets, inflation, semiconductor supplies, and global trade routes. Geopolitically, the U.S. and Iran are locked in a high-stakes standoff with no clear exit. As Doomberg summarized, the war may continue for a long time—and the market’s hope for a quick resolution rests largely on the unpredictable statements of a president posting on Truth Social.

For now, the world watches, waits, and braces for the next turn in a crisis with no easy answers.
