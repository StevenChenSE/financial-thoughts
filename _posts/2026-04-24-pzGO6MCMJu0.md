The numbers are staggering and the implications are enormous, yet the mainstream financial media remains largely silent. In the first quarter of 2026, China imported nearly 48 million ounces of silver—more than double what the country imported in any previous full year. Turkey, a nation of roughly 85 million people, imported another 23.5 million ounces in just three months. Meanwhile, the COMEX warehouses in New York—supposedly the world's most liquid marketplace for precious metals—have seen a net outflow of 132 million ounces of silver and 6.6 million ounces of gold. This represents the largest sustained drain of physical precious metals from Western vaults in modern history.

Ed Steer, veteran precious metals analyst and editor of the Ed Steer Gold & Silver Newsletter, broke down these extraordinary figures in a recent appearance on Capital Cosm, painting a picture of a global monetary system under profound structural stress.

## China's Remarkable Pivot: From Net Exporter to Record Importer

The transformation in China's silver trade position reads like a geopolitical case study in supply chain strategy. Throughout most of 2025, China was a net exporter of silver—selling the metal into Western markets at what now appear to be bargain basement prices. Then, seemingly overnight, everything changed.

"Look at the chart," Steer instructed, referencing a decade-long visualization of Chinese import and export data. "You can see steady movement until the last six months, when there's this tremendous spike in imports. March was the last big spike on the right-hand side—24.8 million ounces. February was 14.5 million. January was 8.5 million. For a total of 47.8 million ounces imported so far this year."

What's particularly striking is the speed of this reversal. Six months ago, China recorded its largest net export month in history. In the six months since, it has swung to its largest net import position ever recorded.

"In six months they've gone from a record net export to a record net import," Steer emphasized. "That is absolutely huge. It tells you right there that they've become a massive importer of silver in the last six months, and they've cut back on their exports considerably."

## The Numbers Behind the Drain

The data from COMEX in New York paints an equally compelling picture of structural depletion. According to Steer's analysis:

### Silver Movements at COMEX (Q1 2026)

- **Silver received**: 20.7 million ounces
- **Silver shipped out**: 152 million ounces
- **Net export**: 132 million ounces
- **Ratio**: For every ounce received, approximately 7.4 ounces are being shipped out

### Gold Movements at COMEX (Q1 2026)

- **Gold received**: 408,000 ounces
- **Gold shipped out**: 7 million ounces
- **Net export**: 6.6 million ounces
- **Ratio**: For every ounce received, 17 ounces are being shipped out

"The feed stock for all the silver being demanded is the LBMA through COMEX," Steer explained. "In gold, it's even more substantial. This is something I've been pointing out for the last couple of months, and nobody's picked up on it yet. But if you think the silver exports are incredible, the gold exports are even more incredible."

The destination of these metals remains somewhat opaque—the LBMA is known for deliberately vague reporting—but the ultimate consumers are becoming increasingly clear.

"This silver is disappearing mostly out of the LBMA and to a certain extent out of Switzerland," Steer noted. "And it's all going to the East—China, Turkey, and other places."

## Turkey's Silver Stack: A Nation Preparing?

While China dominates the headlines, Turkey's silver accumulation has been equally remarkable for a nation not typically considered a heavyweight in precious metals markets.

"In the first three months of 2026, they've imported 23.5 million ounces of silver year to date," Steer revealed. "Even if they didn't import another single solitary ounce the entire year, this is their largest silver import year in history by an absolute country mile."

February marked a record import month for Turkey, and March subsequently shattered that record. The question remains: what drives such aggressive accumulation?

Turkey's gold position presents a more complex picture. While the central bank liquidated approximately 58 to 60 tons of gold from its reserves in recent months, Steer cautions against reading this as a sign of weakening gold conviction.

"That was gold that the Turkey central bank owned," he explained. "Now they either sold it, swapped it, or leased it. They may still own it—they may end up paying the money back and getting the gold back. So to say they sold it is a bit of a misnomer. But everyone notes the fact that they disposed of it in one way or another, and there's a piece of paper written against it that says temporarily they don't own it. That's entirely different from their exports and imports, which are a different animal altogether."

## The Suppression Mechanism: Why Prices Don't Reflect Reality

Despite this extraordinary physical demand, gold and silver prices have remained remarkably subdued—particularly in the face of significant geopolitical upheaval. The Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 20-25% of the world's oil flows, has been closed, yet prices haven't exploded higher as historical precedent would suggest.

"If you look back at the day this war started, gold should have been screaming higher, and it did—but then the commercial traders came in and beat it down," Steer observed. "Every time gold has tried to rally over the last few weeks, they've been there to beat it into the dirt. Take a look at what happened on Tuesday—they came in and absolutely crushed it in the COMEX New York trading session."

The mechanism is well-understood among precious metals analysts: the large commercial shorts—primarily bullion banks—actively suppress prices to prevent the kind of run that would expose the true extent of physical scarcity.

"The bullion banks in general know that this brick wall is up there, and they're just keeping everything suppressed as long as they can until we finally see what they call 'the great reset,'" Steer noted.

The conventional wisdom that "war is bad news for gold" ignores a critical reality: the correlation between geopolitical risk and precious metal prices only holds when markets are allowed to trade freely.

"The only reason it's bad news for gold and silver is because the bullion banks are in there to make sure there's no correlation between the bad news and precious metal prices," Steer explained. "The demand for physical silver and gold on an industrial scale is still immense. They can beat it down all they want, but the amount of gold and silver that's been disappearing into the East is absolutely amazing."

## The Structural Deficit That Can't Be Hidden Forever

Silver has been in a structural supply deficit for six consecutive years, a fact that sophisticated buyers—particularly those with intelligence resources and long-term strategic planning—are well aware of.

"Everybody's got their intelligence resources out there—China, Turkey, the US, Canada—and everybody knows that we're in a structural deficit in silver going on into its sixth year," Steer said. "We should all be seeing a new price for silver by now. We'd be at $500, $600 an ounce right now if that wasn't the case. They can only beat this thing down so long until the structural deficit finally catches up with them."

China and Russia appear to be particularly attuned to this reality. The question is whether they're simply acquiring strategic reserves or positioning for a fundamental restructuring of the global monetary order.

"If China really wants to throw a spanner in the works, they could say, 'Look, if you're prepared to pay in gold, we'd be happy to accept that too,'" Steer speculated. "If they did that, it would change everything in a heartbeat. It would be a real spanner in the works for the paper currency system."

## Mining Sector Dynamics: Rising Costs, Still Healthy Margins

Higher oil prices—currently hovering around $90 per barrel—do increase input costs for mining operations. Diesel fuel, which Steer notes is currently over $2 per liter in the UK, directly impacts equipment operation, hauling, and general logistics.

"There's no question about it," Steer acknowledged. "It will increase the bill for that. However, last year at this time when diesel was less than half that price, silver was selling for $27 an ounce. Now it's selling for $78 an ounce. So yes, it does increase costs, but the profit margins the miners are making now more than make up for that."

With silver at $72-78 per ounce and gold around $4,600, the mining sector remains highly profitable despite rising energy costs—a testament to the underlying strength of physical demand.

- **Turkey imported 23.5 million ounces of silver in Q1 2026 alone**, already surpassing any full-year total in the nation's history by a significant margin.

- **Bullion bank suppression keeps prices artificially low**, but physical demand continues to overwhelm supply; at free market prices, silver would likely trade at multiples of current levels.
