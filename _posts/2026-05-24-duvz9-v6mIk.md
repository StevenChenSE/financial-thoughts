## Inside the Global Energy Crisis That Could Change Everything

The global oil market is at a critical inflection point. As of May 21st, 2026, crude oil has been hovering just south of $100 per barrel for approximately 60 days, but this stability is deceptive. According to energy expert Shubham Garg, who recently appeared on a major energy podcast, the price suppression is largely artificial—dependent on emergency releases from strategic reserves that cannot continue indefinitely.

"The US strategic petroleum reserve releases are keeping prices artificially low," Garg explained during the interview. "Twenty months worth of builds have been drained in just seven weeks. If it weren't for these SPR releases, oil prices would be substantially higher."

This observation cuts to the heart of a mounting crisis that most investors and consumers have completely overlooked. While headlines focus on ceasefire negotiations and geopolitical posturing, the underlying fundamentals of the oil market tell a far more alarming story—one that suggests the structural oil bull market is not just intact, but strengthening by the day.

## The World Runs on Energy: Understanding the Fundamental Demand Picture

One of the most striking revelations from the conversation centers on global energy consumption patterns. Garg shared fascinating data showing that the average US consumer has stalled out at approximately 22 barrels of oil consumption per year—a figure that remains remarkably stable regardless of electric vehicle adoption, hybrid growth, or electrification efforts.

"No matter how many EVs, hybrids, electrification, blah blah, whatever we do, that number stays consistent," Garg noted. "People just keep consuming more and they're willing to spend that same amount of dollars per year on fueling their lifestyle and convenience."

This persistence in demand has profound implications. Despite years of narrative around the "death of oil," the commodity remains the backbone of global economic activity. Coal, natural gas, solar, wind—all forms of energy matter, but crude oil specifically continues to drive the transportation, industrial, and chemical sectors that underpin modern civilization. The suggestion that oil could simply be replaced has not matched the reality of consumption data.

## Strategic Reserves: America's Depleted Safety Net

The discussion took a sharp turn toward the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve, which has emerged as a critical buffer that has been systematically depleted. Garg pointed out that over 200 million barrels have been drawn down since 2020, representing a significant portion of America's energy security cushion.

"Reality is it should never have been drained," Garg stated emphatically. "Yes, you need it in times of emergency and conflicts, but then you refill it at a pace that matters—not the piddly half a million barrels a week that was going on over the last few years."

The depletion extends beyond the US. Japanese crude inventories, global petroleum reserves, Chinese SPR stocks—all have been drawn down as the world burned through its energy insurance policies in 2022 during the Russia-Ukraine crisis. Garg described this period as one where "nobody really realized it" because the initial shock was absorbed by existing buffers.

"We blew through 200 million of SPR. The US drilled uncompleted count got absolutely blown down. Bakken, Eagleford, as well as the Permian—almost a year's worth of drilled uncompleted wells were completed in the last six years. That's gone," Garg warned. "So I think the oil bulls were sort of setting up for this next event to occur."

## The Permian Basin: Geology Limits Production Response

Central to the bull case is the reality that global spare production capacity has become extremely constrained. While some US shale activity has emerged—with companies like Diamondback adding a couple of rigs—Garg emphasized that the major producers are not responding aggressively.

"Nothing from the bigger boys," Garg said. "You're really starting to see things just sort of slow move towards the structural bull cycle."

The recent Devon Energy acquisition at over $300,000 per acre for untapped Permian acreage underscores this point. Even at $100 oil, the world's largest oil producers face geological constraints that prevent rapid production increases. The narrative that high prices automatically solve supply shortages through increased production has been shattered by physical reality.

"For the structural oil bull market to actually happen, the world needs to believe that the Permian cannot add a million barrels per day in that one-year time frame at $100 oil," Garg explained. "And to see that play out over time, to see some of the banks change their tune on things—that's what we're witnessing now."

## Geopolitical Dynamics: Two Egos and No Exit Strategy

The conversation addressed the elephant in the room—the ongoing conflict now at day 83-84—and the confused messaging around ceasefire negotiations. Garg offered a candid assessment of the underlying dynamics.

"Both sides don't have a clue here, you know, who actually is in the driver's seat. They both are in the driver's seat," Garg observed. "The Iranian regime's been out for 47 years, and Donald Trump's got the biggest ego ever. So you've got two parties here, none of whom want to lose."

This stalemate has significant implications for the oil market. Neither side appears willing or able to quickly resolve the situation, meaning supply disruptions continue day after day after day. The market has been rangebound between $95 and $110, but Garg believes the floor is rising.

"Glass ceiling is broken—that we can keep oil down forever no matter what happens in the world," Garg stated. "Oil's at $100, and it's been there for the last 60 days, and it's only going higher if this doesn't solve itself because we have a physical barrel supply loss day after day after day."

## Investment Implications: Where the Opportunities Lie

For investors, the current environment presents a paradox. While oil prices remain elevated, significant uncertainty creates mispriced securities. Garg described how many market participants "have no clue about oil price, supply demand," and "don't even know which companies to invest in."

"What you end up seeing is these skewed risk-reward scenarios that pop up in certain equity names, in certain leveraged equity names, in different kinds of options," Garg explained. "As this becomes more and more the norm, you're going to be able to identify certain dislocations in the market."

The historical pattern suggests that during these volatile periods, the major players move first, followed by small and midcap companies gaining traction. For those willing to do their homework, these dislocations can create substantial opportunities—as long as investors avoid the most speculative or "torqued up" names.

## What Comes Next: The Path Forward for Oil

As the conversation drew to a close, Garg articulated a clear vision of the road ahead. Even when a ceasefire agreement is eventually signed, supply will not immediately respond. Meanwhile, demand destruction at $100 oil remains limited compared to higher price scenarios.

"The difference between $65 and $80 is more or less meaningless to these governments and consumers," Garg noted. "The difference between $80 and $150 is unbearable—not just for the personal consumer, but because people start rioting. You have not just energy shortage but food shortage, and that's the last thing governments want."

This price floor effectively insulates oil producers from major downside while preserving significant upside optionality. The summer driving season approaches, followed by the US midterm elections—periods historically associated with increased demand and potential policy interventions.

"We said this last time. We said this before in 2016 to 2020. Trump is a really good job owner. He is absolutely incredibly effective at using whatever tools—whether it's Twitter, but then the paper markets behind the Twitter—to sort of keep that oil price within a reasonable range," Garg acknowledged. "But being relatively effective means oil stays in a profitable zone for producers."

The bottom line is stark: oil at $100 is not cheap oil. It is the result of emergency measures, depleted buffers, and constrained supply responding to persistent global demand. The structural bull case for oil has never been stronger, and as these buffers continue to erode, the world may soon discover just how exposed it has become.
