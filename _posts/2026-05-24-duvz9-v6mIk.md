# Why Oil at $100 Is the New Floor, Not the Ceiling

## The Market That Refuses to Crash Despite Everything

For the past 60 days, oil has traded stubbornly close to $100—sitting just south of that threshold, occasionally poking above it, and then retreating again when ceasefire headlines hit the wires. It's a pattern that's repeated itself dozens of times since the conflict began 83 days ago. But according to oil expert Shubham Garg, there's a deeper story beneath the headlines that most investors are completely missing.

"The world runs on energy, no matter how you want to slice and dice it," Garg said during a recent episode of the Capital A podcast. "The idea that we could just keep oil down forever, no matter what happens in the world—that glass ceiling has been broken."

The reality is that oil at $100 isn't the result of market strength. It's the result of extraordinary intervention. Without the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve releases and other strategic inventory draws, prices would be significantly higher. But here's the thing: those buffers are nearly exhausted.

## The Hidden Inflation Behind Oil Prices

The US has drained over 200 million barrels from its strategic reserves since 2020, with 20 months' worth of builds burned through in just seven weeks during this current conflict period. But it's not just the American SPR—global crude inventories, Japanese crude stocks, Chinese strategic reserves, and product inventories everywhere have been drawn down significantly.

"We blew through 200 million barrels of SPR," Garg explained. "The US drilled uncompleted count got absolutely blown down. The Permian and Eagle Ford lost almost a year's worth of drilled uncompleted wells—that was your spare buffer, and that's gone."

Add to this the fact that higher interest rates have discouraged gasoline storage at pumps. Operators simply don't want to pay carrying costs on inventory when borrowing money is expensive. The result is a system with almost no cushion left to absorb the next shock.

## The Demand Story Nobody Wants to Hear

Here's the uncomfortable truth that contradicts almost every clean energy narrative: the average US consumer has stalled at about 22 barrels per year of oil consumption, and that number isn't going down. Despite EVs, hybrids, electrification efforts, and everything else—people keep consuming energy at roughly the same rate because they're willing to spend whatever it takes to fuel their lifestyles and conveniences.

"You can have all the EVs and renewables you want, but consumption stays flat," Garg noted. "People want to consume that energy, and that's why people make money in this space."

This is the fundamental insight that structural oil bulls have been making for years. Demand destruction at $100 oil is minimal compared to what happens at $140 or $150. The world adapts to $100. It struggles at $150 and panics at $200.

## Why the Structural Bull Market Is Finally Here

The world needed to believe that OPEC couldn't add a million barrels per day within a one-year timeframe at $100 oil. That belief is now starting to crystallize in the market. You see it in the Permian acreage deals—Devon recently paid over $300,000 per acre for undeveloped Permian acreage. Diamondback added a few rigs, but not the 20% production growth surge that the bears keep waiting for.

"The geology simply isn't there," Garg said. "We're seeing things just sort of slow move towards the structural bull cycle."

The key institutional players are starting to change their tune. The major banks are revising their outlooks, and capital is flowing back into oil equities. For investors who understand the sector, this creates skewed risk-reward scenarios in certain equity names and leveraged positions that the broader market hasn't yet priced in.

## The Geopolitical Trap

Neither side in the current conflict wants to lose face. The Iranian regime has been in power for 47 years, and Donald Trump has what Garg calls "the biggest ego ever." Two parties with enormous reputational stakes, neither willing to blink first. This deadlock keeps the market on edge as we move into the summer driving season and the US midterm election cycle.

"A ceasefire could send prices to $85 or $80," Garg noted. "But you'll see incremental buyers step in—Singapore, Japan, South Korea, the US SPR itself—to take over that barrel at those prices. The difference between $65 and $80 is meaningless to governments and consumers. The difference between $80 and $150 is unbearable."

That floor is real. And as inventories continue draining—over a billion barrels already lost—the structural case for higher prices remains firmly intact.

## The Bigger Picture

Even AI companies are now buying natural gas producers, recognizing that the world runs on energy. This isn't just about oil anymore—it's about the recognition that hard assets, physical commodities, and energy infrastructure are the foundation everything else runs on.

For investors, the message is clear: the buffers are gone, demand is resilient, and supply constraints are real. The oil market has been rangebound at elevated levels not because everything is fine, but because governments are using every tool at their disposal to keep it that way. When those tools run out—and they're running out fast—the market will find its true level. And that level, according to the structural case, is significantly higher than where we are today.
