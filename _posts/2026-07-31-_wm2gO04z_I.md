## Executive Summary
Mike McGlone, Senior Commodities Strategist at Bloomberg Intelligence, argues that markets are due for a significant deflationary reset. He predicts that rising Treasury yields and a potential stock market correction will trigger a cascade across asset classes, potentially driving crude oil to $40, gold to $3,000, and Bitcoin to $10,000.

## The Bond Market and Fed Policy
McGlone begins by analyzing the Federal Reserve’s recent stance, noting that Chair Jerome Powell’s comment that bond markets have effectively raised rates removes the urgency for the Fed to act. With long-end Treasury yields hitting 5.2%, the highest since 2007, bonds are exerting immense pressure on non-income-producing assets. McGlone views Treasuries as a "put on the stock market with positive return," suggesting that the bond market is pricing in an inflation problem linked to soaring equity valuations. He argues that the stock market-to-public-debt ratio is at a 19-year high, creating an extreme environment ripe for reversal.

## Metals as Stock Market "Sock Puppets"
A central theme of McGlone’s analysis is the unprecedented correlation between metals and equities. He describes gold, copper, and other commodities as "sock puppets" of the S&P 500. Currently, the 60-day correlation between the Bloomberg All Metals Index and the S&P 500 is at a historical high. McGlone warns that metals have only one path to outperform: a declining stock market. He notes that gold’s volatility is twice that of the S&P 500, the highest in two decades, and its valuation relative to Treasuries is at a 50-year extreme. Consequently, he anticipates a "trade of a lifetime" if the stock market corrects, which would likely drag metals down with it.

## Copper and the Recession Signal
Copper, often seen as a barometer for industrial health, is closely tracking the NASDAQ and S&P 500. Despite supply constraints in Chile, McGlone argues that copper is fundamentally tied to global equity performance. He points out that hedge funds are heavily long copper, making the asset vulnerable to stop-loss cascades if equities drop. A 10% drop in the S&P 500 could lead to a 10-20% decline in copper. McGlone suggests that a breakdown in copper would signal a broader global recession, reinforcing his bearish outlook on industrial commodities.

## The Case for Lower Oil Prices
McGlone remains bearish on crude oil, predicting a reversion to its long-term mean of $70 or lower, with potential lows around $40. He attributes this to a paradigm shift in global energy dynamics: the US has become a net exporter, reducing OPEC’s influence. Furthermore, the rise of electric vehicles (EVs) in China and elsewhere is decreasing long-term demand for fossil fuels. McGlone emphasizes that supply shocks, such as those in the Middle East, are temporary and that sustained high prices would only accelerate the transition to alternative energy sources, ultimately crushing oil prices.

## Bitcoin’s Path to $10,000
McGlone is sharply bearish on Bitcoin, predicting a drop to $10,000. He argues that Bitcoin has "flunked the test" by failing to hold value as other assets rose, behaving instead as high-beta speculative asset. With the launch of ETFs and political endorsements, McGlone believes the "easy money" trade is over. He views Bitcoin as oversupplied in a crowded crypto market and expects it to suffer significantly if the stock market corrects. He draws parallels to previous bear markets, suggesting that a 90%+ correction is standard for such speculative assets once the narrative shifts.

## Conclusion
Mike McGlone’s outlook hinges on the interplay between bond yields, equity markets, and commodities. He anticipates that the current extremes in valuations, particularly in stocks and precious metals relative to bonds, are unsustainable. The catalyst for his predicted deflationary trade is a stock market correction, which would expose the fragility of non-income-producing assets and drive prices down across gold, oil, and Bitcoin. Investors are advised to watch the bond market and equities closely, as they are the primary drivers of the upcoming macroeconomic shift.
