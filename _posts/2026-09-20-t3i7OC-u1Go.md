## Introduction

The Federal Reserve just executed a 25-basis point rate hike, but the more significant story is a philosophical shift in Washington. John Hopkins University economist Professor Steve Hanke, who recently predicted this exact move, joins the discussion to break down the implications of new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's leadership. Hanke argues that the Fed is no longer just adjusting interest rates; it is actively practicing "monetary supervisionism," a framework that prioritizes controlling the money supply over managing the cost of borrowing. This approach has profound implications for the global economy, the US debt ceiling, and the future of the labor market.

## The Rise of Monetary Supervisionism

Hanke identifies a distinct shift in the Federal Reserve's operational philosophy under the leadership of Kevin Warsh. Unlike his predecessor Jerome Powell, who explicitly rejected "monetarism," Warsh is embracing a framework that places the money supply at the center of monetary policy. Hanke points to Warsh's recent speeches, particularly his remarks at the Jackson Hole symposium, where he emphasized that "money matters."

The core tenets of this new doctrine, as outlined by Hanke, include:

*   **Monitoring Money Aggregates:** The Fed is now actively tracking money supply metrics like M2 and M4.
*   **De-emphasizing Interest Rates:** Warsh has suggested that interest rate changes are less important than the volume of money in the economy.
*   **Rules and Discipline:** A move away from forward guidance toward a more rigid, rule-based framework for monetary policy.

Hanke notes that Warsh is essentially acting as a "monetary supervisor," viewing the Fed's role not as a lender of last resort or an interest rate manager, but as a regulator of the monetary base.

## The "Red Zone" and the Path to 6%

A central question in the current debate is how much higher rates need to go before the economy "blows up." Hanke rejects the idea of a single "tipping point" for the federal funds rate, arguing that the relationship between interest rates and the money supply is loose. Instead, he focuses on the growth rate of the money supply.

According to Hanke, the "golden growth rate" for the money supply is approximately 6% per year, which aligns with the Fed's 2% inflation target. Currently, M4 is growing at a rate of 7.9%, significantly above this threshold. Hanke predicts that the Fed will continue to raise the federal funds rate until money supply growth slows to that 6% level.

When asked about the timeline, Hanke points to objective data from the Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME) futures market rather than prediction markets. He estimates an 80% to 90% probability of at least one more rate hike by December, with a high likelihood of three additional hikes in this cycle. He argues that because interest rates are a blunt instrument for controlling money supply, the Fed will likely over-tighten to achieve the desired monetary contraction.

## The Debt Spiral and the "Vicious Cycle"

Hanke addresses the concern that rising interest rates will lead to an unmanageable debt crisis. He acknowledges that the US Treasury's interest payments have already reached $1 trillion annually. However, he dismisses the idea that the government will cut spending to address this, noting that discretionary spending is already shrinking.

Instead, Hanke describes a "vicious cycle": as the deficit grows, the government must issue more debt. To sell this debt, they must offer higher yields, which further increases interest costs. He argues that the real drivers of inflation are not just monetary but also related to war and energy prices, which are "relative price changes" rather than true inflation.

## Labor Market and the Wealth Effect

Despite the aggressive monetary tightening, Hanke notes that the US labor market remains resilient, though he believes the data is somewhat misleading. He points out that the low unemployment rate is partly due to a decline in labor force participation, as many workers have left the market.

Hanke explains the current economic strength through the "wealth effect." The top 10% of income earners account for 50% of US consumption. Because this demographic holds the majority of the stock market, their consumption is sustained by rising asset values. This dynamic allows the economy to remain strong even as monetary policy tightens, as the "bath" of money remains full despite the "faucet" being turned down.

## The EU's "Lazy Savings" Debate

The conversation also touches on a controversial statement by European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, who described household savings as "lazy." Hanke strongly disagrees with this characterization, arguing that savings are the fuel for bank lending and monetary expansion. He suggests that the EU's real problem is not a lack of savings, but a weak and fragmented capital market that cannot efficiently channel funds to businesses.

## Conclusion

Steve Hanke’s analysis suggests that the US is entering a new era of monetary policy where the focus is squarely on controlling the money supply. While this may eventually bring inflation under control, it comes with significant risks, including a potential debt spiral and a sharper economic contraction than the market currently anticipates. For investors and policymakers, the key takeaway is that the Fed is no longer just reacting to interest rates; it is actively managing the volume of money in the economy, and the consequences of that management will be felt for years to come.
