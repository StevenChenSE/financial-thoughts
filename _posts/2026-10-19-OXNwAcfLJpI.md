## Introduction
While Wall Street often dominates economic headlines, small businesses remain the backbone of the US economy, accounting for roughly 44% of private sector GDP and employing over 62 million Americans. As we look toward 2027, a critical question emerges for workers: will they see pay raises or face layoffs? Holly Wade, Executive Director of Research at the National Federation of Independent Business (NFIB), provides insight into the current sentiment among small business owners, revealing a complex landscape defined by inflation pressures, labor shortages, and rising uncertainty.

## The NFIB Optimism Index: A Leading Indicator
The NFIB tracks small business sentiment through its widely followed Optimism Index, which has been monitored for over 50 years. This index is composed of ten components, most of which are forward-looking indicators regarding hiring, capital spending, inventory plans, and sales expectations.

In September, the index fell by 0.8 points, landing just under the 52-year average of 98. While this deterioration was noted, Wade emphasized that the overall health of the small business economy remains near historical averages. The primary declines were driven by worsening outlooks on business conditions and expansion opportunities.

Although the NFIB Optimism Index often correlates with stock market performance, Wade cautioned that "Main Street" conditions can diverge significantly from Wall Street. Small business owners face unique challenges compared to large corporations, particularly regarding the massive investments flowing into tech giants versus the resource constraints faced by local businesses.

## The Labor Market Paradox: Tightness and Shortages
A central theme in the current economic landscape is the persistent tightness of the labor market. A quarter of small business owners cite labor availability as their single biggest challenge, while 20% point to inflation. Despite a "low hire, low fire" environment, many businesses are struggling not because they lack demand, but because they cannot find qualified applicants.

Key findings from the September survey include:
*   **51%** of small business owners reported hiring or trying to hire in September, down five points from August.
*   Among those hiring, **87%** reported few or no qualified applicants.
*   **20%** of all owners reported receiving zero qualified applicants for their open positions.

These shortages are most acute in specific industries such as construction, manufacturing, and specialized services. For instance, there is a severe shortage of skilled tradespeople, including plumbers, electricians, HVAC technicians, and auto repair specialists with transmission expertise. This scarcity has been a growing trend since the recovery period around 2015-2016, exacerbated recently by competition from large-scale data center construction projects funded by companies like Meta and Google, which offer higher wages and better resources than small local firms can match.

## Wage Dynamics: Forced Increases Over Planned Raises
For employees wondering about their paychecks in the coming years, the data suggests that wage growth is likely to continue, though often out of necessity rather than strategic planning. Wade highlighted a significant gap between businesses *planning* to increase compensation and those *having* to do so.

Far more owners are raising wages to retain talent and recruit new staff than originally intended. This reactive approach puts pressure on bottom lines, as small businesses absorb higher labor costs alongside rising input prices. Consequently, the most wage growth is expected in sectors where retention is critical: services, manufacturing, and construction. Businesses are also leveraging bonus structures as additional incentives, but base wages remain the primary tool for recruitment and retention.

## Inflation, Tariffs, and Cost Absorption
Small businesses are grappling with multiple layers of cost inflation, particularly from energy sources like gasoline, diesel, and fuel oil. While government CPI data may not yet fully reflect these costs in consumer goods, small business owners report that they are absorbing these expenses internally before passing them on to customers.

This process is fraught with difficulty for small retailers competing against large-box stores like Walmart or Target, which have greater pricing power and supply chain leverage. Small businesses often lack the diverse inventory needed to spread out cost increases, forcing them to make difficult decisions about how much and how often to raise prices without alienating their customer base.

Tariffs have further complicated this picture. Initially expected to be a one-time price shock, tariffs have resulted in an "evolving, rolling deployment" of higher costs. Manufacturers and retailers have been hit hardest, with many reporting that it took a year to restore profit margins after adjusting for supplier price increases. The uncertainty surrounding future tariff levels has led many owners to hold off on price adjustments, further squeezing their earnings.

## AI Adoption: Augmentation, Not Replacement
Contrary to fears of widespread job displacement, the NFIB data suggests that artificial intelligence is being adopted by small businesses primarily as an augmentation tool rather than a replacement for human workers. Most owners do not plan to replace their current workforce with AI. Instead, they are using AI to fill gaps created by unfilled job openings and to streamline back-office tasks such as marketing, financials, and correspondence.

Adoption is slower among established businesses due to existing operational processes, whereas newer companies entering the market are more likely to integrate AI into their foundational business plans. The goal for most small business owners is to use technology to increase productivity and compete with larger counterparts, rather than to eliminate jobs.

## Uncertainty and Future Outlook
The NFIB also tracks an Uncertainty Index, which measures how confident owners are in making decisions about hiring, capital investment, and inventory. Interestingly, uncertainty does not necessarily spike during recessions (such as 2008 or the pandemic) but rises during periods of policy flux and unpredictable economic conditions.

Currently, high levels of uncertainty are causing business owners to adopt a "wait and see" approach. Many are delaying hiring decisions or pausing capital expenditures until they have greater clarity on tax policies, regulatory changes, and election outcomes. The upcoming midterm elections are particularly significant for small businesses, as changes in state and federal taxes could directly impact their ability to retain profits and invest in growth.

A notable warning sign appeared in September when self-assessments of business health shifted from "good" to "fair." While one month does not constitute a trend, it signals potential deterioration that economists will be watching closely.

## Conclusion
The path for small businesses leading into 2027 is defined by resilience amid pressure. Owners are navigating a tight labor market where they must raise wages to survive, absorb rising energy and tariff costs, and adapt to new technologies like AI without displacing their workforce. For workers, the outlook suggests continued wage growth in skilled trades and service sectors, driven by fierce competition for talent. However, the high levels of uncertainty regarding policy and inflation mean that businesses are proceeding cautiously, balancing the need to expand with the imperative to protect their bottom lines.
