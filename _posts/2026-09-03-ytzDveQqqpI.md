## Escalating Geopolitical Tensions and Supply Risks

On September 1, 2025, WTI crude oil prices surged to $92 per barrel, marking a 13% increase since August 27 and a 30% rise from July lows. This sharp escalation is driven by renewed strikes on Iran and a significant disruption in the Strait of Hormuz. On August 31, two oil tankers—one Saudi and one South Korean—were hit by projectiles in the strait within minutes of each other. Although no casualties or spills were reported, US Central Command launched retaliatory strikes on Iran’s Revolutionary Guard, citing attempts to lay sea mines in the waterway. President Trump warned that any further retaliation from Iran would result in severe consequences for the Islamic Republic, while Iran vowed to retaliate against the United States.

## The EIA Data Quality Crisis

A critical but underreported factor contributing to the price surge is a data integrity failure at the US Energy Information Administration (EIA). The agency failed to release its June oil report, citing an inability to reconcile front-facing systems with back-office data. Josh Young, CIO of Bison Interest, notes that this is a significant red flag. For a public company, such a reconciliation error would likely trigger an SEC investigation and a 50% stock drop. Young points to historical data quality issues, including an 80% revision bias in EIA weekly demand estimates, suggesting that the agency may have been over-reporting oil inventories. This lack of transparency has heightened market anxiety, drawing parallels to the silver market crash in late 2024, where data issues preceded a massive price spike.

## Strategic Shifts in Iranian Attacks

While US strikes on Iranian targets like Laric Island and Sirik have been ongoing, Young argues that these attacks are largely tactical and immaterial to the oil price. The primary driver of the recent surge is a change in the severity of Iranian and Houthi attacks. Previously, the IRGC relied on drones that caused limited damage, allowing shipping companies to accept the risk. However, recent reports indicate the use of larger missiles that have severely damaged at least two tankers. This increased lethality makes it impossible for insurers and shipping companies to "close their eyes" to the risk, further restricting transit through the Strait of Hormuz.

## The "Stock vs. Flow" Imbalance

The global oil market is currently facing a "stock vs. flow" problem. In February, 20 million barrels per day transited the Strait of Hormuz; today, that number has dropped to an estimated 5 to 10 million barrels per day. This 5 to 10 million barrel daily undersupply is being absorbed by depleting global inventories, including commercial storage and the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. Young notes that while inventories are not yet empty, they are significantly lower than previous levels. The market is currently "spending more than it is making," and as these stocks deplete, prices are expected to rise until demand destruction occurs.

## Refining Margins and Consumer Impact

The current market environment is characterized by extreme refining margins. Diesel prices are approaching $200 per barrel at the wholesale level, while jet fuel is also racing higher. Young suggests that as the US enters shoulder season, refineries may increase utilization from 85% to 95% to meet demand, potentially shifting margins from refined products back to crude oil. This dynamic could allow WTI to reach $110 or even $120 without a corresponding spike in gasoline prices at the pump. However, the high cost of diesel is already forcing inflation into the goods economy, data centers, and the AI value chain. Additionally, European natural gas prices are soaring, creating a broader energy crisis as Europe cannot rely on diesel for power generation due to shortages.

## The Venezuela Deal and Future Outlook

The White House announced a deal giving the US a 35% stake in 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan reserves. However, Young views this as a "useful nonsensical talking point" rather than a near-term supply solution. With only two active Western drilling rigs in Venezuela and a 5 to 10-year timeline to potentially add 1 million barrels per day, this deal is unlikely to impact prices in the short term. Young remains bullish on oil, citing low rig activity in the Middle East and the risk of simultaneous inventory depletion and production constraints. He predicts that the market may see a significant price move even after a potential de-escalation, similar to the 1973 oil shock, where prices surged after the embargo was lifted.

## Summary

The oil market is facing a perfect storm of geopolitical escalation, data integrity failures, and inventory depletion. While US strikes on Iran have been ongoing, the recent shift to more severe attacks by the IRGC and the EIA's inability to reconcile its data have driven prices to $92. With global stocks being depleted to cover a 5 to 10 million barrel daily shortfall, Young warns of "much, much higher" prices, potentially reaching $115 to $120 by year-end, as the market struggles to balance supply constraints with rising demand from Asian refiners and a lack of new drilling activity.
