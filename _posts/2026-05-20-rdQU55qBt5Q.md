## The Summit That Wasn't: How Beijing Snubbed Washington

The highly anticipated China-US summit has concluded with little to show for it—and markets are responding accordingly. Simon Hunt, a seasoned geopolitical and financial analyst, sat down with Danny of Capital Cosm on May 15th, 2026, to dissect the fallout from what many are calling a diplomatic disappointment.

The signs of trouble were visible from the moment Air Force One touched down in Beijing. Trump was not greeted by President Xi at the airport—a stark departure from diplomatic protocol. Instead, an underling lowered the steps to meet him. The cavalcade into the city flew no American flags, and the state-run China Daily relegated Trump's arrival to a sidebar on its front page, with the main story featuring a meeting between Xi and the president of Tajikistan.

"This was a non-event," Hunt explained. "Friends of mine who were in Shanghai, talking to industrial people, they were just shaking their heads."

## A Meeting Without Preparation

According to Hunt, Washington failed to prepare for the summit in any meaningful way. Unlike typical diplomatic summits—where advance teams (known as "sherpas") work behind the scenes to negotiate agreements beforehand—Trump opted to "wing it" in characteristic fashion.

The results reflected this lack of preparation. Xi made it abundantly clear that Taiwan remains the defining issue in US-China relations. Any continued American support for Taiwanese independence would render the bilateral relationship "fraught with difficulty." The problem, Hunt noted, is that Trump faces an immovable obstacle: Congress won't go along with any shift on the Taiwan issue.

## The Delegation of Deal-Makers

Trump arrived in Beijing accompanied by 20 CEOs whose collective equity valuation reached $16.5 trillion—approaching China's entire GDP of $20 trillion from the previous year. It was an attempt at economic theater, suggesting American leverage.

Yet Xi held stronger cards than the optics suggested. When examining the world's 10 largest banks, four are Chinese while only two are American. Throughout the summit, Hunt observed that Trump appeared eager to ingratiate himself with Xi—an approach that telegraphed American dependence rather than strength.

"The tone was set: 'I need you rather than you need me,'" Hunt remarked.

## What Was Actually Achieved

Hunt's assessment of tangible outcomes was blunt: "Very little indeed."

Xi made vague statements that Trump interpreted as commitments on Iran. The Chinese foreign ministry quickly denied any specific promises were made. Meanwhile, China, Russia, and Iran had already agreed to establish a new regional architecture in the Gulf that explicitly excludes American involvement—a direct challenge to US hegemony in the Middle East.

The most Hunt can offer is a "3-year window of potential detente"—but only if both nations play their cards with extraordinary care. Given the structural tensions over Taiwan, that seems unlikely.

## Iran: The Central Battleground

In Hunt's analysis, Iran represents the pivotal country in the entire global picture. The stakes are immense:

- **For Washington:** Defeating Iran would deliver complete control over Gulf energy resources and effectively dismantle the BRICS grouping, since Iran serves as a crucial logistical hub
- **For Israel:** Eliminating Iranian influence has been a strategic objective for years and would clear the path for "greater Israel"
- **For China and Russia:** Iran is essential not just for BRICS cohesion but for their broader strategic expansion into the Global South

Hunt's prognosis is sobering: "My guess—and it's nothing more than that—is that the Straits of Hormuz will be closed for several more months. And there is a big risk now that Trump is back from China of a second, but massive, air attack on Iran. And Iran, as they have repeatedly said, will retaliate."

## The Oil Crisis: Physical vs. Futures Reality

Oil prices are surging, but Hunt urges caution about interpreting futures prices alone. "The futures prices are easily manipulated," he warned. "If you look at physical prices in Asia, you're talking about $200 [per barrel]."

The Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) tells a troubling story. Last week saw the largest weekly drawdown since October 2022—7.12 million barrels. This marks the fifth consecutive weekly decline, the longest streak since 2023. The reserve has plummeted from a high of 416 million barrels to 392 million barrels, representing a 24-million-barrel drop over recent months.

"We're going to be in an extraordinarily volatile period going into 2028," Hunt cautioned. "How that period evolves will depend on how long the Straits of Hormuz remain closed."

### Recession or Stagflation: The Fork in the Road

Hunt laid out two scenarios based on Hormuz developments:

- **If the Straits remain closed through September:** Expect a full recession, potentially arriving by June when physical shortages begin working through global supply chains
- **If the Straits reopen in July:** Massive monetary and fiscal stimulus will push the economy into stagflation, with inflation outpacing GDP growth into 2028

Either path leads to turbulence. "By the end of this month, all the surpluses of oil around the world will be close to being exhausted," Hunt stated. "And when we get into June, we will see the physical shortages working their way through the whole global manufacturing and supply chains."

## The Coming Food Crisis

Perhaps the most alarming dimension of the Hormuz closure involves food security. Approximately 20% of the world's fertilizers flow through the Straits; for certain fertilizer products, the figure reaches 40-45%.

India stands to be hit hardest:

- Rice production peaked at 150 million tons last year—production will decline significantly
- India will likely ban rice exports (which totaled 27 million tons last year)
- Shortages will cascade throughout Asia and beyond

Hunt's analysis of the FAO Food Price Index reveals patterns from the 1970s showing trough-to-peak increases of 38%. "That's the minimum that we can see over the next 2 years," he predicted. "And that's an overall index globally."

### Compounding Factors

The fertilizer crisis arrives at an especially dangerous moment:

- A "super El Niño" weather pattern carries a 61% probability, threatening drought conditions
- Climate projections suggest a potential repeat of the 1930s Dust Bowl era by 2027, driven by the Gleissberg cycle
- "All these things are coming together," Hunt emphasized. "Not just war cycles, geopolitical cycles, but food cycles."

## The Bond Vigilantes Are Watching

With the 10-year Treasury yield breaking above 4.57%—levels not seen in nearly a year—Hunt sees bond markets sending a clear message. Japan is experiencing similar pressure: the 10-year yield sits at 2.7%, while the 30-year has crossed 4%, up 11 basis points in 24 hours.

"What's interesting is that the world loves artificial intelligence and data centers," Hunt noted, explaining why capital had been flowing into US stocks despite the deteriorating backdrop. "It's a money game. And I think that money game will come to an end by the end of this year."

But Hunt's larger concern involves inflation trajectory. His work suggests that by the end of 2028, global inflation could reach levels comparable to 1980's peak of 12-13%. The implications for bond yields are staggering: in 1981, the 10-year Treasury hit nearly 16%.

"No amount of fiscal stimulus and monetary stimulus will stop a big crash," Hunt declared. "The bond vigilantes want a real return on their money."

## A 15-20% Correction—and Possibly More

Hunt anticipates an average market correction of 15-20% across the board, globally. However, he acknowledges the possibility of something larger. Market liquidity analyst Christopher Hull is calling for a top in global liquidity—a concerning signal.

"I don't want to say something you don't want to hear," Danny pressed.

"I know what's going to happen in 2028, which is a big crash," Hunt replied. "Can easily be bigger."

His personal positioning? Cash. "I'm in cash anyway."
