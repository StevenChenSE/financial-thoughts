## Martin Armstrong Reveals Critical Geopolitical Factors Driving Precious Metals

Gold dipped below $4,000 an ounce yet again on June 30th, the last day of the first half of the year, touching a low of $3,945 before recovering to trade around $4,013. This price action has investors asking critical questions: Is this a trend change? Is this the bottom? Could we go lower? And most importantly, when will gold rally again?

Martin Armstrong, founder of Armstrong Economics and advisor to central banks worldwide, joined Soar Financially to provide his analysis on the precious metals complex and the broader geopolitical landscape shaping market dynamics.

## Understanding the Gold Price Decline

According to Armstrong, the recent weakness in gold stems primarily from the perception that the Middle East conflict would not escalate as severely as initially feared. "People were selling gold largely because the banking system was down in Russia because of the sanctions," Armstrong explained. "They came to the conclusion that Iran would lose. This isn't going to be a big war. And so they really kind of took the shine off of gold in particular."

However, Armstrong's computer models tell a different story. His analysis suggests gold is making a low, with major support down to $3,500—whether that level will be reached remains debatable. The critical timeframe to watch is the week of July 6th, when geopolitical tensions are expected to intensify.

## The Geopolitical Premium in Gold

Armstrong emphasized that gold's primary driver is geopolitical risk, not inflation. Historical precedent supports this view. "If you look at even the charts for gold going into 1980, it reached $479 in December of '79 and then Russia invaded Afghanistan," Armstrong noted. "You can see from December to January 21st, in the last few weeks, gold doubled. It was all geopolitics. It wasn't inflation or anything else."

This matters because gold functions as a neutral entity—investors are not betting on who wins a war. As Armstrong explained: "In time of war, it's always a coin flip sometimes. Who wins? And if you lose, your currency is worth nothing." After World War I, all of Europe defaulted, with Britain even suspending interest payments in 1931. This historical context demonstrates why gold serves as a hedge against currency debasement during geopolitical instability.

## Central Bank Buying: A Different Dynamic

Unlike retail investors, central banks operate on entirely different principles when acquiring gold. "You can't look at them like an investor," Armstrong stated. "They're not buying it, oh it's going to go up $30. That's not the issue. If it goes down $30, they're not selling either. It's more of a neutral position in their portfolio."

China has been particularly aggressive in acquiring gold, not merely as a dedollarization strategy but as genuine diversification away from volatile assets. "Europe goes into war, you don't really want to own anything," Armstrong observed. China's gold accumulation represents a fundamental shift in reserve management, reflecting growing concerns about geopolitical fragmentation.

## Iran: The Overlooked Wild Card

Dubai emerged as the financial capital of the Middle East after Switzerland "basically shot itself in the foot" by confiscating Russian assets. Iran recognized this concentration and attacked Dubai more aggressively than Israel, particularly targeting a $30 billion AI complex housing Amazon, OpenAI, and other major tech operations.

Armstrong highlighted Iran's strategic sophistication: "They are one of the most intelligent or educated in the Middle East. Attacking the Gulf States was more than just simply American bases." The strikes temporarily disabled banking systems and critical infrastructure, demonstrating Tehran's capability to disrupt global commerce through the Strait of Hormuz.

Perhaps most significantly, Iran has decentralized its government into four tiers specifically to counter Netanyahu's assassination strategy. "If you take out the leadership, the rest still have their orders and they can be autonomous," Armstrong explained. This model has been so effective that Taiwan has adopted it for its own defense planning.

## Europe's Financial Crisis and the Push for War

Armstrong delivered a stark assessment of Europe's economic fundamentals: "Europe is in financial trouble. This is the real problem." Italy's GDP growth sits at just 0.5% compared to the United States, while Germany has enacted rules preventing draft-age citizens from leaving without government permission—"gearing up for war at this stage in the game."

The structural flaws in the euro remain unresolved. Armstrong, who helped create the euro, noted that European debt cannot be centralized the way U.S. Treasury debt can be managed. "I can pick up the phone and say buy me $10 billion worth of US treasuries. No problem. With Europe, I can't—there is no centralized debt. I still have to make the same decision today as if the euro didn't exist."

Italy has already approached Brussels to issue debt because it cannot sell its own bonds—a warning sign of potential contagion. "A government collapses when you're in these Ponzi schemes, when you can't sell the neutrons to pay off the old," Armstrong warned.

The conflict of interest between Trump's desire for a pre-election victory and Netanyahu's need for some form of victory complicates negotiations. Trump cannot deliver Netanyahu, and Iran cannot surrender without risking civil uprising. "Both everybody's got self-interest here," Armstrong observed. "This is just the way politics works."

## Inflation: Beyond the Headlines

The disruptions from Middle East tensions extend far beyond asset prices. Armstrong detailed cascading effects: "Trade of Hormuz cut off basically fertilizer. They couldn't even get gasoline to put them in a motorbike. The diesel fuel was non-existent. So the ships couldn't even go out to fish."

This creates cost-push inflation reminiscent of the 1970s. Combined with rising government costs at federal, state, and local levels, consumers face sustained price pressures across food and energy categories. "You're looking at inflation coming across the board from rising cost to government," Armstrong explained. "And you're also looking at food prices going up."

## What Lies Ahead

Armstrong expects significant escalation from the first week of July through August, with geopolitical problems extending into the first quarter of 2027. The NATO meeting scheduled during this period will be closely watched for signals about alliance cohesion and potential flashpoints.

Central bank gold buying will likely resume as tensions escalate, providing fundamental support even as speculative selling pressures prices. Armstrong's new 600+ page book, to be released following a July 25th conference in Tampa, Florida, addresses how traditional economic theories—from Keynesian to Marxist—fail to account for currency dynamics, imported inflation, and capital flows in a interconnected world.

The bottom line: while gold has temporarily shed its geopolitical premium, the structural factors driving central bank accumulation and the escalating tensions in multiple theaters suggest the next rally is not a matter of if, but when. The week of July 6th may prove pivotal for markets across precious metals, currencies, and broader financial assets.
