**March 17** – On the surface, today’s market action looks familiar: WTI crude trades near $95, Brent holds above $103, and the S&P attempts a tentative bounce. But this is no ordinary session. It’s the latest chapter in a three-week financial shockwave that began when U.S. and Israeli strikes targeted Iran, culminating in Tehran’s closure of the Strait of Hormuz.

The immediate aftermath was brutal. WTI crude spiked from $77 to $119 per barrel. The Dow plunged 900 points. Asian markets fell 7-12% in a single week. In response, the International Energy Agency (IEA) announced a 400-million-barrel release from global strategic reserves, and sanctions on Russian oil imports were lifted.

Yet, weeks into the crisis, a fundamental disagreement has emerged among top analysts: Is this the beginning of a historic oil price surge, or a fear-driven spike destined to collapse?

## The Bull Case: A Path to $200 Oil

For Josh Young, CIO of Bison Interests, the math is clear. “If this does not resolve soon, we should see all-time high oil prices,” he states, noting that the 2008 peak of $147, when adjusted for inflation, translates to “$200 plus.”

The mechanics, he argues, justify the extreme figure. The Strait of Hormuz carries roughly one-fifth of the world’s crude supply. Iran’s deployment of small boats, sea mines, and anti-ship missiles has effectively blocked the passage for most commercial vessels.

“Every day that this continues, the oil price is ratcheting up about 5% a day,” Young explains. “People keep betting on there being some sort of quick fix… but there isn’t a quick solution here.”

This view finds support from Rick Rule, founder of Rule Investment Media, who warned in early March—with WTI in the low $70s—that any Iranian threat to Hormuz would send the oil market “crazy.”

“Over 50% of the world’s export crude moves through that 50km strait,” Rule noted. “The financial and psychological implications of that before they work themselves out would be tremendous.”

## The Domino Effect: Bonds, Asia, and Strategic Reserves

The crisis’s repercussions extend far beyond the oil ticker. Luke Gromen, founder of Forest for the Trees, warns the oil spike could trigger a second crisis in the U.S. Treasury market.

“The world holds treasuries and the world needs oil and food,” Gromen says. “Is the world going to hold its treasuries and starve itself of food and oil or is it going to sell its treasuries to bid up oil and food?… Ultimately, the longer this goes on I think you will see a huge natural buyer of treasuries turn seller.”

Geographically, the pain is concentrated. Approximately 90% of Hormuz’s oil flows to Asia, making U.S. allies like Korea, Japan, Thailand, the Philippines, and India the most exposed.

“The idea that the Persian Gulf could stay blocked for weeks perhaps months creates legitimate economic challenges for the whole region in a way that… it doesn’t for the United States,” says Louis-Vincent Gave, CEO of Gavecal. “The United States for all intents and purposes is energy self-sufficient. Asia isn’t.”

China, while impacted, may be more insulated. Sean Rein, founder of China Market Research Group, points to Beijing’s 15-year push for energy security through massive investments in renewables. Over 50% of new car sales in China last year were EVs, and the country added more solar and wind capacity than the rest of the world combined.

“Yes, China is going to be hit… but China over the last 10-15 years has been trying to lower its dependence and improve its energy security,” Rein notes.

Historical context also tempers the panic, according to Professor Steve Hanke of Johns Hopkins University. Compared to the 1979 oil shock, the world is less dependent on Middle Eastern oil, the U.S. produces a larger share globally, and “oil intensity”—the amount of oil used per unit of GDP—has “declined dramatically.”

Yet, timing trumps structure. Retired Marine Lt. Col. Hal Kempfer puts the IEA’s 400-million-barrel reserve release in stark perspective: “It can make a big difference for maybe 10 days, 2 weeks… and then we’re right back where we were.”

## The Bear Case: A "Fear Premium" Ready to Implode

Not everyone is bracing for triple-digit oil. Some traders are actively betting against it.

Todd “Bubba” Horowitz, founder of BubbaTrading.com, is short crude. He calls the spike a “pure fear trade” and points to a critical data point: the forward curve for WTI one year out is priced at just $69.

“The futures markets… is telling you that this price will not last,” Horowitz argues. “Only 20% of the world’s oil goes through the straits of Hormuz. And none of it's WTI… I would not be surprised as soon as this thing resolves out to see oil back in the 70s or 60s.”

From a charting perspective, Christopher Vermeulen, Chief Market Strategist at The Technical Traders, agrees. He notes oil’s long-term 150-day moving average is still pointing down, framing the recent spike as a “counter-trend rally within a bear market.”

“News-driven gaps of this kind typically fade unless the underlying fundamentals change permanently,” Vermeulen says. “This type of move is typically a capitulation move… The news is going to fade away, and we’ll see the fear kind of drop as well.”

## The Investor’s Dilemma: Navigating the Split

With experts divided, one path is to look past the crude price itself to the equities underneath.

Danny Moses, famed for his role in *The Big Short*, has been bullish on energy stocks since before the crisis. His thesis is simple: energy was the most undervalued, under-owned sector in the S&P long before a single bomb fell.

“If you just told me that West Texas would sit at $60… look at Exxon Mobil,” Moses says. “They put out a 2030 plan based on $65 oil. If that happens, this is the cheapest stock in the S&P 500.”

## The Bottom Line

The market now hosts two compelling, evidence-backed narratives. The bulls see a physical supply crisis with no quick fix, where prices have yet to reflect the full danger. The bears see a parabolic fear premium in a market still well-supplied outside the Persian Gulf.

What remains undisputed is the Strait of Hormuz’s pivotal role, Asia’s disproportionate vulnerability, and energy’s rapid ascent from a market afterthought to the central variable in the global economic outlook for 2026. As the past three weeks have proven, certainty is the first casualty in this conflict. The only guarantee is more volatility ahead.
