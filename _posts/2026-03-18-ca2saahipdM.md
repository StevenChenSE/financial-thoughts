**March 17** — On the surface, today’s market looks familiar: WTI crude trades near $95, Brent sits above $103, and the S&P attempts a bounce. But this is not a normal day. Two and a half weeks ago, U.S. and Israeli strikes targeted Iran, culminating in the death of its Supreme Leader. Iran’s retaliation was swift and severe: the **Strait of Hormuz—a chokepoint for one-fifth of the world’s crude oil—is now closed.**

The immediate aftermath saw WTI spike from $77 to $119 per barrel, the Dow drop 900 points, and Asian markets plunge 7–12% in a week. In response, the International Energy Agency (IEA) released 400 million barrels from emergency reserves, and sanctions on Russian oil imports were lifted.

What happens next? Experts are sharply divided. Some see a relentless march toward all-time highs; others call the surge a temporary fear premium. Here are the key insights from energy investors, geopolitical analysts, and traders.

---

## The Bull Case: A Physical Crisis With No Quick Fix

**Josh Young, CIO of Bison Interests**, argues that if the Strait remains shut, we could see inflation-adjusted record highs.

> “If this does not resolve soon, we should see all-time high oil prices… In 2008, we got to about $147 a barrel. If you inflation adjust that, you're talking potentially $200 plus.”

Young emphasizes this isn’t speculation—it’s mechanics. Iran has deployed small boats, sea mines, and anti-ship missiles, effectively stranding most commercial vessels. Three ships were hit in a single morning last week.

> “Every day that this continues, the oil price is ratcheting up about 5% a day… People keep betting on there being some sort of quick fix, but there isn’t a quick solution here.”

**Rick Rule**, founder of Rule Investment Media, warned of this scenario in a March 1 recording—back when WTI traded in the low $70s.

> “If Iran is capable of responding by temporarily shutting down the Straits of Hormuz… the oil quote goes crazy. I mean crazy… Over 50% of the world’s export crude moves through that 50km strait. The financial and psychological implications… would be tremendous.”

Rule believes Iran cannot sustain a blockade indefinitely but notes that proving it may take 4–6 weeks—an eternity for oil markets.

---

## The Domino Effect: Bonds, Asia, and Strategic Reserves

The crisis extends beyond oil prices. **Luke Groman**, founder of Forest for the Trees, warns of a secondary crisis in the U.S. Treasury market.

> “The world holds treasuries and the world needs oil and food… Is the world going to hold its treasuries and starve itself of food and oil, or is it going to sell its treasuries to bid up oil and food? To me it’s not a very difficult question to answer.”

Approximately 90% of Hormuz oil flows to Asia, making U.S. allies like **Japan, South Korea, Thailand, the Philippines, and India** the most exposed. **Louis-Vincent Gave**, CEO of Gavecal, notes:

> “The idea that the Persian Gulf could stay blocked for weeks perhaps months creates legitimate economic challenges for the whole region in a way that it doesn’t for the United States.”

China is somewhat insulated due to massive strategic stockpiles and a 15-year push into renewables. **Shaun Rein**, founder of China Market Research Group, points out:

> “China over the last 10–15 years has been trying to lower its dependence… investing billions into solar and wind.” In 2023 alone, China added more solar and wind capacity than the rest of the world combined.

Yet, even with these buffers, the IEA’s 400-million-barrel emergency release may only buy **10–14 days** of time, according to retired Marine Lt. Col. **Hal Kempfer**.

> “It can make a big difference for maybe 10 days, 2 weeks, and then we’re right back where we were.”

---

## The Bear Case: A Parabolic Fear Premium

Not everyone is buying the super-spike narrative. **Todd “Bubba” Horowitz**, founder of BubbaTrading.com, is short crude and calls the rally “the selling opportunity of a lifetime.”

> “This is purely a fear trade right now… Only 20% of the world’s oil goes through the Strait of Hormuz. And none of it’s WTI. If you look out into the future, a year from now, oil is trading at $69.”

He points to the steeply backwardated futures curve as evidence that the market expects prices to fall dramatically once the crisis abates.

**Christopher Mullen**, Chief Market Strategist at The Technical Traders, agrees from a charting perspective. He notes that oil’s long-term trend remains bearish, with the 150-day moving average still pointing down.

> “This is a counter-trend rally within a bear market… The news is going to fade away, and we’ll see the fear kind of drop as well.”

---

## The Historical Context: Better Prepared, But Still Vulnerable

Is this 1979 all over again? **Professor Steve Hanke** of Johns Hopkins University, a veteran petroleum economist, says the structural risks are lower today.

> “Iran in 1978 was producing 8.5% of all the world’s oil production. Now they’re at 5.2%… The oil intensity—the amount of oil we use per unit of GDP—has declined dramatically.”

The U.S. is also less dependent, producing 18.9% of global supply today versus 15.6% in 1978. Yet, as Hanke acknowledges, structure and timing are different—a sudden physical shortfall can overwhelm longer-term improvements.

---

## Where Does This Leave Investors?

The divide is stark: bulls like Josh Young see $200 oil; bears like Bubba Horowitz are shorting at $95. One way to navigate the disagreement is to look beyond the commodity to equities.

**Danny Moses**, famed from *The Big Short*, has been bullish on energy stocks since before the crisis.

> “Energy was the most undervalued, underowned sector in the S&P 500… If $65 oil happens, Exxon Mobil is the cheapest stock in the S&P.”

For Moses, the geopolitical crisis is a catalyst, but the fundamental undervaluation was already there.

---

### The Bottom Line

Two camps, one disrupted strait. The bulls see a physical supply crisis with no easy solution; the bears see a parabolic fear spike in a still-ample market. What’s undeniable is that the **Strait of Hormuz is closed, Asia is on the front lines, and energy has catapulted from a market afterthought to the central variable in global macro.**

As events unfold, one lesson from the past three weeks stands clear: certainty has been consistently punished. The only guarantee is volatility.
