Global markets face mounting pressure as liquidity tightens and key assets flash warning signals that experts say foreshadow broader market turbulence ahead

The Federal Reserve under Kevin Warsh is charting a new course that could prove consequential for global markets. While Warsh doesn't want to raise Fed funds rates directly, he's made clear he wants markets to tighten for him—a subtle but significant shift in monetary policy approach. The question now is how much he can allow those tightening forces to work without triggering cascading effects across the global financial system.

"If the dollar goes up, that's clearly a wrecking ball for the world economy and world financial markets," warned Michael Howell, founder and managing director of GLI indexes, formerly CrossBorder Capital, and author of Capital Wars on Substack, in a recent appearance on the show. Howell, who accurately predicted market defensive positioning six months ago ahead of tighter liquidity conditions and higher inflation, is now sounding another alarm.

## The Liquidity Tide Turns

Global liquidity peaked in late 2025 and has been slowing since—a development that matters enormously for markets because liquidity is the marginal price of assets. Howell emphasizes that the slowdown isn't occurring because the Fed is actively stamping on the brakes yet, but rather because the real economy has remained exceptionally strong.

The Fed's recent interventions tell part of the story. Fed liquidity growth, measured by the rate of cash injection into money markets, surged stunningly high following the COVID crisis—peaking at over 80% on a six-month annualized basis. Since then, significant tightening has occurred, including what Howell calls the "liquidity air pocket" and TGA rebuild, followed by repo market pressures at the end of 2025 that prompted the Fed to introduce what he describes as another "disguised QE program" to inject more liquidity.

"Despite that, the strength of the economy means the net is that we're still looking at some sort of inflection downwards," Howell noted, pointing to the underlying structural challenges that even emergency liquidity injections cannot fully offset.

## Gold and Bitcoin: The Canary in the Coal Mine

Two assets in particular are serving as early warning indicators for broader market stress. Gold has declined nearly 30% from its highs earlier this year, while Bitcoin has fallen more than 50% from its all-time high last year. According to Howell, these moves represent more than just profit-taking—they signal what's coming for the rest of markets.

"These two assets serve as a warning for what's to come for the rest of the markets," Howell stated. "We should be concerned because these are all symptoms of late-cycle phenomenon, and Bitcoin and gold are simply barometers of that too."

The collapse in these historically volatile but influential assets suggests that sophisticated investors are positioning defensively ahead of anticipated liquidity constraints.

## Kevin Warsh's Fed: A New Direction?

Warsh's first performance as FOMC chair revealed his strategic priorities. Howell sees two key takeaways from Warsh's remarks. First, Warsh questioned the traditional role of Fed funds as a single policy lever—a position Howell finds "absolutely correct" given modern economic realities.

"I'm not sure what Fed funds rate means in the modern world," Howell explained. "If you've got huge AI spend rocketing ahead, and these corporations with gross margins of 50% or more, what difference do 25 basis points make to their cap expense? Zero."

The second takeaway involves Warsh's preference for allowing markets to self-tighten rather than making bold policy statements. This approach manifests through a rising dollar, rising bond yields, and a steepening yield curve—all occurring without direct Fed intervention.

"He wants the markets to tighten for me," Howell summarized. "And that's what we're seeing."

## The Dollar's Imminent Surge

Prediction markets are positioning for significant dollar strength. Traders currently assign a 70% probability that the DXY dollar index will climb above 104 by year-end, up from current levels around 101, with 57% odds of exceeding 105.

Why are markets so bullish on the dollar? Howell points to multiple reinforcing factors. The latest surge reflects enthusiasm around US technology leadership, but it's built on a structural foundation established after the Global Financial Crisis. Post-GFC banking and insurance regulations fundamentally changed the attractiveness of US assets for international institutions.

"This is underpinned by capital flow," Howell explained, displaying charts showing the dollar in a sustained uptrend since the GFC. "International banks and international insurance companies are flowing money into dollar assets. And that's coming on top of what you're likely to see—a Fed tightening."

The implication, according to Howell, is clear: "Perceptions of a much stronger dollar are very likely. Warsh has indicated he wants markets to tighten, and that really means a stronger dollar and rising yields or a steepening curve."

## Bond Market Dynamics and the MOVE Index

Bond volatility—measured by the MOVE Index—has emerged as a critical indicator of monetary system health. Howell emphasizes that the modern financial system is "collateral based," meaning borrowers must post collateral before obtaining loans, whether for home mortgages or complex financial transactions involving government bonds.

"When bond volatility is low, credit creation, liquidity creation works very well," Howell noted. "But as soon as you start to see jumps in bond volatility, you get problems."

The Treasury has responded to volatility spikes by implementing buyback programs—removing old "off-the-run" illiquid bonds from the system and replacing them with new issues. This dynamic shows up clearly in the relationship between Treasury buyback volumes and the MOVE Index: every time volatility jumps, the Treasury increases buyback activity, essentially controlling the system. However, this control mechanism itself signals underlying fragility.

## Emerging Markets in the Crosshairs

The combination of dollar strength and tighter global liquidity creates a particularly challenging environment for emerging markets. When the dollar rises and US yields increase, capital flows toward dollar-denominated assets, draining liquidity from developing economies. This dynamic—combined with the "wrecking ball" effect Warsh's approach could have on global financial markets—suggests emerging markets face significant headwinds.

The consensus view at the beginning of this year was overwhelmingly bullish on a steepening yield curve. Instead, markets have experienced curve flattening—a development that contradicts expectations and may signal deeper structural concerns.

## What Comes Next

Howell's track record lends credibility to his warnings. Six months ago, when Wall Street was near record levels, he advised viewers to get defensive ahead of tighter liquidity conditions and higher inflation. That prediction proved accurate, and now he's offering fresh guidance.

Central banks globally are moving toward tightening as inflation picks up, with most operating under pure inflation or anti-inflation mandates that compel action. The Fed's balance sheet reduction, attempted briefly in late 2025, immediately created repo market problems—echoing similar issues from 2015.

In the short term, expect no significant Fed balance sheet shrinkage. The technical reforms needed to reduce the balance sheet while maintaining adequate banking system cushions would take considerable time and political will. But the underlying trend toward tighter liquidity appears established.

For investors, the implications are clear: brace for continued dollar strength, monitor bond volatility closely, and treat gold and Bitcoin's declines as early warnings of broader market stress to come. The "wrecking ball" may not have struck yet, but the warning signals suggest it's only a matter of time before its effects become visible across global markets.
