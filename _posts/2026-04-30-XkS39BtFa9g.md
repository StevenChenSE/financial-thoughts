## The Geopolitical Shockwave Shaking Oil Markets

The global oil market is experiencing unprecedented turbulence as a convergence of geopolitical forces—from the UAE's dramatic departure from OPEC to the escalating US-Iran standoff threatening the world's most critical oil transit corridor—creates conditions that veteran analysts say could push crude prices well beyond current levels.

Bob Ryan, founder of Ryan Commodity Insights and former chief commodity strategist at BCA Research, brings decades of energy market experience to bear on these interconnected crises. Speaking in an extensive market interview, Ryan painted a picture of a market already stretched thin, with physical crude trading at $15 to $20 per barrel premiums over futures—a backwardation that he describes as "absolutely extraordinary" and one that signals refiners in Asia are already in "panic mode."

"We could see prices moving higher in the second half of the year," Ryan warned. "We do have a scenario where it does go to like 200 dollars [per barrel]."

The baseline forecast puts Brent crude averaging around $100 per barrel for the remainder of the year, but with risks firmly tilted to the upside as the standoff between Washington and Tehran shows no signs of imminent resolution.

## UAE's OPEC Exit: A Seismic Shift in Oil Politics

### Long-Brewing Tensions Finally Surface

The United Arab Emirates' announced departure from OPEC represents more than a simple organizational change—it signals a fundamental fracture in the Gulf alliance that has long governed global oil policy. Ryan characterized the move as "negative for the OPEC plus coalition" and "particularly negative for the OPEC producers in the Gulf and elsewhere."

The UAE, which produces approximately 12% of OPEC's total output, has been pushing for higher production quotas almost continuously. Ryan explained that the Saudis "were very persuasive and very effective in convincing everybody to maintain production discipline and keep their production at levels that had been agreed from previous meetings." This set the stage for "disagreement between the UAE and the Saudis almost at every meeting," with the UAE consistently seeking higher output quotas.

### What the Departure Means for Production

With the UAE no longer bound by OPEC production agreements, Ryan expects the nation to pursue an aggressive independent production agenda. "UAE is not going to be bound by any agreement to restrain production," he noted. "Saudi is going to be still running the show within OPEC and OPEC plus, but UAE is not going to be bound by any agreement to restrain production."

The timing of this departure is particularly significant given that no oil is currently flowing through the Gulf to speak of. Ryan suggested this combination "is setting the stage for possibly another fight once the market settles down," and he doesn't expect any market stability "for at least another two years, even if we get an agreement next week on ceasefire."

## The Strait of Hormuz Crisis: Why the US Navy Can't Simply Reopen the Chokepoint

### Military Reality on the Ground

The Strait of Hormuz represents one of the world's most strategically vital—and now most dangerous—oil transit points. Ryan, a US Navy veteran who has personally traversed the waterway, provided a sobering assessment of why American naval power cannot simply guarantee passage.

"The most narrow point of the straight... where it bends around, it's very easy for the Iranian navies, the IRGC, and the Basij—who are the paramilitaries that operate in Iran—to pull missiles out of bunkers in the side of the straight or to zero in on traffic in the mountains," Ryan explained. "The Zagos mountains are up there as well."

The critical vulnerability is asymmetry: "They only have to do that once or twice to shut down the straight should it reopen. They only have to land a drone or a couple of drones or a missile or a couple of missiles and everybody will stop again."

### Iran's Mosaic Strategy: Preparing for Biblical-Scale Warfare

Iran has implemented what analysts call its "mosaic strategy"—a doctrine developed after studying US military experiences in Vietnam, Afghanistan, and Iraq. Ryan described how this approach is designed "to withstand the loss of the supreme leader and the loss of the structural hierarchy," with command and control "pushed down to the lowest possible level."

"These guys are operating somewhat autonomously, running operations and then fading back into the background," Ryan said. "They've been preparing for biblical-scale warfare since 1979, the revolution began."

The implications for naval operations are stark: "The US has demonstrated that it can do damage on a massive scale, but what they're encountering now is Iran has activated this mosaic strategy... they're prepared to absorb a lot of pain."

### Capabilities and Limitations Exposed

The current conflict has revealed both strengths and vulnerabilities in US and allied air defense systems. While "the fourth [generation aircraft] generally in the US and among the Arab allies of the US and the Gulf have been pretty impressive—taking down most of the drones and missiles," the reality is they "are not getting all of them."

The engagement to recover a downed pilot resulted in significant US losses: "I think something like seven US aircraft were damaged in that whole engagement... This is destruction on a biblical scale that's been unleashed there."

## The Ceasefire Question: What Terms Could Bridge the Divide?

### Ryan's Assessment: A Coin Toss

Despite prediction markets pricing a 53% chance of a US-Iran nuclear deal before 2027, Ryan remains cautious about near-term prospects. "The odds of us getting something signed this year is about 50-50," he said. "So it's a coin toss."

Even if a ceasefire agreement is reached tomorrow, the path to normalized oil flows will be long: "It's going to take at least the rest of the year to clear the cargos that are already stranded inside the Gulf and get them moving and doing their round trips so they can fill their vessels and relieve some of the storage problems that they're having in the Gulf."

### Red Lines and Must-Haves

Understanding what each side needs from a potential agreement illuminates why negotiations remain so difficult:

**From the US perspective:**

- Free passage of shipping through the Gulf represents their fundamental red line

**From Iran's perspective (based on their 10-point demand set from initial Islamabad meetings):**

- Continued nuclear enrichment rights
- Reparations for the conflict
- Recognition as the authority controlling passage through the Gulf

### The Toll Road Question

Perhaps the most troubling potential outcome involves a scenario where the US accepts an Iranian toll on passing ships as part of any agreement going forward. Such an arrangement could net Iran "billions of dollars every year in additional oil revenue," potentially funding infrastructure repair and military rebuilding.

Ryan acknowledged this uncomfortable possibility: "And so the question is, what was all this for if that could happen? I do not have an answer... I think you know it... I don't know what the logic is. I don't know what the logic is that's driving this decision-making right now. I think I'm in line with everybody else. This is reality TV diplomacy."

## Oil Market Dynamics: Backwardation and Price Signals

### The Extraordinary Backwardation

The current structure of oil futures markets reveals the physical market's desperate state. Front-month WTI and Brent contracts are trading $15 to $20 per barrel above June contracts—a differential that Ryan noted "normally like a $3 differential."

"This is signaling that refiners right now, refiners in Asia, are in panic mode. They're movering up as much fruit as they can to keep their refineries up and running," Ryan explained. "They're looking at $130 per barrel probably by May and probably by the second half of this year, you're probably looking at $150."

The backwardation reflects refiners pricing into spot markets rather than futures, willing to "pay through the offer" to secure immediate crude supplies.

### Why Futures Don't Reflect Physical Reality

The divergence between physical and futures markets creates particular challenges for refiners. "They're purchasing that crude based on Brent dated—which is cargos that will deliver within the next two weeks or at most within the next month—which is typically the voyage time that a lot of the stuff gets priced on," Ryan said.

While refiners are currently making more money due to the spread between their input costs (indexed to futures) and selling prices (indexed to spot markets), Ryan warned this becomes "way more difficult to do that. And if it starts going ballistic, you run the risk of governments stepping in and not allowing these cost pass-throughs"—referencing European interventions during the Russian invasion of Ukraine.

### The Demand Destruction Question

As oil prices climb, the critical question becomes: at what price level does demand destruction offset supply losses?

Ryan explained: "You're looking at prices taking serious leg higher... What we're trying to sort and what we're trying to solve for is: what's the price level at which enough demand is knocked off the grid to offset the loss of supply?"

The challenge is that oil demand is highly inelastic: "People don't stop driving their cars to work just because prices go to $130 bucks."

## US Oil Exports: The Silver Lining?

### Record Export Levels

Seaborne US oil exports have climbed to record levels, reaching 6.6 million barrels per day in April, with flows to Asia nearly doubling from pre-war levels to 2.5 million barrels per day. S&P Global's Platts has announced changes to Brent crude pricing mechanisms effective May 1st, expanding the number of approved terminals from which WTI Midland crude can be loaded into the Brent pool.

Ryan estimated this could add "up to 500,000 barrels a day" to US exports—bringing total US crude exports to around 6 to 6.5 million barrels per day.

### The Blending Opportunity

US light sweet crude—particularly condensate—creates opportunities for blending with heavier crudes from Venezuela and Mexico. "A lot of the refining capacity that was reliant on Middle East Gulf exports are over in Asia and they are geared to run heavier crude than WTI," Ryan noted. "So they'll have to blend it themselves, or the producers can blend it, get it on the water and over to them."

### The Economic Incentive Question

With US oil exports reaching record levels precisely when Middle East supplies are disrupted, a troubling question emerges: does the current administration have an economic incentive to let the situation continue?

"There might be," Ryan acknowledged. "I could see the case for it."

However, the analysis cuts both ways. Higher WTI and Brent prices risk demand destruction that could be felt throughout the economy, particularly if government intervention prevents refiners from passing through costs.

## Oil, Inflation, and Bond Markets: The Interconnection

### The Hamilton Research Framework

Economic research, particularly the work of James Hamilton at UC San Diego, has established that US recessions are frequently preceded by spikes in oil prices. "The amount of economic activity that is being supported by lesser and lesser oil consumption is the thing to look at," Ryan said, referencing observations made by Goldman Sachs' Jeff Currie.

The critical insight: while oil's percentage of GDP has declined, the absolute level of economic activity supported by oil consumption continues to grow, particularly as e-commerce and transportation expand. "More stuff that Amazon ships, more stuff that refiners have to provide fuel for to transport across the country or across the world."

### The Oil-Yield Connection

Recent years have seen bond yields and oil prices move in unusual lockstep. Ryan identified the mechanism: "The channel where this effect gets registered is through the forward CPI index market, because the CPI is used to settle up inflation-protected treasury inflation-protected securities like TIPS."

Oil prices for delivery three years out serve as effective predictors of inflation expectations, which in turn influence Treasury yields. This creates a direct relationship between crude prices and the long end of the bond market.

### The Liquidity Concern: Swap Lines and EM Stress

Reports that Asian and Gulf country allies have requested swap lines from the Federal Reserve or Treasury signal potential dollar funding stress. "80 or 90% of global trade is invoiced in dollars and paid in dollars," Ryan noted. "So just having access to dollars, being able to borrow dollars when you need to purchase stuff that you need to bring into your country—having those lines available is critically important."

This request for dollar backstops may indicate that markets are not fully pricing in bond turmoil brewing in the background, particularly as US debt markets face pressure from higher interest rate demands.

### Fed Policy in the Crossfire

The CME FedWatch Tool currently shows 100% probability of no rate change at the upcoming FOMC meeting, with meaningful rate cut expectations not emerging until January 2027. If the Fed does not cut rates this year, Ryan raised the possibility of alternative monetary policy responses.

"You get life gets really complicated when you start having real inflation coming into the market through the commodities, and that shows up as a factor that the debt markets have to price in," Ryan observed. "And then on top of that, you're trying to do QE, right? You're trying to keep rates low, which allows you to encourage borrowing when you know fiscal spending is going up."

This dynamic—quantitative easing potentially deployed to manage Treasury issuance while commodity-driven inflation pressures the opposite—echoes Japanese bond market management and represents uncharted territory for US monetary policy.

## The Second-Half Outlook: Scenarios and Sector Implications

### Base Case and Upside Scenarios

Ryan's base case projects Brent crude averaging around $100 per barrel through year-end, but with significant upside risks: "There is a pretty good likelihood that we do see this standoff between Iran and the US persist into the second half of the year, and prices really start moving higher to destroy demand."

The $200 scenario becomes relevant "if we continue to lose supply or we return supply slower than we're assuming, and with the supply losses that are coming through the market and not being replaced... you're looking at prices taking serious leg higher."

### Where Damage Meets Demand Destruction

The ongoing transit crisis creates compounding risks for production: "Every day you don't resolve this transit crisis, you're putting forth more oil into storage, which means you reach a point where you can't put any more in. You have to start cutting. And that risks damaging the wells that are responsible for all this production. And that's a long-term process to restart. And sometimes it won't be restarted because the damage has just been too extensive to the casings and the well itself."

### Sectors That Could Benefit

Beyond obvious beneficiaries like refiners and producers, Ryan noted emerging evidence that technology stocks may serve as a "safe haven" during oil shocks: "Tech spending is going to occur no matter what... They're not immune to what's going on here, but their shelter from the storm is my impression."

### Commodity Ripple Effects

Oil-driven natural gas price increases—particularly ex-North America where prices are trading much stronger—threaten fertilizer markets (most fertilizers derive from natural gas), with direct implications for food prices and processed food costs. Ryan acknowledged the difficulty of finding direct plays: "Very difficult... like what do you pick out? A lot of these trading companies were caught wrongfooted when the US and Israel started bombing Iran and they had hedges on for food that they had in the Gulf waiting to go to its destination."

The broader implication: "You're very directly taking an oil view when you do that" [signing contracts for commodity delivery].

## Key Takeaways

1. **The UAE's OPEC departure marks a fundamental fracture in Gulf oil cooperation**, likely resulting in higher production from Abu Dhabi and complicating Saudi Arabia's efforts to manage supply—a development that Ryan doesn't expect to stabilize "for at least another two years."

2. **The Strait of Hormuz cannot be reopened by force alone** due to Iran's mosaic strategy of dispersed, autonomous operations; the US faces an adversary prepared to absorb massive damage and capable of shutting the strait with minimal assets each time commercial traffic attempts to resume.

3. **Physical oil markets are in crisis**, with refiners in Asia paying $15-$20 premiums over futures to secure supplies; this extraordinary backwardation signals potential prices of $130 by May and $150 by year-end under current trajectory.

4. **Oil prices and bond yields are interconnected through inflation expectations**, meaning continued oil market stress could push Treasury yields higher even as the Fed holds rates steady—creating a policy dilemma that may force unconventional responses.

5. **The economic and geopolitical incentives may be misaligned**, with US oil exports reaching record levels during the crisis, potentially creating domestic economic benefits that conflict with the goal of resolving the underlying conflict.
