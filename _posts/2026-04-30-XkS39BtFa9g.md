The oil world was shaken on April 28th when the United Arab Emirates officially announced its departure from OPEC—a decision that veteran oil analyst Bob Ryan describes as "negative for the OPEC plus coalition" and particularly damaging to Gulf producers who have relied on the cartel's collective discipline. This seismic shift comes at perhaps the worst possible moment: with WTI crude hovering near $100 per barrel, the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed, and U.S. refiners absorbing extraordinary costs in a market plagued by backwardation. As Ryan, founder of Ryan Commodity Insights and former chief commodity strategist at BCA Research, explains: "We're losing 10 million barrels a day of production," and even a ceasefire between the U.S. and Iran—which he rates at just 50/50 odds for this year—wouldn't begin to normalize markets until well into 2026.

## The UAE-OPEC Split: A Long Time Coming

The UAE's exit from OPEC shouldn't come as a surprise to those who've followed the organization closely. According to Ryan, the tension between the UAE and Saudi Arabia has been "long simmering," with Abu Dhabi pushing for higher production quotas at "almost every round of meetings."

### The Core of the Disagreement

The Saudis, Ryan explains, have been "very persuasive and very effective in convincing everybody to maintain production discipline and keep their production at levels that had been agreed from previous meetings." This set the stage for recurring friction at OPEC gatherings, where the UAE consistently sought increased output quotas.

### What UAE's Departure Means

With the UAE no longer bound by OPEC agreements:

- **Production restraint obligations end**: Abu Dhabi can now pursue its own production agenda without cartel constraints
- **Saudi Arabia maintains influence**: Riyadh will continue running OPEC and OPEC+ decisions
- **Signals coalition fragility**: The exit underscores deeper fractures within the petroleum alliance
- **Competitive dynamics shift**: UAE can freely invest in capacity expansion

Ryan notes the UAE produces approximately 12% of OPEC's total output, making this a consequential defection for the organization's collective production management.

## The Strait of Hormuz Crisis: Why the Navy Can't Simply Reopen It

As a U.S. Navy veteran who has personally transited the Strait of Hormuz, Ryan offers unique insight into why American naval power hasn't resolved the shipping crisis. The narrow waterway—where traffic must navigate a bend near the Iranian coast—is precisely designed to be a chokepoint, and Iran has prepared for this scenario since 1979.

### Iran's Mosaic Defense Strategy

Iran has implemented what it calls its "mosaic strategy," developed after studying American military experiences in Vietnam, Afghanistan, and Iraq:

- **Distributed command structure**: Even if leadership is eliminated, command and control operates at the lowest possible levels
- **Autonomous local operations**: IRGC and Basij paramilitaries can launch attacks independently
- **Terrain advantage**: Missiles can be deployed from bunkers and mountains overlooking the strait
- **Retreat and reload capability**: Attackers can "fade back into the background" and launch again

### The Insurmountable Challenge

The fundamental problem for the U.S. Navy, Ryan explains, is that Iran "only has to do that once or twice to shut down the street should it reopen." A single drone or missile strike will stop all commercial shipping again, regardless of American military superiority.

"They've been preparing for biblical scale warfare since 1979," Ryan notes, adding that while the U.S. has "demonstrated that it can do damage on a massive scale," Iran is prepared to absorb that punishment and continue operations.

The toll on American forces has been significant. During the search for a downed pilot, "something like seven U.S. aircraft were damaged," illustrating the real costs of this asymmetric engagement.

## Oil Market Outlook: $100 Is Just the Beginning

Even with Brent crude trading near $100 per barrel, Ryan sees substantially higher prices ahead. His base case assumes $100 for the remainder of 2025, but he acknowledges this may be too optimistic given weekend developments where "Iranians just walked away from the Islamabad talks."

### Near-Term Price Projections

- **May 2025**: Potentially $130 per barrel as Asian refiners bid aggressively for spot cargoes
- **Q2/Q3 2025**: $150 range as supply constraints bite harder
- **Disruption scenario**: $200 per barrel is "very difficult to call right now, but it's becoming a lot more likely"

The catalyst for higher prices: "We continue to lose supply or we return supply slower than we're assuming... you're looking at prices taking serious leg higher."

### The Refiner's Burden

The current market structure is placing extraordinary stress on refiners. Ryan highlights the extreme backwardation:

| Normal Spread | Current Spread |
|---------------|----------------|
| $3 per barrel | $15-20 per barrel |

"Refiners are the ones that are bidding through the offer to get more of that crude into the Brent pool," Ryan explains. "They're wearing that." While refiners will report higher revenues, the hedging complexity and cost absorption make operations increasingly difficult.

### US Export Infrastructure Opening Up

One potential offset: Platts (owned by S&P) announced changes to Brent benchmark methodology effective May 1st, expanding the number of approved loading terminals for WTI Midland crude destined for the North Sea benchmark pool. This could add 500,000 barrels per day to U.S. exports—potentially reaching 6.5 million barrels per day—though this remains far short of the 10 million barrels per day being lost.

## The Demand Destruction Question

Higher oil prices will inevitably destroy demand, but Ryan cautions against assuming this happens quickly. "People don't stop driving their cars to work just because prices go to 130 bucks," he notes. "It's very inelastic demand."

### Where Damage Shows Up

- **Industrial activity**: Manufacturing and heavy industry face immediate input cost pressures
- **Household spending priorities**: Transportation spending becomes harder to cut, squeezing other categories
- **Food prices**: Natural gas price increases (already rising outside North America) push fertilizer costs higher, transmitting oil shock into agricultural markets

Ryan references research by economist James Hamilton at UC San Diego, whose "benchmark study" established that U.S. recessions are frequently preceded by oil price spikes. "We're going to be seeing more of that for sure into the second half as the oil price shock makes its way through," Ryan predicts.

### The GDP Intensity Paradox

While some argue modern economies are less dependent on oil than in the 1970s, Ryan cites Goldman Sachs colleague Jeff Curry's counterargument: "The amount of economic activity that is being supported by lesser and lesser oil consumption is the thing to look at." More economic activity—Amazon shipments, transportation networks, data center cooling—still requires oil. The inverse relationship means less oil supports more GDP, not less.

## Oil, Bonds, and Federal Reserve Policy

The relationship between crude oil and Treasury yields has become "very close" since 2020, as Ryan's research shows. The transmission mechanism runs through the forward CPI index market, which settles Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS).

### The Yield-Oil Connection

"When you regress inflation expectations—so like 5-year, 5-year treasury rates on that—you pump that into a regression on yield and that's where you get that alignment occurring," Ryan explains. Oil price movements directly impact inflation expectations, which flow through to nominal bond yields.

### Fed Stuck Between a Rock and a Hard Place

With futures markets pricing zero rate cuts at the upcoming FOMC meeting and no meaningful probability of cuts until January 2027, the Fed faces constrained options:

- **Higher oil → higher inflation expectations → higher yields**
- **Fiscal expansion continuing** (the "big beautiful bill" and depreciation provisions)
- **Growing U.S. debt** requiring market absorption

Ryan sees potential pressure for the Fed to implement quantitative easing to manage long-end yields. "Japan ran that—they managed the 10-year rate by making credit available to keep the 10-year Japanese bond rate within a desired range," he notes. "So, we may be forced to do that."

The danger: "You're trying to keep rates low... so you're encouraging borrowing when you know fiscal spending is going up."

## The Dollar Swap Line Implications

Reports that Asian and Gulf country allies have requested dollar swap lines—from either the Fed or Treasury—suggest underlying stress in dollar funding markets that may not be fully priced by equity markets.

### Why Swap Lines Matter

- **80-90% of global trade** is invoiced in dollars and settled in dollars
- **Dollar liquidity critical**: Countries need dollar access to purchase essential imports
- **Preventing disorderly asset sales**: Swap lines prevent forced selling of U.S. assets

"EM currencies that are trading stronger right now on a relative basis" may be partly reflecting this dollar scarcity premium, Ryan suggests. Countries are pricing in potential dollar funding constraints and hedging accordingly.

## Geopolitical Resolution: What Would a Ceasefire Require?

For a U.S.-Iran agreement, both sides have clearly defined red lines:

**U.S. Requirements:**

- Free passage of shipping through the Gulf (the absolute minimum)

**Iranian Requirements (from their 10-point demand framework):**

- Continued nuclear enrichment rights
- Reparations for military damage
- Recognized authority over Gulf passage (potentially including tolls)

The most controversial potential term: Iran accepting an "Iranian toll on passing ships" as a condition of any agreement. This would generate billions in annual revenue—potentially funding military reconstruction—while raising uncomfortable questions about the purpose of the entire military campaign.

## Investment Implications: Finding Shelter in the Storm

Beyond obvious beneficiaries like oil producers and refiners, Ryan sees limited safe havens. However, some sectors offer relative protection:

### Technology as Unexpected Shelter

"Tech being a kind of safe haven because that spending is going to occur no matter what," Ryan observes. Despite concerns about hyperscaler capital expenditure requirements, technology demand appears structurally resilient.

### Agricultural and Base Metal Spillovers

The interconnected nature of commodity markets means oil shocks transmit widely:

- Natural gas prices rising globally → fertilizer costs up
- Fertilizer costs → food prices → processed food inflation
- However, trading companies holding Gulf inventories "were caught wrongfooted" and may struggle to capitalize

### Trading Market Dysfunction

"Liquidity in the market particularly for oil is pretty much constrained to about 6 months forward now," Ryan reports. Traders are reluctant to commit to longer-dated positions, creating pricing uncertainty that compounds market stress.

## Key Takeaways

1. **UAE's OPEC departure signals cartel fractures**: Abu Dhabi's exit represents a fundamental weakening of OPEC+ discipline and Saudi Arabia's ability to control production decisions.

2. **Strait of Hormuz may remain closed regardless of military action**: Iran's mosaic defense strategy—designed over decades to withstand overwhelming force—makes permanent reopening nearly impossible without political resolution.

3. **Oil prices likely to test $150+ without rapid ceasefire**: Refiners are already paying $130 for May deliveries; continued supply losses could push prices to $200 in worst-case scenarios.

4. **Demand destruction will arrive but slowly**: Oil's inelastic demand means consumers and businesses will absorb substantial price increases before reducing consumption meaningfully.

5. **Fed faces impossible policy trilemma**: Higher oil → higher inflation expectations → higher yields, while fiscal deficits require bond market absorption and QE may prove necessary despite inflationary optics.
