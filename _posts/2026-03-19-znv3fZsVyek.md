**March 18** — The Federal Reserve’s decision to hold interest rates steady sent immediate shockwaves through financial markets. The S&P 500 fell 1.3%, gold dropped 3%, and Bitcoin slid 4.5%. In an exclusive interview, Chris Whan, Chairman of Whan Global Advisors, argues the central bank is making a critical policy error by focusing on transient inflation while ignoring mounting economic vulnerabilities.

## A Misplaced Focus on Tariffs

During the FOMC press conference, Chair Jerome Powell emphasized that progress on inflation this year hinges on a reduction in goods inflation as tariff effects pass through the economy. Powell estimated that between a half and three-quarters of the current 3% core inflation stems from tariffs.

Whan pushed back strongly on this focus. “The Fed is too fixated on tariffs,” he said. “Chairman Powell made a pretty specific statement about how much of inflation comes from tariffs, but how does he really know? That’s one of these unknowable things.”

He believes the Fed should instead “look through” the current oil price spike stemming from Middle East conflict, arguing elevated energy costs will persist but the underlying economy is slowing. “They should be lowering rates now,” Whan stated, pointing to rising unemployment and a “K-shaped” economy where consumption is increasingly concentrated at the top of the income ladder.

## The Gathering Storm in Private Credit

Whan identifies the unraveling **$2 trillion private credit market** as the systemic risk the Fed is overlooking. He draws a direct line from years of low interest rates to poor investment decisions, particularly the rush into private credit funds.

“Why did people go into private credit? They didn’t like hedge funds… So everybody thought, well, we’ll go into private credit. We’ll make more money and we don’t have the noise from having to value the investments on a daily basis. This has turned out to be a disaster,” Whan explained.

The crisis is now unfolding in real-time. The Financial Times recently reported wealthy investors tried to pull over **$10 billion** from major private credit funds this quarter alone, forcing managers like Blackstone and BlackRock to limit withdrawals. Whan notes the contagion is spreading to funds holding consumer and small-business loans from fintech firms.

“When you see people demanding their money back from a fund which really doesn’t have a terrible amount of risk in it… It tells you that the contagion is spreading,” he warned.

## A Hidden Default Crisis

Perhaps the most alarming data point Whan highlighted is the surge in **Payment-in-Kind (PIK) loans**. In these arrangements, borrowers who cannot pay cash interest simply add more debt to their balance.

“According to KBW, nearly 9% of private credit… is now being paid via payment in kind,” Whan noted. “If a borrower cannot pay interest in cash and instead just adds more debt… is that not just another form of default with a different name? Oh, it is. Yeah, very much.”

He predicts significant markdowns are coming for private assets, as sponsors have been conflicted in reporting valuations. The potential scale is staggering: while average default rates are around 2.6%, UBS has modeled a tail-risk scenario where they could reach **14-15%**. Whan believes the probability of this worst-case scenario is “actually higher” due to opaque private market valuations.

## Labor Market Cracks and Fiscal Fantasy

The weakening job market adds another layer of fragility. Whan revised his previous outlook after the economy lost **92,000 jobs** in the last BLS report. He sees employers using AI as a pretext for headcount reductions, exacerbating the economic stall for the bottom 50-60% of earners.

Looking ahead, Whan is deeply concerned about the U.S. fiscal trajectory, with interest payments on the national debt projected to double to **$2 trillion** by the end of the decade. His forecast is grim: “The Fed is going to be forced to buy more and more Treasury debt… and the result will be inflation.”

## Investment Implications: Caution and Opportunity

So where does this leave investors?

Whan believes the private credit shakeout could ironically benefit public equities, as capital flees back to transparent markets. “There’s not a lot of places to go… That’s one of the reasons I don’t think the public equity market in the US is really going to trade off that much.”

For now, his personal strategy remains cautious. “I’m still mostly focused on precious metals for my own portfolio and income,” he shared, adding that he’s been mostly out of U.S. financials. His advice to readers: “Be cautious.”

The core of Whan’s argument is that the Fed is fighting yesterday’s war against tariff-driven inflation while a debt and liquidity crisis builds in the shadows. By keeping rates “relatively high,” the central bank is increasing stress on over-leveraged parts of the financial system, setting the stage for a potentially sharper policy reversal down the road.

“I think the Fed is going to be forced to act because the markets are under stress,” Whan concluded. “And we’re going to see more of that as time goes forward.”

*Chris Whan is the Chairman of Whan Global Advisors. This analysis is based on an interview conducted on March 18.*
