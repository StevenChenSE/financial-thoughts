## The Legendary Speculator Sounds the Alarm on Economic, Financial, Social, and Military Turmoil

In a wide-ranging conversation on Capital Cosm, legendary investor Doug Casey shared his candid assessment of the global landscape—and the picture he paints is not for the faint of heart. Speaking from the shores of Chesapeake Bay, Virginia, Casey revealed his belief that humanity stands "on the edge of a precipice economically, financially, and sociologically, probably militarily, world war." The next decade, he argues, "stand[s] to be pretty grim."

But this conversation wasn't merely about doom and gloom. It was a masterclass in geopolitical analysis, personal experience, and the nuanced thinking of a man who has spent decades navigating global markets.

## South America: Investment Opportunity or Money Pit?

One of the most compelling segments of the interview centered on Latin America as an investment destination. Casey, who splits his time between Uruguay and Argentina, offered a boots-on-the-ground perspective that challenges conventional wisdom.

"I've put a lot of money in Argentina and Uruguay," Casey admitted. "About 20 years ago in Argentina." He recounted purchasing 200,000 hectares of land—roughly 500,000 acres—in Salta Province in northwestern Argentina, describing it as affordable "like Australia this way. You can buy gigantic pieces of land for nickels and dimes."

Yet the returns haven't materialized as expected. "We could sell it, but there's not really much of a bid," he noted. His Buenos Aires apartment, purchased for approximately $950,000 in 2007, might now be worth "maybe a million and a half now"—meaning he's actually lost money in real terms after nearly two decades.

"South America is basically a place where money goes to die," Casey concluded, while acknowledging some personal success stories in the region.

However, recent political developments are drawing attention. The Argentine ETF (ARGT) has surged approximately 5x since July 2022, while Brazil's EWZ has climbed 50% from its lows. Casey attributes much of Argentina's rally to the 2023 election of Javier Milei, the self-described anarcho-capitalist who campaigned with a chainsaw as his symbol, promising to gut the government.

## The Milei Experiment: Promise Meets Reality

Milei's ascension represented what Casey called "the bottom of the barrel," following decades of Peronism—a movement he describes as "a direct offshoot of Mussolini's fascism."

Anarcho-capitalism, Casey explained, means viewing "government as a predator...an act of evil" rather than simply criticizing whoever currently occupies political office. Milei ran on abolishing the central bank, which Casey sees as "the engine of inflation."

Yet over a year into Milei's presidency, the central bank still exists. "He's done none of these things," Casey observed, expressing concern that "Milei is going to wind up giving libertarianism a bad name."

One particularly puzzling move: Milei has shipped roughly half of Argentina's theoretical 2 million ounces of gold to London. "There's no explanation for it," Casey noted. "What was strange was a couple months after Milei got into office, he took another 440,000 ounces and shipped it to London, too."

The speculation naturally turns to Peter Thiel, the Palantir founder reportedly purchasing property in Argentina and Uruguay—neighbors Casey describes as sharing in Patagonia. "Theo also identifies himself as an anarcho-capitalist," Casey noted, "so I guess he's got a good relationship with Milei." However, Casey points out an irony: "Palantir is actually anti-capitalist. So apparently Thiel has a Chinese wall in his mind for philosophy—the way he may live it personally and where his money goes."

## China: The Unlikely Business Haven

Perhaps the most controversial segment addressed China. Despite Western narratives about communist control, Casey argues it's become "the best place in the world of the major countries for business."

"If you start a business in China," he explained, "you don't have to deal with unions. You have a bare minimum of regulations." The Communist Party, in his view, is "just a scam for the people that are members of the Communist Party."

He paints a picture of remarkable efficiency: "You live in a major city in China. You can get anything you want delivered to your apartment in 20 minutes, and it costs you a dollar or $2 to have it delivered." Most transactions occur through mobile phones rather than cash or credit cards. The country has "leapfrogged from being a really disastrous, horrible, backward place" under Mao to becoming a functional modern economy.

But what about the Russia lesson? Investors who held Russian assets saw them frozen when the Ukraine war began. Casey acknowledges similar risks exist for China, particularly given escalating tensions with Washington. However, he notes China's strategic advantage: "rare earth elements where they have pretty much developed a market monopoly for themselves."

His counterargument: "There's risk everywhere, but I think you've got even more risk not being diversified and staying entirely in the US."

## The Coming Super Bubble and Government Response

Casey's warnings about the United States are perhaps his most dire. He sees the stock market as "grossly overpriced...in a super bubble, not just a bubble, a super bubble." Compounding this, the average American is "more involved in the stock market now than ever before in history."

The scenario he describes is a cascade: "If it [the stock market] bursts and the average American...loses all of his money, he's going to be unhappy. And what will the government do if the stock market crashes? They have to do something, they say. So they'll print up money to make the stock market go up. But if they do that, then prices start rising because that's the cause of inflation is printing money."

The sequence continues: "Everything becomes really unaffordable and it's chaos. And then the government will feel it has to put on controls of some type. Can't ship your money out of the country, can't buy this."

## The Case for Geographic Diversification

Throughout the conversation, Casey emphasized a principle that extends beyond financial assets: geographic diversification.

"People understand that financially," he noted, "but they don't understand that from a personal point of view. If you're just under the control of one government, even if it's the US government—which in my opinion is quite dysfunctional at this point—anything bad can happen unpredictably."

He can cite "a dozen countries in recent times" where government changes led to policies that "absolutely ruined the life of everybody in the country."

## A Contradiction in Optimism?

Despite his grim short-term outlook, Casey revealed he's a fan of Peter Diamandis' work. "He is not just optimistic. He is super optimistic for not just the long-term future but the really short-term future like the next couple three years."

Casey admits he may be right, though he finds Diamandis' talk of the approaching "singularity" extreme. It's a tension Casey himself embodies: clear-eyed about immediate dangers while acknowledging long-term human potential.

---

The conversation with Doug Casey reveals a thinker who has learned through decades of global investing that no system, no government, and no asset class offers permanent safety. His prescription—diversification across geographies, asset classes, and even personal residency—reflects not pessimism but hard-won wisdom. The question isn't whether the precipice will arrive, but whether we've positioned ourselves to survive it.
