The global financial landscape is shifting dramatically, with mortgage brokers and fixed-income executives sounding the alarm on a potential housing crisis that could rival or exceed the severity of 2008. Ron Butler, principal mortgage broker at Butler Mortgage and host of the *Angry Mortgage Podcast*, joined the discussion to analyze the converging forces of rising bond yields, sticky inflation, and crumbling real estate markets in both the United States and Canada.

## The US Mortgage Market: High Rates and Frozen Sellers

In the United States, the housing market is grappling with rates at a nearly three-year high. Freddie Mac reports that the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage average has hit **7.4%**, a significant jump from 6.27% just a year ago. Despite these elevated costs, the shelter component of the Consumer Price Index (CPI) rose 3.2% year-over-year, indicating that housing costs remain a persistent driver of inflation.

However, the market is showing signs of a structural freeze. According to Redfin, there are currently **62.6% more sellers than buyers**, the largest gap in the firm's historical records. This imbalance is largely due to the "lock-in effect." Approximately 40% of US mortgage holders still possess rates around **3%**. These owners are extremely reticent to sell their homes, as purchasing a new property would mean taking on a mortgage rate of 7% or higher. As Butler notes, this is not an economically rational move for those holding low-rate mortgages, leading to a scarcity of inventory and a stalled transaction cycle.

### Adjustable-Rate Mortgages: A Risky Bet?

With fixed rates soaring, some buyers are turning to Adjustable-Rate Mortgages (ARMs). CNBC reports that the share of ARM demand has increased by 10.5%, the highest level in a year. In the US, about 3.1 million ARMs are outstanding, representing roughly 5.6% of active mortgages.

Butler warns that choosing an ARM is a gamble based on the assumption that rates will decrease. However, fixed-income executives suggest a bearish outlook for bond yields, implying that rates may stay higher for longer. "You better be right," Butler cautions, noting that if rates do not fall, the adjustable product can "come around to bite you."

### The Struggle for Homebuilders

The downturn is taking a heavy toll on the construction sector. The State Street Home Builders ETF (**XHB**) is down **22%** from its June highs. Residential construction completions are falling, and potential buyers are waiting on the sidelines, hoping for lower rates or prices. Butler explains that no amount of incentives from builders can overcome the psychological and financial barrier of high mortgage rates combined with economic uncertainty.

## Canada: Foreclosures and the "Dog Crate" Condo Crisis

The situation in Canada presents an even more complex picture, characterized by a different mortgage structure and regional disparities. The Canadian market is currently experiencing what Butler describes as possibly the worst housing crisis since 2008.

### Rising Foreclosures in British Columbia

In Vancouver, court-ordered foreclosures have hit a **10-year high**, surpassing levels seen during the 2008–2009 financial crisis. Delinquencies have jumped by 52%. Butler distinguishes the current crisis from 2008, noting that the previous downturn in Canada was "muted and temporary." Today, the pressure is grinding and sustained.

A critical dynamic emerging is the behavior of underwater borrowers. In a rising or stable market, a homeowner in trouble would sell the home to preserve equity. However, with values falling dramatically, many borrowers cannot sell for enough to cover the mortgage. Consequently, they choose to stop paying entirely, effectively "living for free" while the bank navigates the lengthy power-of-sale process. Butler notes that foreclosed homes typically sell for **8–10% below** the lowest previous sale in the neighborhood, creating new, lower comparables that drag down the entire regional market.

### The "Dog Crate" Condo Phenomenon

Butler coined the term **"dog crate condo"** to describe small, sub-500-square-foot units that suffer from poor layouts and high maintenance fees. This segment is the most vulnerable part of the Canadian real estate market.
*   **Negative Cash Flow:** Many investor-owned condos are underwater, with asking rents falling for 24 consecutive months while property taxes and condo fees rise.
*   **Market Pressure:** Butler advises owners of these negative-cash-flow properties in Ontario and BC to get serious about selling, even at a loss, before conditions worsen further.

### Mortgage Terms: The Shift to 3-Year Fixed

In Canada, the traditional 5-year fixed mortgage, which dominated the market for nearly 90 years, now accounts for only **8%** of new mortgages. Borrowers are increasingly opting for **3-year fixed terms** (often saving 20 basis points) or variable rates, which now comprise over **38%** of the market. Butler recommends a **30-year amortization** for flexibility, highlighting Canada's favorable prepayment options, such as lump-sum payments up to 20% of the mortgage value, which allow borrowers to accelerate payoff and reduce interest costs without committing to a short amortization schedule.

## Central Banks and the Path Forward

The outlook for interest rates remains grim. Bank of Canada Governor Tiff Macklem has signaled a hard shift toward fighting inflation, following similar moves by the US Federal Reserve, the Bank of Japan, and the European Central Bank.

*   **Inflation Prints:** Recent US inflation data was poor, unnerving consumers. Canada’s upcoming inflation print is expected to be similarly weak, likely triggering a rate hike.
*   **Global Synchronization:** Butler argues that central banks are synchronized in their determination to raise rates, regardless of local economic weaknesses like job losses in Canada.

### What Needs to Happen for Recovery

For the Canadian housing market to stabilize, Butler identifies three necessary conditions:
1.  **End of the Trade War:** Resolution of geopolitical economic tensions.
2.  **Full Employment:** A clear path to job security.
3.  **Interest Rate Relief:** A pause or reduction in rates.

Without these factors, Butler predicts that home prices will continue to fall through **2027**, with a potential recovery beginning in **2028**. He advises potential buyers to prioritize the **home price** over the **interest rate**. "If you can wait 8 months and save $75,000 on the price... you've locked that savings in for eternity," Butler says. Interest rates fluctuate, but the purchase price is permanent.

## Conclusion

The bond market crash and subsequent rise in mortgage rates are reshaping real estate in North America. With inventory frozen in the US due to the lock-in effect and foreclosures rising in Canada, the market faces a prolonged correction. Buyers are advised to be aggressive with offers—potentially bidding **20% below asking**—particularly on distressed or foreclosed properties, while investors hold off on speculative assets like small condos until the broader economic conditions stabilize.
