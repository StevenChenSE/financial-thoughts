## How Nvidia and AI Hype Could Drive Precious Metals Higher

### Introduction

Felix Prehn argues that the next major move in gold and silver may not be driven by inflation, Fed policy, or geopolitical shocks—but by the structure and fragility of today’s AI boom. Drawing on recent data, he outlines how Nvidia’s financing, shifting corporate buybacks, drained oil reserves, and extreme U.S. valuation levels create a scenario where precious metals and quality stocks are positioned to benefit. He also stresses that being right on direction is not enough—timing exits is critical.

### Four Signals That Converged in a Single Week

Prehn highlights four developments reported separately but potentially linked:

- Nvidia arranged roughly $500 billion in financing so its own customers could buy Nvidia chips, with the stock rising on the news.
- At the same time, the cost of insuring Nvidia’s debt against default via credit default swaps doubled. Bond traders were quietly demanding more protection even as equities hit all-time highs.
- U.S. mega-cap tech companies shifted from buying about $190 billion in their own shares annually to selling around $147 billion in 2026, turning the long-standing net buyer of equities into a net seller.
- America’s Strategic Petroleum Reserve fell to its lowest level since 1983, leaving little energy buffer for a supply shock.

Taken together, these signals suggest a system running hot on leverage, with diminished cushions and a changing source of equity demand.

### The Trigger: AI, Circular Financing, and Pension Fund Risk

Prehn’s central concern is how the AI boom is funded:

- Nvidia partnered with large asset managers—Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR—to help customers finance GPU purchases.
- The capital ultimately traces back to pension funds and insurance companies.
- The narrative is that GPUs are long-lived assets like toll roads, but AI chips rapidly depreciate as newer, faster models arrive.
- The asset managers collect fees upfront and transfer risk to the lenders—retirement and insurance funds.

He compares this structure to late-1990s telecom financing used by companies like Lucent and Nortel, which lent customers money to buy their own equipment, creating inflated revenue and eventual massive write-downs and bankruptcies.

Meanwhile, the doubling of Nvidia’s CDS spreads signals that bond markets see rising leverage and risk in this model, even while equity markets celebrate AI growth.

### Drained Buffers and the Inflation-Gold Link

With the Strategic Petroleum Reserve at multi-decade lows, the system has minimal shock absorption. Prehn argues:

- A financial accident or AI-related unwind could trigger stress.
- Governments have repeatedly responded to crises over the last 20 years by printing money.
- Large-scale money creation tends to weaken the dollar and boost inflation expectations.
- Gold and silver historically perform well when fiat currencies are debased and real assets become more attractive.

He presents a chart showing that the biggest structural buyer propping up U.S. equities for the last decade—tech buybacks—has now flipped to net selling as companies issue shares to fund AI spending. This removes a key support for stock prices.
