## The Disconnect Between Wall Street and Main Street

### Introduction
Despite recent modest improvements, consumer sentiment in the United States remains at historically low levels, reflecting deep-seated anxiety about the economy. In a recent discussion, Joanne Hsu, Director of the Survey of Consumers at the University of Michigan, analyzed the latest data, revealing that while there was a slight uptick in July, the overall trend remains somber. The prevailing sentiment is driven less by financial market performance and more by "kitchen table" issues, particularly persistent inflation and geopolitical instability.

### The Impact of Gas Prices and Geopolitics
A primary driver of consumer unease is the volatility of energy prices, heavily influenced by the ongoing military conflict in the Middle East and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz. Hsu notes that consumers are not reacting to geopolitical headlines directly but are highly sensitive to the resulting fluctuations in gas prices. While sentiment improved in June and July due to temporary relief, recent spikes in global energy prices threaten to reverse this progress. Consumers remain focused on immediate costs, making sustained sentiment improvement dependent on stable and lower gas prices.

### AI: A Mixed Bag for Consumer Confidence
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has emerged as a significant topic in consumer discussions, with mentions rising from 1.6% two years ago to 11%. However, the sentiment surrounding AI is largely negative on net. While some consumers acknowledge potential productivity gains and stock market boosts, a larger group expresses concern over job losses and layoffs. Additionally, there is growing anxiety about the negative externalities of AI infrastructure, such as data centers driving up local electricity prices and environmental concerns.

### The Wealth Divide: Stocks vs. Real Life
There is a stark divergence between the experiences of high-wealth and low-wealth consumers. High-wealth individuals, who hold significant stock portfolios, feel more optimistic due to record-high market levels. In contrast, low-wealth consumers, who do not own stocks, have not benefited from market gains and remain pessimistic. Hsu emphasizes that the stock market's performance, driven largely by a few AI-focused firms, does not reflect the broader economic reality faced by the majority of Americans. This disconnect suggests that financial conditions at the top are not trickling down to improve general consumer confidence.

### Inflationary Psychology and Future Expectations
Inflation remains the number one factor weighing on consumers, with high prices cited as the primary concern in open-ended survey responses. The Survey of Consumers tracks "inflationary psychology," where consumers accelerate big-ticket purchases due to fears of future price hikes. This behavior, seen recently regarding tariffs, can become a self-fulfilling prophecy, driving up inflation. Currently, consumers expect inflation to remain elevated in both the short and long term, a trend that policymakers are closely monitoring.

### Restoring Confidence Requires Stability
To restore consumer confidence, Hsu argues that the economy needs sustained disinflation, not just temporary dips. Consumers do not want deflation, which signals economic distress, but they desperately desire price stability. Until there is resolution in geopolitical conflicts and a clear, sustained downward trajectory in inflation, sentiment is unlikely to return to pre-pandemic levels. The Federal Reserve's dual mandate of price stability and maximum employment remains crucial, but for now, the persistence of high prices continues to undermine the American consumer's outlook.
