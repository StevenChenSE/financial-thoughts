When analyzing the current surge in gold prices, it’s essential to recognize that the bull market is being driven by three distinct forces—each emerging at different times and from different parts of the world. According to market observer Kai, the framework for understanding this rally begins with a fundamental, often overlooked reality: **the majority of physical gold demand comes from emerging markets.**

“We in the western world tend to forget that,” Kai notes. “Just travel to China, to India, to Dubai—although that’s a little more complicated now—and talk with investors about their view on gold. This is a completely different attitude. For them, it’s normal to own gold at significantly higher percentages compared to the western world.”

This cultural and financial inclination forms what Kai calls the **secular trend**—a long-term, structural shift driven by steady demand in Asia and the Middle East.

The second major driver emerged sharply in 2022, following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and the subsequent sanctions. “Central bank gold demand kicked in,” Kai explains, highlighting a strategic move toward diversification, particularly by institutions in emerging economies. This shift has been substantial: **three years in a row of net purchases exceeding 1,000 tons**, with last year alone reaching **864 tons**—an all-time high in value terms.

“This is the second part of this bull market,” Kai says, “diversification from central banks, also primarily in emerging markets.”

The third and most recent force has only begun to gain momentum. “Basically in 2024 [and the] beginning of 2025, western financial investors started allocating some capital into gold,” observes Kai. “They’ve been the Johnny-come-lately. They only recently came in, and I think this is going to be the driver for the next couple of quarters.”

Together, these three layers—**emerging market private demand, central bank accumulation, and late-arriving Western institutional investment**—create a powerful, multi-stage bull market. While Kai believes the secular trend remains intact, he cautions that the inclusion of Western investors may introduce greater volatility compared to the earlier, steadier phases of the rally.

In summary, gold’s rise isn’t the story of one region or one catalyst, but a sequential global movement—one that began in the East, was amplified by official institutions, and is now being accelerated by Western capital playing catch-up.
