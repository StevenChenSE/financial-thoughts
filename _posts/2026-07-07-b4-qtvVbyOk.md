Dave Collum, the outspoken organic chemistry professor at Cornell University, returned to Capital Cosm for another sprawling macro conversation. What began as a discussion about insider access and security clearances quickly evolved into a sweeping critique of the AI trade, Federal Reserve stewardship, and the structural fragility of a 45-year bull market now sitting at roughly 200% over its historical average valuation.

## The Ellsberg Lesson and Tulsi's Reveal

The conversation opened with a quote Collum shared with Tucker Carlson — a remark originally made by Daniel Ellsberg to Henry Kissinger upon Kissinger receiving top security clearance. Roughly: *You will see what's really going on. At first you'll be elated, then euphoric, then you'll realize the three-star generals briefing you don't actually know what's happening — because they don't have your clearance.*

Collum noted that Tulsi Gabbard echoed similar sentiments, suggesting she was "flabbergasted" at what she could see once cleared. He framed this as a warning that much of the geopolitical theater visible to the public is just the tip of the iceberg — what Collum called "an emergent collapse or a control burn" engineered behind the curtain. The follow-on reporting on Gabbard's cult background, he suggested, was a deflection from the substance of what she actually saw.

## The Fed's Long Con

Collum laid out a thesis he has refined over many years: each Federal Reserve chair appears to have been "assigned a job" that fits the broader economic agenda.

- **Paul Volcker** engineered a controlled recession to set the stage for the Reagan-era boom.
- **Alan Greenspan**, the so-called Ayn Rand acolyte, ran an unregulated 45-year bull market in assets.
- **Ben Bernanke**, conveniently the world's foremost expert on the Great Depression, was installed just before the Great Financial Crisis to print his way out.
- **Janet Yellen** served as a placeholder.
- **Jerome Powell** raised rates higher than expected — buying time for a coming reset.
- **Kevin Warsh**, Powell's successor, is, in Collum's view, the next Volcker: a 15-year hawk and QE critic placed there to "shake the etch-a-sketch" and inflict the pain required to reset the banking system.

Collum dismissed the expectation that Trump would simply install a "money printer." Trump, he argued, has no incentive to crash the markets before the midterms — but the structural reset, not presidential preference, determines the outcome.

## The Market Resilience Paradox

For a decade, serious analysts have called for collapse. Markets have shrugged off every warning, delivering roughly 5–6% annualized real returns (or 11–12% nominal) through 2025. Collum acknowledged this stubborn resilience but warned that the muscle memory is misleading: no one currently active at Goldman or the major desks was trading in 1981.

Meanwhile, the S&P 500 trades at approximately 42× earnings — a 2.5% earnings yield before inflation, taxes, and fees. In his telling, that's a starvation-level return for retiring boomers who will have no choice but to hold.

## The AI Bubble and the China Factor

The bulk of the conversation centered on the AI trade, which Collum considers "a total disaster" for several reasons.

**Valuation.** AI-related companies now constitute roughly half of the stock market's value. Collum paraphrased Buffett's observation that an early-20th-century investor who saw cars and planes coming would have "shot the guys who invented them" — both technologies destroyed investor capital even as they transformed daily life. Collum made the same argument about early electricity companies, which peaked as investments in 1911, long before electricity became ubiquitous.

**Competition.** China keeps releasing cheap AI models — DeepSeek and others — undercutting American AI companies. The response from U.S. firms has been to release features they had previously withheld, either due to safety concerns or competitive positioning. This amounts to a national security concern, in Collum's framing: American AI is exposed.

**Capacity overhang.** Collum cited commentator Ed Zitron extensively. AI data centers, many under construction or never to be completed, sit alongside stockpiled chips that become obsolete in roughly five years. Unlike railroads or interstate highways — assets that deliver returns for a century — AI servers depreciate into obsolescence on a short cycle. One trillion dollars will need to be plowed back in every few years just to maintain capacity.

**Energy costs.** Collum noted that a single Indiana data center consumes the equivalent electricity of a million homes. At a plausible $4,000 annual utility bill per household, that implies $4 billion per year in revenue requirements per facility — before the chip depreciation and capital costs are even counted.

**Not GDP.** Collum's key conceptual point: building barns and buying tractors before planting a single crop is not economic output. It is, in his phrasing, "a gigantic tax" — a self-administered blood transfusion that bleeds capital in the process of transit. Healthcare expenditure on an aging boomer cohort is similarly an illusion of GDP; a fleet of 20-year-old rental cars perpetually in the shop is a tax, not output.

**Lock-in.** Agentic AI services, Collum argued, trap customers in the same way subscription services trap inattentive consumers. Companies that hand their workflows and intellectual property to Anthropic or OpenAI cannot easily migrate. If they stop paying, everything they've built is gone. Combined with the legal and ethical creep of "flooding the zone with nonsense" online — a strategy Cas Sunstein openly discussed in Obama-era academic papers — the digital world looks less like an open frontier and more like a managed landscape.

## Crypto and Gold

On crypto, Collum described himself as "the most podcasted non-coin no-coiner on the planet." While sympathetic to the spirit, he is unconvinced. His darkest interpretation: Bitcoin was released to let the brightest minds build the on-ramps and off-ramps, and debug the infrastructure, so that central bank digital currencies could ultimately be deployed seamlessly. The libertarian community cheering Larry Fink's entry into crypto puzzled him — "a plague blanket" entering your neighborhood is rarely good news.

He was more bullish on physical gold and silver, noting that GLD may have been designed decades ago to absorb demand and cool the metal's price.

## Platinum: The Trade He Likes

Platinum was Collum's most concrete conviction. The numbers he worked through are stark:

- Roughly 2 million ounces of above-ground platinum remain readily available.
- Annual deficits have run around 600,000 to 1 million ounces for several years.
- Roughly 70% of supply originates in South Africa (a potential failed state) and 20% from Russia.

At current prices, an entire global supply of two million ounces would cost about $4 billion — small enough to fit, as he put it, in a three-car garage. With sustained consumption, the available stockpile could be exhausted in roughly two years.

He noted having bought platinum repeatedly, including eight purchases during one rally and a ninth one he wrote about publicly, in some cases slightly underwater. "I don't lose sleep over it," he said. Einhorn's advice — buy the asset directly, not a derivative — informed his approach.

## Energy and the Trump Era

Collum revealed he had loaded up on energy around 2022, including ExxonMobil, which he told Howard Linden was his "one trade." He reported a 500% gain on a prior energy position before Fidelity — running a university endowment — liquidated it as "too risky." His broader view on energy is bullish, but he expects the next leg down in markets to feel deflationary rather than inflationary.

He reserved some praise for Trump's limited Iran strike as "completely brilliant" — both sides evacuated, the nuclear question was shelved, and Israel was checked. But he argued Trump then erred by bombing Iran "in earnest," an outcome Trump "did not get elected" to deliver. Collum's geopolitical reading: Iran is not the medieval caricature portrayed in Western media, and he recommended viewers search "Streets of Tehran" on YouTube to see the city's actual character.

## Russia, NATO, and the Western Cold War

The sharpest portion of the exchange concerned Russia and Ukraine. Collum argued that NATO expansion into Ukraine was an existential red line that Putin communicated for twelve years. He placed 99% of the blame on NATO and neoconservatives like Victoria Nuland, accusing the West of effectively bombing Russia through proxy drone strikes deep inside Russian territory. He argued that Putin has been "playing with kids' gloves" only to restrain Russian hardliners, not out of weakness.

Collum concluded that Europe — not Trump — is the obstacle to resolving the conflict, and accused European elites of still fighting the Cold War.

## Summary

Collum's framework remains consistent across episodes: the post-1981 financial architecture is due for a hard reset, whether emergent or engineered. The Powell-to-Warsh handoff is the next Volcker-style inflection point. AI valuations have decoupled from cash-flow reality in a way reminiscent of the early automobile boom. Physical commodities — particularly platinum — are constrained by geology and politics. And the geopolitical narrative pushed to Western audiences, on Iran and Russia alike, bears little resemblance to what insiders with cleared access describe.

The implied trade: prepare for a control burn rather than another round of money printing, own scarce physical assets directly, and discount almost everything the cable news apparatus is selling.
