In a world where geopolitical conflict often sends commodity prices soaring, metals analyst Eric Jung offers a counterintuitive perspective. In a recent interview on *Capital*, Jung argued that the ongoing war in the Middle East might not trigger the sustained rally in gold, silver, and copper that many investors expect. Instead, he laid out a complex, multi-phase scenario where industrial disruption could eventually suppress demand for key metals like silver.

### The Unexpected Dynamics of a "Grinding War"

The conversation opened with host Danny noting the puzzling resilience of metals prices despite heightened war risks. Jung, who has detailed his views on his Substack, attributed this to the war becoming a protracted "grinding war of attrition" rather than a swift, decisive conflict.

He challenged a critical assumption held by many analysts: that the Strait of Hormuz would be completely closed, halting oil exports. "In reality," Jung observed, "we [see] that Iran is actually shipping a lot of oil to China and apparently they are now shipping stuff to Russia and also to India." Satellite imagery, he noted, supports that some oil flow continues.

This reality sets in motion a chain of second- and third-order effects that Jung breaks into distinct phases.

**Phase One: The Dollar Scramble**
With oil still flowing—but at a reduced margin—Jung’s "Phase One" is already underway. Major oil-importing nations are selling dollar-denominated assets, including gold, silver, and bonds, to secure U.S. dollars.
> "Sell US dollar assets that includes gold and silver, get US dollars, okay, and buy as much of the marginal oil denominated in US dollars that is... getting out of the Strait of Hormuz."

This explains the strong U.S. Dollar Index (DXY) and selling pressure on precious metals, as markets prioritize liquidating holdings for essential energy.

**Phase Two: A Shift to Gold and a Sulfuric Acid Squeeze**
If the stalemate continues with a partially open Strait, Jung envisions a "Phase Two." Gulf states, losing confidence in U.S. security guarantees, may begin diversifying their oil proceeds into physical gold.

Simultaneously, a less obvious crisis emerges: a shortage of sulfuric acid. Jung explained that most of the world's sulfuric acid is a byproduct of refining "sour crude" oil from the Middle East. Reduced oil flow means less sulfuric acid, a crucial agent for extracting base metals like copper and zinc from ore.
> "So if that... yield is cut in half it's possible that the silver production by non-primary silver producers... also reduce."

This supply crunch could initially boost prices for copper, zinc, and—because silver is often a byproduct of these mines—silver as well. Jung noted this could benefit primary silver producers.

**Phase Three: Industrial Collapse and Silver Demand Destruction**
The scenario turns bearish in "Phase Three," a speculative but dangerous possibility. If the Strait's throughput falls below a critical level (e.g., 10-20%), the sulfuric acid crisis becomes severe.
> "You have a severe supply crunch of the sulfuric acid and then your... base metals refinement is in serious trouble, right? Extraction and refinement in serious trouble."

Nations would burn through above-ground copper stockpiles for months. Once exhausted, industrial production for solar panels, EVs, and electronics could crash.
> "Then it might it might crash the price of silver because if you can't make solar panels or like EVs because you don't have the copper then you don't need the silver even if the silver is available."

Jung criticized analysts who jump from the initial war shock to this endpoint, missing the intermediate phases of potential price strength.

**Phase Four: A New Equilibrium**
Eventually, Jung believes supply chains would reconfigure, bypassing the Strait via rail or other routes. Russia would increase market share, and a new equilibrium would form. In this world, having lost credibility, the U.S. dollar would increasingly be swapped for physical gold and silver as a store of value, reigniting their long-term bull markets.

### Base Case vs. Bull Case

When asked for his base case, Jung pointed to the phased scenario already unfolding.
> "It's already happening. I mean, look at gold is at... $5,080 right now... Silver... down 3%... They managed to cap [oil]."

The bull case—a swift end to the war—would still damage U.S. credibility and lead to diversification into metals, but likely with less volatility. However, Jung finds this less likely given the current stalemate.

### Personal Strategy: Hold, Don't Trade

Given the extreme uncertainty and conflicting headlines, Jung’s personal strategy is straightforward: hold physical gold and silver. He likened short-term trading to "pure gambling."
> "I'm holding on to all my gold and silver... I think it's extremely difficult to trade this stuff."

For those under-allocated, he suggested dollar-cost averaging, as pinpointing a bottom is impossible. He concluded that the intricate web of oil flow, currency dynamics, and industrial chemistry makes the metals market far more complex than a simple "war = higher prices" equation.
