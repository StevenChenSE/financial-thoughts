## The Intersection of Energy Costs and Food Prices

The current surge in diesel prices, driven by geopolitical tensions in the Middle East, is creating a direct and immediate pressure on American grocery bills. With diesel prices up 61% to $5.64 per gallon, the cost of transporting food is escalating rapidly. Since diesel trucks move 83% of America's farm products, every increase in fuel cost has a ripple effect on the price of food at the retail level. This crisis is compounded by soaring fertilizer prices, which have made it impossible for seven in ten crop farmers to buy the necessary inputs for the 2026 season, leading to a six-year high in farm bankruptcies.

## The Mechanics of Food Transportation

According to Joel Salatin, co-owner of Polyface Farms in Virginia, the average morsel of food in America travels 1,500 miles from field to plate. Transportation fuel accounts for approximately 4% of the total calories of energy required to produce one calorie of food. It takes 15 calories of energy to put one calorie of food on a plate, with four of those calories coming directly from diesel fuel for transportation. As diesel prices rise, the question remains whether grocery stores will absorb these costs or pass them on to consumers. Salatin suggests that while grocery stores may initially try to absorb some costs, the competitive landscape is shifting. The rise of efficient door-to-door logistics is beginning to challenge the "stranglehold" of traditional supermarkets, potentially allowing small producers to compete more effectively by bypassing large distribution hubs.

## The Fragility of the Industrial Farming Model

The industrial food system is showing signs of fragility as it relies heavily on chemical fertilizers and petroleum. Salitan argues that the world does not need the volume of soybeans and corn currently produced; half of US soybeans are exported, half of corn goes into ethanol, and half of alfalfa is fed to racehorses in Japan and Saudi Arabia. This production model is energy-intensive and increasingly unprofitable. In contrast, biological farming systems, which use compost and integrated livestock, are more resilient. On Polyface Farms, fuel constitutes only 5% of total expenses, compared to the 50% average for conventional farms. This biological approach, which involves rotating animals and using compost, reduces the need for purchased inputs and maintains soil health, offering a model that is less vulnerable to energy price shocks.

## The Cattle Herd Crisis and Political Implications

The US cattle herd is at a 75-year low, a result of several compounding factors including the 2021 drought, the aging of the average cattle owner (who is about 65 years old), and a decade of low profitability. The current situation is further complicated by the Trump administration's decision to allow 300,000 metric tons of duty-free imported beef to enter the US starting September 1st. This move, which represents 2% of annual American consumption, is seen by many as a political ploy to lower prices before the midterm elections. Critics argue that importing below-market-value beef depresses domestic cattle prices, making it harder for American ranchers to rebuild their herds. Salitan notes that this imported beef is often the "poorest quality" product, and the move may ultimately drive consumers toward pork and chicken as they become concerned about the quality of imported beef.

## Pathways to Stability and Healthier Eating

To ensure price stability and return to more sustainable farming practices, Salitan advocates for a reduction in government involvement in agriculture. He suggests a phased elimination of subsidies, which currently prop up "bad farming" by protecting producers from the consequences of their decisions. Instead, he proposes allowing the market to work, which would encourage a shift toward more efficient, biological production methods. For consumers, the path to eating healthier despite rising prices involves developing domestic culinary arts, buying single-ingredient whole foods, and purchasing in season and in volume. By investing in freezers and canning, and by cooking from scratch, consumers can reduce their reliance on expensive convenience foods and support local producers who are adapting to the new economic realities. The goal is to move away from the "aircraft carrier" of industrial agriculture toward the "speedboat" of intentional, localized food production.
