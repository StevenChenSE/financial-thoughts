## Introduction

In a candid conversation on *Capital Calls*, host Danny sits down with veteran geologist and investor Rick Rule to dissect the complex economic landscape of mid-2026. With US Treasury yields at multi-year highs and global currencies in flux, Rule offers a contrarian perspective on inflation, the true cost of capital, and the structural vulnerabilities in the energy sector. This article summarizes Rule’s key insights on interest rates, currency diversification, and the imperative for long-term patience in resource investing.

## The Illusion of Positive Real Rates

Rule begins by addressing the surge in long-term interest rates, noting that the 30-year yield has hit 5.26% and the 10-year yield is at 4.73%, the highest levels since 2008. He argues that while the Federal Reserve controls short-term rates, the market—acting as "bond vigilantes"—is asserting control over long-term rates.

Crucially, Rule challenges the official narrative on inflation. He contends that the Consumer Price Index (CPI) fails to reflect the true cost of living. According to Rule, the purchasing power of savings, salaries, and pensions is deteriorating at a compounded rate of 8% to 10%. Consequently, an investor earning 5.7% on a 30-year bond is actually losing approximately 3% in real terms. This "real negative rate" environment has profound implications for capital-intensive industries like mining and oil and gas, where the cost of capital is a fundamental determinant of viability.

## The End of the Yen Carry Trade and Dollar Weakness

The discussion shifts to currency markets, specifically the recent drop in the US dollar against the Japanese yen. Rule explains that rising interest rates in Japan are ending the "yen carry trade," a strategy popular for four decades where investors borrowed cheap yen to invest in higher-yielding dollar assets. As this trade unwinds, surplus Japanese capital returns to Japan, strengthening the yen and weakening the dollar.

Rule draws parallels to the volatile currency period between 2000 and 2008, noting that commodity-centric currencies like the Canadian and Australian dollars often outperformed during such transitions. He advises US investors to diversify their currency holdings away from 100% dollar exposure, recommending gold as a critical hedge against the deterioration of purchasing power. He emphasizes that this is not about short-term speculation but about preserving wealth over the long term.

## Foreign Inflows and Market Valuations

Despite concerns about the dollar, Rule notes record foreign inflows into the US stock market, likely driven by enthusiasm for artificial intelligence and technology. However, he remains skeptical of valuations in this sector, admitting he lacks the expertise to value tech stocks like Nvidia. Instead, he focuses on absolute valuation in natural resources.

Rule highlights that small-cap US equities are not expensive relative to interest rates, but he remains indifferent to broad market signals like bond ETF (TLT) valuations. His investment philosophy ignores market timing, focusing instead on identifying undervalued assets. He notes that mid-tier and high-quality junior gold stocks are relatively cheap and poised for a merger and acquisition (M&A) boom over the next three years.

## The Looming Oil Shortage of 2029–2030

A central theme of the interview is Rule’s bullish long-term outlook on oil. He predicts a structural shortage of oil in 2029 or 2030, driven not by war but by deferred sustaining capital investments. The global oil industry is currently deferring over $1 billion a day in necessary maintenance and development to boost dividends and share buybacks.

While this strategy pleases shareholders in the short term, it cannibalizes future production. Rule argues that oil stocks of companies that continue to invest in sustaining capital are undervalued. He advises investors to look beyond near-term geopolitical noise and focus on companies maintaining production capacity, which will be richly rewarded when the supply crunch hits.

## Housing, Gold, and the Discipline of Saving

Rule uses charts of US single-family home prices priced in gold and relative to the M2 money supply to illustrate that housing is at real lows, despite nominal highs. This suggests that affordability issues stem from currency devaluation rather than asset overpricing. He notes that gold’s market share in US portfolios is less than 0.5%, implying significant room for growth as investors seek to preserve purchasing power.

Finally, Rule addresses the psychological barriers to wealth creation. He critiques the modern tendency toward short-term trading, noting that the average stock holding period has dropped to under six months. He argues that true wealth requires a long-term horizon, distinguishing between "living rich" (consuming) and "becoming rich" (saving and investing). He encourages listeners to adopt the discipline of a saver, recognizing that generating more utility than one consumes is the foundation of capital accumulation.

Rick Rule’s analysis for 2026 highlights a world of real negative interest rates, a weakening dollar, and an impending structural oil shortage.
