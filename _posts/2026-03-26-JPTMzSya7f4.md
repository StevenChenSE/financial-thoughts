**By Gary Bow, Metals and Miners**

In a stark warning on *Metals and Miners*, economic strategist Luke Groman presented a chilling timeline: the global economy has roughly three to four weeks to resolve the closure of the Strait of Hormuz before facing a crisis "worse than anyone alive has seen."

Groman, with over three decades of experience analyzing markets through wars and cycles, distilled the complex Iran conflict into one essential metric. "The Strait of Hormuz is the only thing that matters," he stated. "If it's open, then we can talk about all the other stuff... If it's closed in three, four weeks time, I recommend they start stocking up on essentials."

### The Ticking Clock to Catastrophe

The conversation quickly centered on this critical deadline. Groman argued that the longer the vital waterway—through which about 20% of the world's oil passes—remains closed, the more dire the consequences.

"If Hormuz is still closed in 2 to 3 weeks, 4 weeks... it's going to be the worst economic crisis in anybody alive's career," Groman said. "It will be the worst of COVID and the worst of the Great Financial Crisis put together."

He elaborated that the global economy would begin to converge "towards Iran's economy," a trajectory he described as "not a good thing." This scenario would unleash cascading failures: stagflation, gas shortages in the West, and a sovereign debt market crisis, glimpses of which were seen in volatile bond movements last week.

### Baked-In Recession vs. Unprecedented Collapse

Even if the strait reopens within the narrow window, Groman believes a recession is likely baked into the cake. He pointed to immediate supply chain ruptures, particularly in plastics and fertilizers, which will drive price increases. Using the 2022 period as a reference, he noted that many recession indicators flashed red, despite official GDP avoiding two consecutive negative quarters.

"Your [tax] receipts will fall, employment will fall, ISM will fall, profits will fall," he predicted for the current situation.

However, a closure extending beyond a few weeks would trigger something far more severe. Groman highlighted "an astonishing amount of complacency" about how supply chains would break down "in a cascading manner." He gave specific examples:
*   40% of the world's sulfur supply, critical for copper mining, would be disrupted.
*   The world's largest aluminum smelter in Bahrain is winding down operations.
*   This would cripple the ability to build AI infrastructure and modernize the electrical grid.

### Geopolitical Miscalculation and Iran's Strategy

Groman was critical of the U.S. strategic approach, suggesting a profound miscalculation. "We were in a bad enough position. We could not afford to have higher oil. The Treasury market could not afford it," he argued. The plan to front-load Treasury issuance and then cut rates is now colliding with rising short-term rates driven by war risk.

He framed Iran's strategy as brutally simple: send oil to $120 per barrel to collapse the Western bond market and economy. A former IRGC commander publicly outlined this plan, noting that Western logistics indicated preparation for only a short war.

"Why wrestle a pig? You get dirty and the pig gets happy," Groman said, arguing that a wider regional war would only accelerate this outcome, potentially sending oil to $140-$160.

### A Stalled Negotiation and Shifting Alliances

A significant obstacle to resolution, according to Groman, is Iran's dwindling incentive to negotiate. He pointed to a pattern of Iranian negotiators being assassinated, creating a climate of bad faith. Furthermore, with time on their side, the Iranian calculus is clear: "If they just don't negotiate for two to three more weeks, they're going to get their 'collapse the economy' outcome."

The idea of a unified Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) front forcefully reopening the strait was met with skepticism. Groman noted that Iranian missile strikes on host countries have made them reluctant to support U.S. operations. He also cited reports that the U.S. and Israel are "largely out of air interceptors," diminishing defensive capabilities.

### The BRICS and Dollarization Factor

Groman acknowledged that the BRICS coalition's de-dollarization efforts, particularly selling oil in yuan, were likely a factor in escalating tensions. The U.S. has engaged in proxy conflicts or sanctions with major yuan-oil sellers: Russia, Venezuela, and Iran.

However, he questioned the war's effectiveness in curbing this trend, noting reports that Iran is negotiating with eight non-Middle Eastern countries to sell oil in yuan. "I would say the war has done the exact opposite if that was what we were trying to do," he concluded.

### A "No-Win" Military Situation

On the military feasibility of forcibly reopening the strait, Groman relayed analysis from former Navy personnel. The geography makes it a deadly choke point. An assault would be "a knife fight in a phone booth" akin to the opening scene of *Saving Private Ryan*, with estimated casualties of 80-90%.

He emphasized the broader strategic dilemma: "We can't re-industrialize without China, we can't reshore without China, we can't go to war without China." Yet, the conflict implicitly targets a China-Russia-Iran axis. "I don't think we can overpower two of them, let alone three of them."

### The Grim Outlook

Groman's assessment was bleak. The best-case scenario, in his view, is now a "strategic loss like Suez in 1956." The reliance on Chinese supply chains for everything from military supplies to corporate America's goods means a protracted conflict would devastate the S&P 500.

"**This is a no-win situation**," he stated bluntly. "We never should have done this. This was the dumbest thing to have done because we can't get out."

The ultimate takeaway is a race against a clock measured in weeks. All other economic and market discussions are secondary to the status of a narrow waterway in the Middle East. If it remains closed, the world will face an economic shock beyond modern memory. If it opens, a severe recession may still be the cost of the conflict. The countdown has begun.
