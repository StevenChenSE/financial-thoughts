## Rising Interest Rates and the Japanese Yen Crisis

We are currently entering what historians may look back on as the beginning of a currency debt spiral. This phenomenon is marked by soaring interest costs in the United States and Japan, a situation neither government can easily control. A primary indicator of this instability is the Japanese yen. Recent market interventions by the Bank of Japan (BOJ) and the U.S. Treasury have caused temporary spikes in the yen's value, but these efforts are reaching diminishing returns.

The root cause is Japan’s status as the most indebted government per capita in history. For decades, this debt was manageable due to near-zero interest rates. However, as Japanese bond yields rise—with the 10-year and 30-year yields showing unrelenting upward trends—the net interest cost on this debt is becoming debilitating. This weakens the yen and undermines the once-profitable yen carry trade, where investors borrowed cheap yen to invest in higher-yielding assets like U.S. or Brazilian debt. With Japanese interest rates rising into the 2% to 3% range, this trade is becoming less viable, potentially leading to financial crises as "bad paper" based on these trades begins to fail. Governments are intervening to delay this fallout, particularly with U.S. midterm elections and a new Japanese government seeking stability, but the fundamentals point to further currency weakness.

Political cycles often influence the timing of financial turmoil. Historically, significant economic problems are admitted and turmoil is allowed to play out after elections, rather than before. With the U.S. midterms approaching, it is rational to expect that any major financial instability or "bad news" may be delayed until after November. This pattern suggests that the first six months following the midterms could see increased volatility as the political landscape shifts toward the next presidential campaign.
