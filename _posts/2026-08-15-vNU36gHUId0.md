## Subtitle: Peter Krauth on the shift from physical silver to mining equities and the macroeconomic forces driving the market

## Introduction

In a recent discussion on the *Capitalist Exploits* podcast, silver expert Peter Krauth analyzed the current state of the precious metals market. While silver prices have recently bottomed and are showing signs of recovery, Krauth argues that the most significant investment opportunity is no longer in the physical metal itself, but in silver mining stocks. He explains that the market is entering a phase where the broader investing public will begin to price in the new, higher silver prices, creating a "lag" that benefits equities.

## The Silver Price: A Textbook Summer Bottom

Krauth notes that while gold has already made a structural breakout from a descending triangle, silver was still consolidating within a similar pattern. However, the recent price action has been "textbook perfect." Silver historically tends to bottom in mid-summer, and this year it hit a low of $55 in mid-July.

Krauth emphasizes the importance of perspective: while some critics complain about the price at $65, the metal was at $37 just one year ago, representing an 80% gain. The previous high of $120 in January was a "blow-off top," leading to a correction of over 50%. However, the recent low of $55 is significant because it sits above the 45-year peak of $50. This structural shift indicates a very healthy and bullish medium-to-long-term outlook.

## The Shift to Silver Stocks

A key insight from Krauth is the changing nature of the opportunity. For the last couple of years, the silver price outperformed silver stocks. However, the **SIL to Silver Price Ratio** (comparing the iShares Silver Trust ETF to the spot price) recently broke out of a long consolidation range.

This breakout indicates that silver stocks are now moving higher faster than the silver price itself, providing "positive leverage." Krauth states that the broader market has not yet fully revalued silver stocks to reflect the new, higher price paradigm. He predicts that over the next two to four quarters, analysts will update their models with higher average silver prices, leading to significant upward revisions in price targets and share performance.

## Structural Deficits and Inventory Drawdowns

Krauth explains the mechanics behind the "structural supply deficit," which is projected to be around 46 million ounces in 2026. For several years, the market was able to absorb these deficits by drawing down "above-ground" inventories held on exchanges like the COMEX, LBMA, and Shanghai.

By early 2024, it became clear that these secondary inventories were being depleted at a rate of 60-80%. Once these buffers are exhausted, the market can no longer rely on existing stock; it must be met by new mining production. This transition has created a "pent-up demand" that is now putting sustained upward pressure on prices.

## The Byproduct Paradox

A unique dynamic in the silver market is that 75% of mined silver is a byproduct of other mining operations (gold, copper, lead, or zinc). These companies are not driven by the silver price; they mine for their primary product. Consequently, higher silver prices do not necessarily lead to an immediate increase in supply from these sources, as they cannot easily change their mine plans.

This "byproduct paradox" means that even as prices rise, supply remains inelastic. Furthermore, high-cost primary silver miners may choose to mine lower-grade ore to maximize profits per ounce, potentially reducing the total volume of silver delivered to the market. This exacerbates the shortage.

## Macroeconomic Tailwinds: The Fed and Inflation

Krauth discusses the macroeconomic environment, noting that the market has priced in concerns about Federal Reserve rate hikes. He suggests the Fed is more likely to cut rates, particularly given the $40 trillion national debt and the need to keep Treasury yields manageable.

A controversial point raised is the potential shift in how the Fed measures inflation. Krauth notes that the new Fed chair has expressed a preference for "trimmed averages" over the Personal Consumption Expenditure (PCE) index. Interestingly, trimmed averages have historically shown inflation closer to the 2% target. This could provide the Fed with the "window dressing" needed to justify cutting rates even if consumer-facing inflation (food and energy) remains high. This environment of financial repression—where interest rates are kept low despite high inflation—is historically a "bullish" setup for precious metals.

## Conclusion

Peter Krauth concludes that the "door is closing" on the easy gains in physical silver, but the door is swinging wide open for silver stocks. With a confirmed structural deficit, depleted inventories, and a macro environment that favors rate cuts, silver mining equities are poised to outperform. For long-term investors, the revaluation of these companies based on higher silver prices is the next major move to watch.
