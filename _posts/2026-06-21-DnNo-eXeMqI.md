## Simon Hunt on the MOU, Petrodollar Politics, and the Coming Economic Collapse

The world is watching as another Memorandum of Understanding with Iran approaches—yet experts are warning that this could be nothing more than an elaborate smokescreen. In a recent interview on Capital Cos, geopolitical strategist Simon Hunt laid out why the proposed deal represents not a path to peace, but a dangerous game of positioning that could end in another major regional conflict.

### What Is the MOU Actually Saying?

An MOU, as Hunt pointed out during the discussion, carries no legal binding whatsoever. "An MOU has no legal binding whatsoever," he explained. This distinction matters enormously when examining Iran's 14 conditions for the agreement—conditions that have not yet been made public.

The most contentious condition appears to be Iran's demand that Lebanon and Gaza be recognized as part of its sphere of influence, making any attack or occupation of these territories an infringement of the MOU. This puts Washington in an impossible position, particularly given Israel's response.

Israel's Defense Minister has been unambiguous: "We are not party to the agreement and we will continue to occupy and if necessary bomb both Gaza and Lebanon." The fundamental question becomes whether Trump can hold back Israel—and whether Netanyahu and his allies in America's Zionist lobby have enough power to control the administration itself.

### The Energy Equation Behind US Policy

Hunt revealed a critical framework for understanding American policy in the region. "His policy has been if we control global energy, we control the world," Hunt explained. This explains the sequence of moves: Venezuela first, more actions in Latin America, and then the larger objective.

"If we defeat Iran, then all the Arab countries will come under our umbrella, as they will back whoever is the winner," Hunt added. This strategic calculus means that accepting Iran's conditions would be "an admission of defeat"—something Washington may find politically impossible to swallow.

The petrodollar remains central to this equation. "You lose the petrodollar, you lose the dollar. And that's a huge loss to America," Hunt warned. The alternative—the CNY petro—represents everything Washington is fighting to prevent.

### Israel, Syria, and the Outsourcing Question

A curious development emerged when Trump suggested outsourcing the handling of Hezbollah to Jolani's Syria. "Israel will withdraw from Lebanon and move into Syria," Hunt predicted. "Hezbollah is part of Iran. Same thing."

This arrangement raises questions about Turkey, which backed Jolani and maintains interests in Syria. "Turkey is supposedly next on the list after Iran is dealt with," Hunt noted. Yet simultaneously, "Turkey's relations with Israel are not friendly. And you also have Turkey now moving into the Iranian camp."

This apparent contradiction points to something larger: a new architecture for the Middle East where "Iran is the central hub" and Turkey, Egypt, and Pakistan may form what Hunt described as a "Middle East NATO"—a coalition that would fundamentally reshape regional power dynamics.

### The UAE's Quiet Pivot

Perhaps most telling is the reported behavior of the UAE and Abu Dhabi. "The UAE and Abu Dhabi led by Abu Dhabi are quietly ditching their association with Israel," Hunt reported from Dubai. A delegation of their leaders reportedly visited Tehran on June 9th with a simple message: "X, Y, and Z billion dollars. Please don't bomb us."

The earlier period saw debt levels nowhere near current heights. Today, with inflation potentially reaching 10-15% and US 10-year bonds yielding 10-15%, the crash could be catastrophic. "If we get an immediate escalation of oil prices, the credit system would not be able to be held together. We would have the crash—equity markets falling by 25% plus."

### Military and Diplomatic Shifts

Russia and China have supplied Iran with advanced missile and radar defense systems. "You can see the impact of that on the accuracy of the strikes that they made, particularly in Jordan, Kuwait, and Bahrain," Hunt noted.

The diplomatic choreography reveals the emerging multipolar alignment: Putin in Beijing, followed by the Iranian foreign minister, then Pakistan's prime minister—setting "the game plan" in Beijing. Shortly after, Xi visited North Korea for the first time in nine years, "putting together the players in the multipolar world."

### The Nuclear Question

Perhaps most alarming: Hunt believes Iran will have a nuclear weapon by 2027 or 2028. "Regardless of where they got it from—places like North Korea," he noted, the strategic implications are profound. As North Korea demonstrates, the nuke is a deterrent. "Why is North Korea able to carry on? Because everybody fears that they've got the nuke."

### Economic Timeline

Hunt's economic predictions paint a grim picture:

- **End of 2026**: American economy enters recession, spreading across the Western world
- **Fourth quarter 2026**: Relief rally in equities
- **Through 2027**: Markets fall, potentially hitting around 4,500 on the S&P
- **2028**: With November elections approaching, the Fed begins "liquidifying the system," bringing lower interest rates

The appointment of Kevin Warsh signals the Fed's intent. "They will encourage a recession to bring bond yields down," Hunt explained. A 3% or lower 10-year yield would allow Treasury to refinance at least $10 trillion in debt.

### The Bottom Line

"Whether war restarts in the immediate future, like the next 3 months, or whether it's 12 months, a second war is coming," Hunt concluded. An Iranian official has confirmed they're preparing for "the next round."

The MOU, in Hunt's analysis, is merely "kickoff"—the first move in what will be a prolonged series of escalations. "Changes always end with war," he observed. "We're only using soccer jargon. We then have semi-finals sometime between now and the middle of 2027. And then we have the finals sometime between 2028 and 2030."

What's at stake is nothing less than the transition from a unipolar world dominated by American power to a multipolar order organized around new alliances and new currencies. And as history demonstrates, such transitions rarely occur without conflict.
