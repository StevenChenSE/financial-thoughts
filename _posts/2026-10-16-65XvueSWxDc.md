## The Mechanics of Gold Revaluation

A central theme of the conversation was the possibility of not just one, but multiple gold revaluations. Bill Holter introduced the concept of "competitive revaluation." In this scenario, one nation could revalue its gold reserves to, say, $10,000 per ounce, while another nation, such as China, could respond by revaluing theirs to $20,000.

Holter explained that when gold is revalued higher, it creates more collateral for the banking system. This collateral can be borrowed against to issue new loans, effectively increasing the money supply. This process is described as "re-inflation" or "re-flation," designed to counteract the deflationary pressures caused by debt defaults. Holter cited the 1934 revaluation of gold by the US government, which increased the price from $20.67 to $35.00 per ounce, as a historical precedent for using gold revaluation to combat deflation.

The question remains: how high will gold be revalued? To cover the entire US national debt, gold would theoretically need to be valued at over $160,000 per ounce. However, Holter and Morgan debated whether such a massive revaluation is necessary or if smaller, incremental adjustments are more likely. Morgan argued for a market-clearing approach where bad debts and zombie companies are allowed to fail, suggesting that a lower revaluation (e.g., $30,000) might suffice if the system undergoes a necessary purge.

The conversation also touched on the current state of the stock market, particularly the technology sector. This phenomenon relates to the concept of the "Invisible Crash," a term from Jim Dean’s book. It describes a situation where stock market gains are illusory when measured against the rising price of gold. When gold rises faster than stocks, the real value of stock portfolios declines, even if their nominal dollar value increases.
