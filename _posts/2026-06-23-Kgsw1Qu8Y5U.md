# Zeberg: .com Appetizer, 2008 Main Course Looms

## Macro Strategist Henrik Zeberg Predicts a Final Melt-Up, NASDAQ Target of 34,000, and a Q3 Top Before a Deflationary Crash

In a recent interview with Maggie Lake, Henrik Zeberg, Macro Strategist at Swiss Block and founder of the Zeberg Letter, delivered a stark warning regarding the current state of global markets. Zeberg describes the present environment as a convergence of a weak economy, the largest IPOs, and the largest bubble in history. He draws a direct parallel to past financial crises, suggesting that the dot-com crash was merely the "appetizer," while the 2008-2009 financial crisis represents the "main course" that investors should now fear. Despite these warnings, Zeberg predicts a significant "final melt-up" in U.S. equities before the inevitable downturn, driven by psychological factors and capital rotation.

Zeberg emphasizes that market timing is notoriously difficult, noting that valuations can remain extreme long after fundamental realities deteriorate. He points to technical indicators and liquidity conditions that suggest the market has not yet peaked. However, he warns that the current rally is fueled by "pure air" rather than value, and the subsequent collapse could trigger a massive deflationary factor. This article explores Zeberg's analysis of market history, his technical projections for the NASDAQ, the risks posed by AI and private credit, and the characteristics of the final stages of a bubble.

### The Historical Parallel: Appetizer vs. Main Course

Zeberg argues that investors are failing to learn from history, repeating the mistakes of previous decades. He posits that the current tech bubble mirrors the dot-com era but with even more extreme valuations. According to Zeberg, the dot-com crash will be the "appetizer," and the 2008-2009 crisis will be the "main course." This comparison highlights the combination of a massive equity bubble with underlying structural weaknesses in the economy.

He points to valuation metrics such as the CAPE (Shiller P/E) and standard P/E levels to support his view. Zeberg notes that the valuation of "basex" compared to the Yahoo era shows we are dealing with extremes typically seen at the end of a bull market, not the beginning. While the current cycle lacks a real estate bubble of the same magnitude as 2008, Zeberg warns that household debt levels and housing affordability are critical issues. He observes that people can barely afford their homes, and when the economy slows and businesses begin to lay off workers, the fragility of the balance sheets will be exposed.

### Market Projections and Technical Indicators

Despite the bearish long-term outlook, Zeberg maintains a bullish stance on the short-term trajectory, predicting a final melt-up. He forecasts that the NASDAQ could see another "at least 15%" gain from current levels, targeting a range of 33,000 to 34,000. Zeberg believes the market top will likely occur in Q3 of this year.

His projection is supported by technical analysis. Zeberg highlights a fractal pattern in the market data that closely resembles the development seen in the NASDAQ and S&P from 1997 to 2000. He also references "Fib master levels" that align with his 33,000–34,000 target. Furthermore, Zeberg utilizes a "risk gauge" based on the ratio of the S&P versus the 10-year bond. He explains that this ratio typically sees two to three months of declines before a market top forms. Since April and May showed strong rallies in this ratio, Zeberg concludes that the liquidity situation has not deteriorated enough to signal a top yet, though this can change rapidly.

### AI, Productivity, and Labor Market Risks

A significant portion of the discussion focused on the impact of Artificial Intelligence (AI) on the labor market. Zeberg challenges the narrative that AI will immediately generate jobs. He argues that while AI may create jobs over a five-to-10-year horizon, the short-term net effect of increased productivity is likely to be a reduction in headcount.

Zeberg points to the "quarter-to-quarter shareholder environment" as a catalyst for this trend. Companies are under pressure to cut costs and boost profits, and the easiest way to achieve this is by reducing human capital. He warns that the transition through AI will likely result in a short-term emphasis on layoffs before the nature of new jobs becomes clear. This dynamic exacerbates the risks to the economy, as rising unemployment could occur even while the economy appears to be peaking with relatively low official unemployment numbers.

### Anatomy of the Final Melt-Up

Zeberg describes the characteristics of the final phase of a bubble, noting that it is often the "last 20% which makes people crazy," a sentiment he attributes to Ray Dalio. This phase is driven by human psychology, where investors continue to buy and accept more leverage until a breaking point is reached.

Key features of this melt-up include capital rotation. Zeberg expects capital to flow from abroad into the U.S., particularly as markets like the Hang Seng and the KOSPI in Asia show signs of topping. Investors taking profits in these regions may rotate into the U.S., perceived as a safer haven. Internally, Zeberg anticipates a rotation within U.S. markets. Capital often moves first into "safe" bets like banks, pushing them higher and attracting attention. Subsequently, capital may rotate into sectors that have lagged, such as the software sector, as investors chase the AI narrative and seek new opportunities. This broadening of the rally can create a false sense of market health before the collapse.

### Black Swans and Deflationary Risks

The discussion concluded with a focus on systemic risks and potential "black swan" events. Zeberg highlights the private credit market as a source of significant stress, noting that there are stretches of risk globally that are not reflected in public markets. He cites the "Rand situation" as an example of a black swan event that risk assets largely ignored, demonstrating the disconnect between asset prices and underlying realities.

Zeberg warns that when the stock market eventually implodes, it will unleash a "huge deflationary factor." The combination of a bursting equity bubble, rising unemployment, and stressed credit markets could lead to a severe economic downturn. He advises investors to be cautious, noting that while speculators with small pots of money might profit from the melt-up if they are nimble, chasing this train is dangerous for long-term investors. The alignment of Zeberg's 20 indicators suggests the top is approaching, and the "party" will eventually stop, likely triggered by an unforeseen event or a rapid deterioration in liquidity.

### Summary

Henrik Zeberg's analysis paints a picture of a market in the final stages of a bubble, characterized by extreme valuations, a weak underlying economy, and significant structural risks. While he predicts a substantial melt-up with the NASDAQ reaching 33,000 to 34,000 and a top in Q3, he warns that this rally is unsustainable. The dot-com crash and the 2008 crisis serve as historical precedents for the severity of the upcoming correction. Investors are urged to recognize the dangers of the "appetizer" and "main course" dynamic, understand the short-term risks of AI-driven layoffs, and remain vigilant for black swan events in the private credit market. Zeberg's message is clear: the current gains are driven by psychology and rotation, not value, and the eventual deflationary impact of a market implosion will be profound.
