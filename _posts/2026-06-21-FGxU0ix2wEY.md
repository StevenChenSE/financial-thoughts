## Canada's most famous economist reveals what's really happening with inflation, jobs, and interest rates

The Federal Reserve is walking a tightrope, and according to David Rosenberg, president of Rosenberg Research and Associates, the markets might not be pricing in the real risks. In a recent appearance, Rosenberg challenged the conventional wisdom about inflation, labor markets, and what the Fed's next moves mean for investors.

The Iran Nuclear Deal: Market Overreaction?

When President Trump announced the framework for an Iran nuclear deal, markets erupted. The Dow jumped 600 points intraday, and the Nasdaq surged 3%. But Rosenberg urges caution.

"This is not a deal," Rosenberg said. "There's a memorandum of understanding—it's a deal to do a deal. And there's 60 days of negotiations."

Oil prices dropped a modest $4 following the announcement. While the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz would be significant, Rosenberg questions whether the market's euphoria is warranted. "What did you really get out of it today? A modest decline in bond yields. The stock market was just having an extension from what happened on Friday."

The bigger question, according to Rosenberg, is whether Trump will actually follow through. "We've been down this road before," he noted. "The big question is going to be—even if there's a deal, what's that deal going to look like?"

The Real Rate Problem: Why Bond Yields Matter

The 10-year Treasury yield sits near 4.5%, and Rosenberg believes this level is critical. "If you were to go back and test 5%, which is where we were at the peak in the fall of 2023, that's another 50 to 60 basis points away. That could cause some angst in the equity market."

The danger isn't necessarily inflation expectations. Rosenberg points to 10-year TIPS break-evens at just 2.3% and 5-year forwards at 2.2%—both stable despite persistent Fed rhetoric about inflationary risks. Instead, the problem is real interest rates.

"This has been a move in real rates," Rosenberg explained. "That could be partly reflecting fiscal policy, or the demands on capital coming from the AI boom. But it's been driven by real interest rates on inflation expectations."

In other words, even if inflation stays contained, higher real rates could still pressure markets. "You get another 50 basis points or higher in Treasury yields, and I think that's going to be a pretty big obstacle for the risk-on trade."

Inflation: The Story That Isn't

Despite headlines about hot CPI readings, Rosenberg sees a different picture. Headline CPI came in at 4.2%, but that was driven "mostly by gasoline and oil prices." Strip out energy and energy-adjacent items like airfares, delivery services, and utilities, and the story changes dramatically.

"When you adjust the CPI for those metrics, it's running at 1.8% year-over-year," Rosenberg said. "The full prices stabilize or come down, airfares and delivery services and utility costs in the CPI are going to come down too. So you adjust for those, and it's not data mining—it's analysis. Inflation's already running below target."

The critical question is whether energy shocks have seeped into wages. Rosenberg's answer is definitive: "No."

Real wages have contracted for three consecutive months. Meanwhile, nominal wage growth is decelerating—down to roughly 3% year-over-year from 4% a year ago. "How is the labor market tight and nominal wage growth slowing down? That is mutually inconsistent."

Breaking Down the Jobs Numbers

The May jobs report showed 172,000 non-farm payrolls added, beating expectations. But Rosenberg isn't buying it—or at least, isn't putting much weight on it.

"Do you really believe the economy added 172,000 jobs in May? I wonder how much numbers have been revised. 100% of the time in the past year, and about 85% of the time they've been revised lower."

The more telling indicator is the year-over-year trend. Even with May's bounce, employment growth is running at just plus 0.3% annually. The companion household survey shows minus 0.3%. "We have flat employment growth," Rosenberg stated. "The only reason why the unemployment hasn't ratcheted up is because we don't have any growth in the labor force."

The Fed, according to Rosenberg, is "anally focused on the U3 unemployment rate" and missing the broader picture. If the labor market were truly tight, wage growth would be accelerating—not decelerating.

Consumer Spending: A Tale of Two Economies

Here's where the real stress is hiding: the savings rate has collapsed from 5% to nearly 3%. Without that decline, real consumer spending would be negative 1%.

"You then get negative real consumer spending," Rosenberg explained. "The only reason why consumer spending hasn't been negative is because the high-end consumer is spending the equity wealth effect, the equity gains."

Meanwhile, lower-income households are "tapping credit cards like they never have before. That's a sign of stress." The high-end consumer continues spending beyond groceries, but the foundation is increasingly fragile.

Looking Ahead: What to Expect from the Fed

Kevin Worsh's first FOMC appearance is generating significant attention. Rosenberg expects Worsh to emphasize the PCE deflator's trim mean version, which runs around 2.3%—just slightly above target.

The tail risks, Rosenberg suggests, are asymmetric. "If things go wrong, that's the bigger risk—and things going right. The markets have already been moving in that direction ever since Trump made it overt that he wants to walk on that exit ramp."

The bigger risk isn't geopolitical escalation or inflation spiraling out of control. It's actually things going right—meaning the Fed might not deliver the rate cuts markets are hoping for, precisely because it remains focused on a labor market and inflation picture that don't match economic reality.

Key Takeaways

Rosenberg's analysis challenges several market assumptions. First, the Iran deal is still preliminary, and markets may be getting ahead of themselves. Second, while yields at 4.5% haven't caused major disruption, moving toward 5% could significantly pressure equities. Third, inflation is primarily energy-driven and likely temporary rather than persistent. Fourth, the labor market appears much weaker than headline unemployment figures suggest, with flat employment growth and decelerating wages. Finally, consumer spending is increasingly dependent on depleted savings and equity gains rather than fundamental income growth.

For investors, the message is clear: don't mistake market exuberance for rational pricing. The S&P 500 may be treated as a riskless asset by equity investors, but the underlying economic dynamics suggest otherwise.
