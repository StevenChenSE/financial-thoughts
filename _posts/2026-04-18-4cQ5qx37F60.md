## The Strait of Hormuz: A Modern-Day Chokepoint

The interview began with immediate focus on the naval blockade of Iran and its implications. Just 24-48 hours prior, negotiations in Islamabad had collapsed amid reports that a Chinese-bound ship had been turned back by U.S. forces in the Strait of Hormuz.

Dixon had predicted something similar to the 12-day war. What unfolded was considerably more.

"We're condensing down into one final war what may have taken two years," he explained. "The parameters of the game right now is that I don't think the market can have the Strait of Hormuz closed and blockaded for more than another month from now until we start to really see destruction in financial markets."

The implications extend far beyond military considerations. WTI crude oil, which had climbed to $105 earlier in the trading day, was already showing signs of retracement as markets absorbed the reality of the blockade. Dixon noted significant dislocations between Dubai, Oman, WTI, and Brent crude pricing—evidence that markets are struggling to find equilibrium.

"The game in my mind is bounded," Dixon said. "We're dealing with a bounded escalation cycle where both Iran and America understand the terms of engagement in terms of what it means for markets."

## The $115 Ceiling: Where Markets Break

Throughout the conflict, Dixon identified a critical threshold: whenever oil approaches $115 and the 10-year Treasury hits approximately 4.5% (with the 30-year approaching 5%), Trump introduces what Dixon calls "a new rabbit out of the bag"—some new escalation or de-escalation move designed to calm markets.

"Once you get oil prices above $150, we've already started to see CPI on Friday around three and a half percent," Dixon noted. "That doesn't really factor in anything from here yet. So we are back to an inflationary cycle."

The Trump administration had promised the opposite—a resolution to the Middle East situation that would bring rates down. Instead, they face a perfect storm:

- **Higher energy prices** when relief was expected
- **Private credit seizing up**, a major funding source for the AI and robotics trades
- **Gulf sovereign wealth funds facing financial stress**, limiting their investment capacity
- **AI infrastructure guzzling capital** at precisely the wrong moment

"We were meant to get lower rates. We're not getting those. We were meant to get AI productivity increases to tampen inflation. We're not getting those. So what's left? Lower oil and energy prices. I don't think we're getting those."

The only mechanism remaining for taming inflation, Dixon argues, is demand destruction—oil prices rising high enough to trigger recession and potentially depression.
