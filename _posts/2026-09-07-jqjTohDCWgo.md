## Introduction

In a recent interview, Jeff Currie, CEO and founder of Real Macro and former head of commodities research at Goldman Sachs, argued that global markets are facing a complex and multi-layered supply shock that extends far beyond the headlines of the Iran conflict. Currie, a veteran commodity strategist, posits that while the media and investors are fixated on crude oil prices, the true measure of economic stress is found in refined products—specifically diesel—and agricultural commodities.

Currie’s central thesis is that we are in the midst of a "shock and awe" scenario, where the cost of energy products and food is decoupling from the price of crude oil. He warns that the Federal Reserve is largely powerless against these supply-side dynamics, and that the current market structure is driven by a combination of geopolitical disruption, chronic underinvestment in refining capacity, and a global shift toward electrification.

## The Misunderstanding of Crude Oil vs. Refined Products

One of Currie’s most critical arguments is the distinction between crude oil and the products derived from it. He emphasizes that "nobody consumes oil." Unless one owns a refinery, the price of crude oil (WTI or Brent) is irrelevant to the average consumer. What matters is the price of gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel.

The market is currently witnessing a historic divergence: while crude oil has hovered around $94 to $97 per barrel, the price of diesel has surged to approximately $189 per barrel. This has resulted in the US diesel crack spread—the difference between the price of diesel and the price of crude oil—hitting a record high of over $106 per barrel.

Currie explains that this "crack" is the signal, while crude oil is the noise. The widening spread is driven by a severe shortage of refining capacity. With the loss of capacity in Russia, Ukraine, and the Middle East, the world is producing more crude than it can refine into usable fuel. This has created a situation where refineries are the bottleneck, not the extraction of crude.

## The Global Supply Shock: More Than Just the Strait of Hormuz

While the closure of the Strait of Hormuz has dominated headlines, Currie argues that the current supply shock is "extraordinarily large" and multifaceted. He breaks down the various factors contributing to the global energy and food crisis:

*   **The Strait of Hormuz:** Approximately 5 to 7 million barrels per day of oil are shut in due to the conflict between the US and Iran.
*   **The Red Sea:** An additional 2 million barrels per day of supply have been lost due to Houthi attacks on shipping.
*   **Refining Capacity:** The conflict in Ukraine has destroyed approximately 3 million barrels per day of refining capacity. Furthermore, China has cut another 1 million barrels per day, and 3 million more are trapped behind the Strait of Hormuz.
*   **Logistical Bottlenecks:** The Rhine River is too low to transport cargos, and the Panama Canal is operating at reduced draft levels.

Currie notes that the US government and media often focus on the volume of oil transiting the Strait of Hormuz (with estimates ranging from 9 to 17 million barrels per day), but he argues that the price action tells a different story. The premium on local crude against Brent and the record-high diesel crack spread indicate that the market is pricing in a much more severe shortage than the raw transit numbers suggest.

## The "Abundance Illusion" and the Role of the Strategic Petroleum Reserve

A key theme in Currie’s analysis is the "abundance illusion," a term originally coined by President Jimmy Carter in his 1977 "Sweater Speech." Currie argues that governments and media often downplay scarcity to avoid panic, but the reality of inventory levels tells a different story.

The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) is currently being drawn down at a rate of approximately 3 million barrels per week, down from previous highs of 7 to 10 million. Currie points out that the "magic number" for the SPR is between 270 and 300 million barrels. Below this level, the caverns begin to experience pressure problems and can collapse, making it difficult to extract oil.

Historically, countries draw down commercial inventories before touching strategic reserves. However, the US is doing this "backwards," relying on commercial inventories to stabilize the market while the SPR is depleted. Currie warns that this is a dangerous strategy that could lead to a sudden spike in prices if a further shock occurs.

## The Diesel-Grain Connection: Why Food Prices Are "Going to the Moon"

Currie argues that the link between energy and food is stronger than ever. Diesel is the primary input for agriculture, used in tractors, harvesters, and transportation. As diesel prices rise, the cost of producing and transporting grains increases.

This dynamic is currently being exacerbated by two factors:
1.  **Harvest Season Demand:** We are in the middle of the harvest season, and the demand for diesel is spiking at a time when prices are already high.
2.  **Biofuels:** Grains are increasingly being used for biofuels, further tightening the supply of food.

Currie points to the price of wheat, which has been "a line going straight up." The destruction of the Black Sea grain corridor by Ukraine and the impact of a "super El Niño" on US yields are creating a perfect storm for food inflation. He predicts that grains are the "real sneaker" (a surprise move) in the commodity complex, with prices likely to continue rising.

## The Fed's Dilemma and the Death of the Petrodollar

The Federal Reserve is facing a unique challenge: it cannot use interest rate hikes to combat a supply-driven inflation shock. Currie notes that Fed Governor Christopher Waller has suggested holding rates steady in September to "give disinflation a chance," but Currie argues that the Fed is powerless against a global supply shock.

The Fed's inability to control prices is further complicated by the "death of the petrodollar." Currie argues that the petrodollar system died in 2022 when the US and Europe sanctioned the Russian Central Bank. This led to a reversal of the dollar recycling mechanism, where oil revenues were previously used to buy US Treasuries. Instead, countries like Russia and China have begun buying gold, leading to a surge in gold prices.

This shift has implications for the US dollar. The strength of the DXY (US Dollar Index) is partly due to the lack of dollar recycling, but Currie warns that this is a fragile position. The US is facing a debt problem, with interest payments on the national debt rising from $1.1 trillion to $1.5 trillion. To manage this, the US may be forced to engage in "financial repression" (capping yields), which will further drive investors toward hard assets like gold.

## The New Oil: Copper and the Electrification of the World

While diesel and grains are the immediate drivers of inflation, Currie identifies copper as the "new oil." As the world moves toward electrification, driven by both environmental concerns and military security (e.g., the use of drones), the demand for copper will skyrocket.

Copper is essential for transformers, batteries, power cables, and data centers. Currie notes that the AI hyperscalers are spending over $800 billion on infrastructure, a significant portion of which goes into copper. He argues that copper is the strategically most important commodity in the coming decade, as the world electrifies for security reasons, not just environmental ones.

However, Currie also highlights the role of aluminum as a substitute for copper in certain applications, particularly in the US where land is abundant and cables can be hung overhead. In densely populated areas like China and Europe, where cables must be buried, copper is preferred because it does not ground out as easily as aluminum.

## Investment Strategy: Own the Hard Assets

Currie’s investment strategy is clear: own the hard assets. He recommends a diversified approach that includes:
*   **The Oil Complex:** Own the entire oil complex (crude, gasoline, diesel) rather than just crude oil. The "Municent 7" (the top seven oil producers) offer a high free cash flow yield (15.5%) compared to the Magnificent 7 tech stocks (2.7%).
*   **Grains:** Own the grain indices broadly, as the supply shock is likely to continue.
*   **Precious Metals:** Own a basket of gold, silver, platinum, and palladium. Gold is particularly attractive due to the risk of debasement and financial repression.

Currie advises against trying to "time" the market or pick individual commodities. Instead, he suggests owning the full commodity indices, as the trend is for all commodities to move higher in a super cycle. The rotation may change (e.g., from coffee to cocoa to diesel), but the overall direction is up.
