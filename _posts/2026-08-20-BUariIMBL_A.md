## Subtitle: Why the US Treasury’s aggressive move to cap long-end yields is reshaping the bond market and signaling a shift in economic strategy.

## Introduction

On Wednesday, August 19th, the US Treasury announced a significant shift in its debt management strategy, doubling its long-end Treasury buybacks to at least $4 billion per operation. Previously, the program was set at $2 billion. This move, executed by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, triggered an immediate and sharp reaction across global financial markets. The 30-year Treasury yield, which had recently hit a 19-year high, fell 9 points following the announcement. Simultaneously, risk assets surged: gold rose by 3%, Bitcoin climbed 5.5%, and the stock market moved into positive territory. The US Dollar Index (DXY) dropped by 80 basis points.

This intervention comes at a critical juncture, coinciding with the release of the Federal Reserve’s FOMC minutes. The timing has led many market participants to speculate about a coordinated effort between the Treasury and the Fed to manage interest rates and stabilize the economy.

## The Mechanics of the Intervention: Operation Twist?

The Treasury’s buyback program is not a traditional act of "money printing" or Quantitative Easing (QE), as the Treasury cannot create new reserves. Instead, it functions similarly to the historical "Operation Twist." Under this strategy, the government buys back long-duration debt (such as 20-year and 30-year bonds) and issues new short-duration debt (Treasury bills).

By exchanging long-duration risk for short-duration government liabilities, the Treasury effectively shifts the maturity profile of the outstanding debt. This action puts downward pressure on long-term yields while potentially allowing short-term rates to remain stable or rise slightly. Danielle DiMartino Booth, CEO of Qi Research, notes that while this is a controversial intervention, it is preferable to the Federal Reserve stepping in with QE. She argues that the Treasury taking a direct role is a more appropriate mechanism for managing the debt structure than leaving the onus on the Fed.

## Market Reaction and the "Bond Vigilantes"

The market's reaction was immediate. The 10-year Treasury yield initially dropped by 7 basis points before settling at a 5 basis point decline. However, the market is now in a mode of testing the Treasury’s commitment. Known as "bond vigilantes," large investors and hedge funds with record positions in the US Treasury market are monitoring whether the $4 billion monthly buyback is sufficient to cap yields.

DiMartino Booth suggests that if yields remain persistently high, the Treasury may be pressured to increase the buyback amount, potentially to $8 billion per month. The goal is to prevent the long end of the curve from spiking, which would turbocharge the current credit crisis. The 5% US 10-year Treasury yield is widely considered a "line in the sand" where systemic risks could begin to break across the financial system.

## The Federal Reserve’s Stance and the FOMC Minutes

The release of the FOMC minutes added another layer of complexity. The minutes revealed that several participants favored a 25 basis point rate hike, arguing that price pressures were broad-based and that policy needed to be more restrictive. However, the majority of the committee, led by Chair Kevin Warsh, chose to hold rates steady.

Warsh’s stance is rooted in the belief that incoming data will dictate policy, rather than providing forward guidance. Since the last meeting, economic data has been overwhelmingly dovish. Core inflation metrics have come in cooler than expected, with core Truthflation (TRU) at 1.62%. Furthermore, the labor market has shown signs of weakness, with the US losing 23,000 payroll jobs in July. DiMartino Booth argues that the data supports the decision to hold rates, as the disinflationary winds are blowing, making a rate hike difficult to justify to the committee.

## The Labor Market and Consumer Sentiment

Despite the focus on inflation, the labor market is facing significant headwinds. Since the end of last year, the US has lost 1.6 million full-time jobs. This loss is more impactful on aggregate consumption than part-time job losses, as full-time employment is a primary driver of household income.

Consumer sentiment, as measured by the University of Michigan index, remains near historical lows. A record high percentage of Americans report financial insecurity. The combination of stubbornly high gasoline prices (a dollar more than a year ago) and the loss of full-time income is tearing up family financial stability. Retailers report that consumers have less wherewithal for discretionary purchases, spending only on essentials. This environment suggests that the Fed’s dual mandate of price stability and maximum employment is under strain, with the labor market needing stabilization more than further tightening.

## The AI Factor and Future Outlook

The FOMC minutes also noted that productivity gains from AI adoption could eventually reduce production costs and increase aggregate supply, putting downward pressure on inflation. However, DiMartino Booth cautions that the jury is still out on whether AI will be broadly disinflationary or inflationary. While AI may increase efficiency for those who keep their jobs, it is also leading to significant job displacement.

Looking ahead, the major events on the radar include the Jackson Hole symposium and the September FOMC meeting. However, DiMartino Booth highlights the midterm elections as a critical, often overlooked risk. The potential for a "blue wave" could lead to political and economic turmoil. With the US debt clock approaching $40 trillion, the market is in a state of "slow simmer," where gradual increases in debt and rates are currently being absorbed, but the potential for a sharp correction remains if the balance between inflation, employment, and debt management is not carefully maintained.

## Summary

The Treasury’s decision to double its long-term bond buybacks to $4 billion is a strategic move to cap long-end yields and stabilize the credit market. While it is not QE, it functions as a form of Operation Twist, shifting the debt maturity profile. The move has triggered a rally in risk assets and a decline in the dollar. As the Fed holds rates steady in the face of cooling inflation but weakening labor data, the market is closely watching whether the Treasury will need to escalate its intervention to keep the "bond vigilantes" at bay. The coming months will be critical in determining whether this coordinated effort can successfully navigate the US economy through a period of high debt and shifting economic fundamentals.
