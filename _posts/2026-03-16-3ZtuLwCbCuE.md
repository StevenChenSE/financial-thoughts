**An Interview with the Legendary Crisis Investor on War, Wealth, and Survival**

*Note: This article is adapted from a recent interview with Doug Casey, bestselling author of *Crisis Investing* and founder of International Man.*

---

## The Overlooked Danger: Political Risk

“The big danger that everybody has today is not financial or economic,” warns Doug Casey. “Your biggest danger today is actually a political danger.”

Speaking from Buenos Aires, the seasoned investor and geopolitical observer cut straight to the core of his thesis: we are entering a period where geography may matter more than portfolio allocation. While financial systems are “coming unglued,” Casey insists the paramount threat is political. His advice? “You have to diversify politically as well as financially.”

“If you're able to, you should have a crib in another country in case things go bad,” he says, acknowledging that for many, economic strain makes this impossible. “We're entering something that looks like World War II.” He points to historical examples where people were “caught flat-footed being in the wrong country at the wrong time”—Russia in 1917, Germany in 1933, China in 1947, Vietnam in 1975. “Certainly before we get foreign exchange controls, which I also think are on the way.”

## The Iran War: A Costly Miscalculation

The conversation turned to the ongoing conflict between the U.S., Israel, and Iran. Casey is deeply skeptical of the war’s trajectory.

“It’s funny. When Trump started this war for Netanyahu, they said it’ll be over in two or three days,” he recalls. “But they didn’t seem to understand that Iran is a big country… The Iranians are going to make this an asymmetric war, a long war, which the US is not suited to fight.” He believes this is “bad news for us here in the West,” comparing it more to the protracted, losing conflict in Afghanistan than the conventional Iraq war.

The recent deployment of 2,500 Marines to the region strikes him as futile. “They can’t do anything against the Iranian Guard, which is 200,000 dedicated, fanatical type soldiers.” The economic consequences are already severe: with the Straits of Hormuz—a chokepoint for 20% of the world’s oil—partially closed, oil nears $100 a barrel. “The world runs on oil, frankly.”

## How Does America Lose? By Bankrupting Itself.

When asked how the U.S. loses this war, Casey’s answer is fiscal: “Just by letting time go by and bankrupting itself.”

“When you’re already $40 trillion in debt and running two to three trillion dollar deficits, which have to be monetized… the debt goes up, inflation goes up, the standard of living goes down. That’s how the US will lose this war.”

He dismisses the idea that war is good for markets broadly. “War is not good for the economy. You’re basically destroying real wealth… The amount of real wealth in the world goes down. War is a very bad thing for everybody and everything—except the state. The state gets bigger.”

## The Case for Gold and Undervalued Commodities

Given the turmoil, where does Casey see value?

“Gold is now trading at more than it should be by historical levels,” he admits, comparing it to the price of a house or a suit. “But that doesn’t mean it couldn’t go to $10,000 an ounce or more because who wants to hold the dollar? The dollar is a hot potato.” He sees gold as “the only financial asset that’s not simultaneously somebody else’s liability.”

His greater enthusiasm lies elsewhere: “I’ve been a big gold buyer for 50 years, but what I’ve really done that’s been good is speculate… in small gold mining stocks.” He believes the retail public is still largely absent from this sector, which remains tainted by ESG concerns, leaving mining stocks “still very cheap.”

Beyond precious metals, he’s looking at broader commodities. “Commodities are grossly underpriced relative to financial assets.” He recently bought corn ETFs, citing grains as “the cheapest area of the commodities market.” He’s also interested in uranium and coal.

He distinguishes his approach as that of a “speculator,” not a trader. “A speculator is someone who looks for distortions in the market… A trader is somebody trying to second-guess the market, meanwhile getting eaten up by fear and greed.”

## The Economic Storm Clouds Are Here

The data, Casey argues, confirms a grim outlook. He cites the U.S. GDP revision down to 0.7%, Canada’s worsening job numbers, and a record share of Americans making hardship withdrawals from their 401(k)s.

“I’ve been saying for years… we’re heading for a depression, a period of time when most people’s standard of living drops significantly.” That wonderful standard of living, he contends, “has been financed by debt.”

His stark advice for the average person: “Cut back your standard of living… sell the expensive car… take two jobs if you can and put aside the money prudently. A lot of people are going to wish they’d done that a year from now.”

As for equities, he’s largely out of the general stock market, which he sees as a “high-tech market” centered on an AI bubble. “I wonder whether all this money going into AI isn’t a misallocation… it’s going to blow up.”

## Final Thought: A Call for Prudence and Geographic Awareness

Doug Casey’s message is a sobering one. In a world where political danger eclipses financial risk, survival hinges on preparation, prudence, and a clear-eyed view of a global landscape in flux. His final reflection on the war perhaps sums it up best: “The best thing to do is don’t be involved in it. And bankrupt yourself, make new enemies, get a lot of people killed… That’s what the US should be doing. Period.”

For investors, the path forward involves looking beyond borders and conventional assets, toward tangible value and political havens—before the gates close.
