*An interview with legendary investor Doug Casey on war, economic crisis, and how to protect your wealth.*

---

## The Political Danger

“The big danger that everybody has today is not financial or economic,” says Doug Casey, the famed author of *Crisis Investing* and founder of International Man. “Your biggest danger today is actually a political danger.”

In a recent interview, Casey argued that while the economy and financial system are “coming unglued,” the primary risk to individuals is political instability. His advice? Diversify not only financially, but politically.

“If you’re able to, you should have a crib in another country in case things go bad,” he says. “We’re entering something that looks like World War II.”

He points to historical examples where people were “caught flat-footed being in the wrong country at the wrong time”—Russia in 1917, Germany in 1933, China in 1947, Vietnam in 1975. For those who can, he urges moving assets—and even themselves—abroad before foreign exchange controls make it impossible.

---

## The Iran War: A Costly Miscalculation

The conversation turned to the ongoing conflict between the U.S., Israel, and Iran. Casey is deeply skeptical of the war’s trajectory.

“It’s funny. When Trump started this war for Netanyahu, they said it’ll be over in two or three days,” he recalls. “But the Iranians are going to make this an asymmetric war, a long war. That’s bad news for us here in the West.”

He believes the conflict could spiral, drawing in other nations despite many U.S. allies refusing to participate. “America doesn’t need allies,” Casey argues. “American allies are nothing but tripwires and burdens on the U.S.”

Comparing it to Afghanistan rather than Iraq, he warns: “The U.S. walked away with its tail between its legs, defeated by backward people basically using AK-47s… It’s not going to end much better in Iran.”

The economic costs are already mounting. With the Straits of Hormuz—a chokepoint for 20% of the world’s oil—partially closed, oil prices have neared $100 a barrel. Casey notes that the war is “immensely expensive” for a U.S. already “$40 trillion in debt and running two to three trillion dollar deficits.”

“That’s how the U.S. will lose this war,” he says. “Just by letting time go by and bankrupting itself.”

---

## Markets, War, and Safe Havens

Does war benefit markets? “War is not good for the economy,” Casey states plainly. “You’re basically destroying real wealth.” While defense contractors may profit, the broader economy suffers. “The state gets bigger. That’s why the people in places like Washington and Tel Aviv… are all for war, but not the common person.”

On safe havens, Casey remains bullish on gold—but with nuance.

“Gold is now trading at more than it should be by historical levels,” he admits. “But that doesn’t mean it couldn’t go to $10,000 an ounce or more because who wants to hold the dollar? The dollar is a hot potato.”

He sees greater opportunity in gold mining stocks, which he believes are still undervalued. “Gold slightly overvalued. Mining stocks still very cheap. I think they’ve got a big run in front of them still.”

He’s also looking beyond precious metals. “Commodities are grossly underpriced relative to financial assets,” he says, mentioning recent purchases in corn ETFs, uranium, and coal. “The grains are very cheap at this point.”

---

## The Economic Storm Is Here

Casey has long warned of a “greater depression,” and he believes it’s now unfolding.

Citing troubling data—U.S. Q4 GDP revised down to 0.7%, Canadian unemployment jumping to 6.7%, a record share of Americans making hardship withdrawals from 401(k)s—he says the debt-fueled standard of living is unraveling.

“My advice to the average guy out there is… cut back your standard of living,” he urges. “Sell the expensive car… take two jobs if you can… put aside the money prudently. A lot of people are going to wish they’d done that.”

For investors, he’s largely out of the general stock market, which he sees as a tech-driven bubble. “I wonder whether all this money going into AI isn’t a misallocation,” he muses. Instead, he’s focused on specific commodities and special situations.

---

## Final Thought: A Return to Sanity?

Casey leaves with a sobering reflection on interventionism.

“The two bad actors in the Middle East are the Israelis… and the Iranians,” he says. “But it’s their problem. It’s not the problem of us here in North America. What they do, you’ve got to leave them alone.”

His prescription for America is simple: stop hunting dragons abroad, stop bankrupting yourself, and avoid making new enemies. In a time of political and economic danger, that may be the ultimate act of self-preservation.
