## Veteran energy analyst Josef Schachter breaks down the structural supply deficit, refining constraints, and the ongoing commodity supercycle.

### Introduction

Surging fuel costs are placing immense pressure on the global economy. In the United States, average diesel prices have climbed toward historic highs, directly straining the commercial trucking sector, which relies on diesel for the vast majority of freight transport. At the same time, crude oil prices remain elevated amid escalating geopolitical tensions in the Middle East and shipping risks around key chokepoints like the Strait of Hormuz.

Josef Schachter, president of Schachter Energy Research Services and a 40-year veteran of the energy sector, explains that while near-term geopolitical negotiations and seasonal shifts will generate volatility, the fundamental drivers point toward a prolonged commodity supercycle. Structural underinvestment, refining constraints, and persistent depletion rates indicate that oil prices may reach unprecedented highs over the next decade.

### The Looming Diesel Export Ban and Refinery Bottlenecks

Political debate in the United States has increasingly centered on potential policy interventions, including the proposal of a diesel export ban ahead of elections. While intended to lower domestic fuel prices, industry experts and Energy Secretary Chris Wright have cautioned that such a measure could trigger unintended consequences.

Refineries operate as integrated systems, cracking crude oil into specific yields of gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel. With US refining capacity operating near peak utilization—typically between 94% and 98%—restricting exports could quickly lead to domestic storage shortages for diesel. If refineries are forced to scale back runs due to a lack of storage, the output of gasoline and jet fuel would contract, paradoxically driving those prices higher. 

Furthermore, while countries in Latin America and Europe that rely on US distillates would face tighter supplies, domestic refiners would see their operational flexibility curtailed despite currently historic crack spreads.

### Geopolitics and the Strait of Hormuz

Geopolitical instability in the Persian Gulf continues to inject a substantial war premium into crude markets. Attacks on commercial shipping and heightened rhetoric between the United States and Iran have driven up insurance rates and prompted the US Navy to run escort convoys through the Strait of Hormuz to keep crude and LNG flows moving.

Schachter notes that a lasting resolution or diplomatic breakthrough is unlikely to materialize quickly, likely extending well beyond near-term political cycles. While alternative transportation routes—such as pipelines through Oman or Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline to the Red Sea—provide partial workarounds, physical infrastructure takes years to expand. 

In the absence of military conflict, oil prices might naturally settle in the $70 to $75 range during seasonal shoulder periods. However, the existing war premium keeps prices elevated near $100 per barrel, with potential spikes to $120–$140 if tensions escalate further.

### The Structural Supercycle: The Road to $200 Oil

Beyond geopolitical headlines, Schachter emphasizes that the true catalyst for the multi-year bull market in energy is structural underinvestment:

* **Global Consumption:** Worldwide demand hovers around 105 million barrels per day (bpd), driven increasingly by industrializing economies requiring metals, power, and data infrastructure.
* **Depletion Rates:** With an estimated global decline rate of roughly 8% annually, producers must bring approximately 6 million bpd of new output online every year merely to offset natural field depletion.
* **Capital Spending Deficit:** Global exploration and production spending remains well below the levels needed to replace exhausted reserves.

This dynamic mirrors previous commodity supercycles (1974–1981 and 1999–2008). As legacy fields deplete and global spare capacity shrinks, Schachter projects crude oil could surpass its 2008 all-time high of $147 per barrel before the end of the decade, potentially crossing $200 per barrel in the 2030s due to pure supply-demand imbalances.

### Global Production and Infrastructure Challenges

Hopes that international producers can rapidly step in to flood the market face severe logistical hurdles. While Venezuela holds vast reserves, decades of neglected infrastructure, degraded processing facilities, and deteriorated ports mean returning to historic production levels of 3 million bpd will require hundreds of billions of dollars and years of environmental remediation.

Similarly, Canada continues to serve as a vital, secure energy partner for North America, exporting roughly 4 million bpd to the United States. To hedge against US policy shifts and secure global market access, Canadian producers are increasingly focused on expanding infrastructure, including the Trans Mountain expansion (TMX), increased LNG export capacity on the West Coast, and prospective shipments through northern routes such as the Port of Churchill.

### Navigating Energy Investments and Value Plays

Given the long-term bullish outlook, Schachter recommends an opportunistic approach to energy equities. While many oil-weighted producers, service companies, and royalty trusts trade at elevated valuations following substantial runs from early 2025 lows, select bargains remain.

Schachter looks for companies where cash flow multiples trade at a substantial discount to proved developed producing (PDP) reserve values. Among current opportunities, natural gas and diversified producers like Tourmaline Oil stand out for their strong reserve life and conservative valuations, alongside selective names such as Birchcliff Energy and Bonterra Energy. Any near-term pullbacks caused by de-escalation of the war premium should be viewed as strategic entry points for long-term investors positioning for the broader commodity cycle.

### Summary

The global energy market faces both near-term volatility from Middle Eastern conflict and severe long-term constraints due to refining bottlenecks and structural underinvestment. As natural reservoir depletion outpaces current capital expenditure, crude oil is well-positioned for a sustained supercycle that could drive prices well above historic highs over the coming decade.
