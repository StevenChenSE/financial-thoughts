## Introduction

Recent market turbulence has left many investors questioning whether the Nasdaq’s sharp decline signals the end of the AI boom or merely a temporary correction. Following a significant sell-off marked by the largest retail investor outflow since COVID-19, markets have shown resilience with strong rebounds. In this analysis, Jim Thorne, Chief Market Strategist at Wellington Altus Private Wealth, joins David to dissect these events. Thorne provides a detailed outlook on the S&P 500, addresses concerns surrounding Nvidia and AI capital expenditures, and explains why he believes we are in the early stages of a secular growth phase rather than a bubble burst.

## The Liquidation Trade and Market Targets

Thorne characterizes the recent Nasdaq decline not as a fundamental breakdown, but as a "liquidation trade." He explains that some hedge funds had leveraged positions heavily in AI-related stocks and were forced to liquidate when volatility spiked. Despite this panic, Thorne remains bullish on his long-term targets:

*   **S&P 500 Target:** He maintains an 8,000 target for the end of the year.
*   **2027 Outlook:** He predicts the S&P could reach 10,000 with $450 in earnings per share, assuming a 22x multiple and the 10-year Treasury yield dropping to 4%.

Thorne notes that "bottoming is a process," and the extreme volatility seen recently—swinging from huge bounces to sharp declines—is part of this natural adjustment. He emphasizes that while retail investors panicked, institutional flows have stabilized, and Treasuries took a bid during the sell-off, indicating a flight to safety that has since reversed.

## AI CapEx: Capital Discipline vs. The Prisoner’s Dilemma

A central concern for many is whether AI data center construction will slow down due to rising interest rates and political shifts. Thorne addresses two main worries:
1.  **Political Pressure:** Democrats’ recent election wins raise questions about potential regulatory slowdowns for AI frontier growth.
2.  **Financing Constraints:** Recent earnings reports show that hyperscalers often rely on external financing because CapEx exceeds free cash flow.

Thorne argues that the era of the "prisoner’s dilemma"—where companies spent aggressively to avoid being wiped out—is over. Instead, we are entering a phase of **capital discipline**. This shift has positive long-term implications:
*   **Scarcity in Compute:** Reduced spending will create scarcity, potentially extending the AI cycle past 2030.
*   **Free Cash Flow:** Hyperscalers like Microsoft and Meta will become free cash flow positive sooner as they moderate near-term CapEx.
*   **Separation of Winners and Losers:** High-quality hyperscalers will still access capital (e.g., Microsoft issuing 20-year paper at 6%), while lower-quality participants in the data center space may struggle to get financed.

Thorne projects $1 trillion to $1.5 trillion in U.S. CapEx for AI infrastructure in 2027 and 2028, stating that this momentum is intact regardless of political noise. He notes that if states like Texas resist data centers, capital will simply move to friendlier jurisdictions like Alabama or Florida.

## Nvidia and the Jensen Huang Controversy

The interview also touches on recent news regarding Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s personal life, specifically allegations involving an alleged Chinese spy. Thorne separates the "noise" from the "signal." He argues that while this situation creates a board-level problem for Nvidia, it does not fundamentally alter the company’s technological moat or earnings potential.

*   **CUDA Moat:** Nvidia’s dominance is secured by its CUDA software ecosystem, which remains intact.
*   **Supply/Demand Balance:** Excess demand for GPUs persists, unaffected by leadership controversies.
*   **Market Reaction:** Thorne views the stock dip as temporary noise. He suggests investors should let the situation "shake out" and cool down before making decisions, noting that any impact on earnings is likely negligible compared to the broader AI trend.

## Fed Policy, Interest Rates, and the Cyclical Economy

Thorne highlights a critical divergence in the U.S. economy: while AI-driven sectors show strong non-inflationary growth, interest-rate-sensitive sectors (like housing and traditional construction) are buckling under tight monetary policy. He argues that the Federal Reserve has made a policy mistake by anchoring inflation expectations on energy prices distorted by geopolitical conflicts (specifically referencing the Iran war).

Thorne’s macro thesis relies on normalizing the yield curve:
*   **R-Star Variance:** The neutral interest rate (R-star) is lower for cyclical sectors than for the AI sector.
*   **Energy Shock Resolution:** Thorne predicts that resolving geopolitical tensions will cause oil prices and tanker rates to drop significantly, removing inflationary pressure.
*   **Rate Cuts:** If the 10-year Treasury yield falls to 4%, the yield curve should steepen. Historically, the 2-year yield leads the Fed funds rate; if the 2-year drops to 3%, the Fed should cut rates to 3% by the end of next year.

He believes that once energy shocks are resolved and inflation base effects roll off (expected by May), CPI and PCE will drop rapidly, potentially reaching 2% in the second quarter of next year. This environment would allow for a "hot economy" without inflation, driven by supply-side economics.

## Investment Strategy: Balancing Tech and Cyclicals

Given this outlook, Thorne recommends a balanced portfolio approach that captures both the structural AI trade and the cyclical recovery:

*   **Overweight Cyclicals:** He suggests leaning into sectors that have not participated in the AI rally, such as banks, small-cap value stocks, and the broader S&P 500 equal-weight index.
*   **Trimming Tech on Bounces:** While tech remains a strong structural theme, its extreme oversold nature may lead to a violent short-term bounce. Investors excessively overweight in tech should consider trimming positions into this rally to rebalance into cyclicals.
*   **Real Estate Opportunity:** Thorne views real estate as the sector most sensitive to Fed cuts. He suggests that now is the time to "kick tires" on real estate, anticipating that rate cuts will stimulate the housing market. He draws a parallel to 2007-2008, where central bankers hiked into a negative growth shock (energy), but expects a smoother transition this time without a crisis.

## Conclusion: Escaping Secular Stagnation

Thorne concludes with a broader historical perspective. He argues that the U.S. is escaping "secular stagnation," a phenomenon affecting many other developed economies like Japan and China. By combining supply-side policies (tariffs, infrastructure, reindustrialization) with technological innovation (AI, blockchain, tokenization), the U.S. can achieve strong GDP growth without inflation.

He references the post-WWII era as a roadmap, where America grew out of debt through industrial expansion. With productivity rising and unit labor costs negative, Thorne sees a secular growth phase extending into the next decade. For investors, this means maintaining exposure to risk assets, particularly during the typically bullish period following midterm elections, while staying disciplined about sector rotation between AI leaders and recovering cyclicals.
