In the world of market analysis, few things confuse investors more than a self-proclaimed bear who remains aggressively long equities. Chris Vermeulen, chief market strategist at TheTechnicalTrers.com, finds himself in exactly that position—and his reasoning exposes one of the most important lessons for anyone trying to navigate today's markets.

During a recent appearance on the David Lin Show, Vermeulen made a striking admission: despite believing "we're coming into a huge market correction that's going to devastate most investors," he remains long the S&P 500, long the NASDAQ, and continues to hold positions in energy stocks through the XOP ETF. To the uninitiated, this sounds like cognitive dissonance. To Vermeulen, it's simply following the rules.

## The Price-First Philosophy: Why Technical Analysis Trumps News

When asked how he navigates the treacherous waters of earnings season—the domain of increased volatility and unpredictable moves—Vermeulen doesn't hedge or second-guess. He simply points to his charts.

"There's two core ways you can look at the markets," Vermeulen explained. "One way is you follow price and momentum, money flows, and sentiment—which is reading the charts, reading the market itself. Or you follow fundamentals and news, which is all over the place. It's like trying to run through a minefield."

This isn't idle philosophy. Vermeulen applies this framework daily, and the recent earnings season provided a textbook example. On the day of the interview, Google reported strong earnings and surged 7%, while Meta missed expectations and dropped 10%. Rather than scrambling to react to each headline, Vermeulen watched the NASDAQ fall 1.2% in rapid succession on heavy volume as investors executed the classic "buy the rumor, sell the news" play.

"The market naturally is going to stick with its underlying trend," Vermeulen noted. "If the trend is up, any bad news usually is going to get absorbed by buying pressure. Any good news usually gets bought up—except when it doesn't, as we saw today."

His QQQ position, representing NASDAQ exposure, sits just fractions of a point away from hitting a 10% gain target. His band strategy, trading 2x leverage, is up approximately 20%. These aren't lucky guesses; they're the mechanical result of following a system.

## The Defensive Driver Analogy: Bearish Outlook, Bullish Positions

The apparent contradiction in Vermeulen's stance dissolves under closer examination. He compares his approach to defensive driving.

"It's kind of like my daughter's going through Young Drivers—she's learning to drive," Vermeulen explained. "You've got to constantly be checking the mirrors. You've got to know that there's something around you at any given time. If something jumps out and you swerve, you've already been checking. You're like, there shouldn't be anybody over there."

Applying this to markets, Vermeulen maintains constant vigilance for warning signs. The macro picture indeed raises concerns:

- **Precious metals have been "going ballistic"**
- **Wars are "cracking out" globally**
- **Oil is "skyrocketing," with energy stocks hitting all-time highs**
- **The US dollar is building a base**
- **Sector rotation continues aggressively**—one month a sector leads, the next month it lags

To Vermeulen, these conditions paint a bearish picture. Yet this doesn't mean betting against the market.

"Just because I'm bearish doesn't mean I'm betting on falling prices," he emphasized. "We're riding this market higher. It doesn't matter how bearish I am. I still follow my rules, I follow the strategy, I follow the money."

His methodology relies on multiple confirmation factors: daily price charts, money flow indicators, and sentiment readings. When all three align bullishly, he remains long. When they shift, he exits regardless of his personal macro views.

"The market went from a bounce, the money flows kick back in, and here we are with the S&P 500 rotating up," Vermeulen observed. "Price typically gives us a heads up before the news happens. Investors aren't idiots—they tend to move and make decisions as big things are starting to unfold."

## Oil and Energy: Trading the Equity, Not the Commodity

With geopolitical tensions escalating—specifically the ongoing conflict involving Iran and the rejection of uranium proposals that sent Brent crude spiking to $126 before pulling back to $114—many investors wonder if high oil prices might derail the equity rally.

Vermeulen's answer is nuanced. "I wouldn't short the S&P 500 just because oil is moving higher," he stated. "It is in an uptrend. So I won't bet against the current trend. But I'll definitely be like, 'Hey, this looks a little frothy.'"

His reasoning: high oil prices eventually squeeze consumer spending, pushing inflation higher and creating headwinds for stocks. However, he won't trade against momentum, even when he sees trouble brewing.

"I do watch the price, and a lot of times I'll see these big pops and I'll be like, 'Whoa, this is most likely going to have a quick pullback over the next few days.' But I never actually trade those intraday or one-day spikes on news, simply because they are really, really random."

Instead of trading crude oil futures directly—a vehicle that "blew up" two of his trading accounts many years ago—Vermeulen prefers to express his energy thesis through equities. His XOP position (an energy sector ETF) hit its first target, locking in gains while maintaining exposure to rising oil prices.

"As long as energy stays high, these energy companies can make a lot more money," Vermeulen noted. "I think the energy space and stocks are still going to do very well."

The lesson for retail investors is stark: commodities futures carry extreme leverage that can vaporize accounts in hours. "You can make a ton of money, but you can also lose it. It's much easier to lose it," Vermeulen warned. "I would recommend people don't trade futures unless you are an absolute expert in that commodity and you understand markets, trading, and risk management."

## Gold and Silver: Dead Money for Years?

Perhaps no asset class better illustrates Vermeulen's disciplined approach than precious metals. After gold's dramatic fall from its peaks—dropping sharply at the end of January and again in mid-March—the metal has hovered in a $4,500 to $5,000 range.

"The long-term picture of gold, there's no doubt the long-term trend is still up," Vermeulen acknowledged. However, the short-term picture tells a different story:

- The 50-day and 20-day moving averages are sloping downward
- Price sits below the 50-day moving average
- The 5-day moving average sits below both
- The chart pattern points toward $3,500-$3,600 for gold over the coming months

"This consolidation, this pullback, is actually a pretty controlled pullback," Vermeulen noted. "Based on the size of this move and this rally, this is actually a very controlled pause. And it's saying, 'Hey, this could be a bull flag—meaning this is a pause before the next target of $8,000 to $8,800.' Or it could continue to unwind and go down a little bit more over time and reset."

The uncertainty is precisely why Vermeulen remains on the sidelines. "Gold and silver could trade sideways for a year or two or three," he warned. "It could go dormant for a very, very long time, which nobody expects—and that's one reason why it could do it."

This is the crux of his approach: money is piling into equities, and until precious metals "start screaming" with clear technical signals, he'll follow the capital flows.

"Right now, money is piling into the equities market," Vermeulen stated flatly. "Let's just get long the asset that is actually going up in value and forget about these other ones for now—until they're screaming like, 'Bye, bye, bye.'"

## Bitcoin: The Contrarian Signal

Bitcoin's recent performance has earned it a new moniker among investors: "low beta." Once considered a high-beta play on risk assets, Bitcoin has underperformed stocks since the beginning of the year. Vermeulen's technical reading confirms this narrative.

"Bitcoin is stairstepping its way down," Vermeulen described. "It has a bearish chart pattern—a bear flag. You have price go down, it creates a flag pole, and then the flag usually flags in the opposite direction of the trend. So if the flag pole was down, it usually flags in the opposite direction. And then you'll usually have the second half of that move."

The pattern points toward $50,000-$52,000 per Bitcoin on the next leg down.

However, Vermeulen sees mixed signals in the near term:

- The long-term trend remains down (price below the 150-day moving average)
- The short-term trend shows bullish characteristics
- This creates a "little bull flag" that complicates the picture

"I don't like to trade when there's mixed signals," Vermeulen admitted. "I really wait for both or multiple time frames to generate a signal, and then we get in and we get a huge, powerful move—which is how we traded gold a bunch of times last year, catching these huge impulse waves to the upside."

The takeaway: Bitcoin's long-term technical picture remains bearish, with chart patterns reflecting the collective psychology of market participants. "Charts are really just the vibration of market participants of what they're thinking and feeling," Vermeulen observed. "Everything in this world is moving—it's all vibration. The stock market is literally the vibration of investors."

## Real Estate: Brace for a 10-20% Correction

When it comes to real estate, Vermeulen's outlook turns decidedly bearish. Both in the United States and his native Canada, housing markets face mounting pressure.

"I think by the end of this year, real estate pricing is going to be sharply lower," Vermeulen predicted. "We're going to see a lot more selling and probably prices down 10%, 15%, 20%—potentially a very big correction for housing prices."

He traces the problem to pandemic-era excess. "Everything got bit up and got overpriced. There was a feeding frenzy for people to buy and upgrade. We saw pretty much everything with a three-bedroom or more really take off after COVID. Everybody wanted a home office. Farm properties got swooped up. It just created this big feeding frenzy, and I think the momentum has stalled."

Beyond momentum, structural factors weigh on the sector:

- **Construction costs remain "ridiculous"** due to inflation
- **Supply is abundant** but demand is weak
- **Buyers are demanding significant price drops** before transacting
- **The seasonal spring surge may fail** to reverse the trend

From a trading standpoint, however, Vermeulen sees potential in real estate ETFs. The IYR (iShares U.S. Real Estate ETF) shows a bullish chart pattern pointing toward approximately 5% upside, driven by investors seeking defensive plays during periods of uncertainty.

"I think real estate overall, I don't think it's a time to buy real estate in terms of buying an actual physical home," Vermeulen cautioned. "I think the real estate ETF has potential to run higher, but I don't think actual physical home prices are really going to surge or rally 5%."

The wealth effect concern compounds his worries. "If people lose on their house or they don't get as much, especially when the markets start to turn south, they start to close their wallets," Vermeulen explained. "When you are uncertain, you don't spend money, and then it's a snowball. Business sales projections go down. Investors see things slowing. They start to sell stocks. If people take a hit on their house, they're not going to have as much money to put into the stock market."

## Following the Plan: Why News Shouldn't Drive Decisions

Throughout the interview, a consistent theme emerged: the news doesn't drive Vermeulen's decisions. Whether discussing Fed meetings (four descents and eight dissents—the highest since 1992), geopolitical developments, or weekly economic data releases, his response remains the same.

"They play zero part of what we do," Vermeulen stated unequivocally. "What these news events do is elevate a little bit of anxiety—like, 'Oh, we got the Fed out later today.' So it doesn't change our trades in any way, shape, or form. It just puts us more on high alert saying, 'Okay, we might see a big swing.'"

This emotional discipline separates successful traders from those who constantly shoot themselves in the foot. "If you follow a strategy and you have rules and risk in place, you'll never blow your account up," Vermeulen emphasized. "But as soon as you start trading on emotions and fear, that is how you make big mistakes. You end up getting in at the wrong time, getting out at the wrong time, skipping trades, putting on positions you shouldn't have."

His prescription is straightforward: "Plan the trade, then trade the plan."

## Finding Opportunity in Market Cycles

Vermeulen's approach doesn't simply follow trends—it seeks to exploit recurring market cycles that most investors ignore or cannot patience to play.

"The key here is to let the market work itself out," he explained. "We don't change our targets. We literally just play how the markets typically move."

His framework identifies specific percentage thresholds where markets historically exhaust moves and pull back. For the NASDAQ, a 10% move after a trend signal typically marks a point where correction becomes likely. When reached, he locks in partial gains, waits for the inevitable pullback lasting days or weeks, then reinvests with full portfolio conviction when a new signal emerges.

"I love focusing on the S&P 500 and NASDAQ because there are these huge, major cycles that roll through every week, every two weeks, every month, every quarter, every six months, every year," Vermeulen noted. "Based on economic data and paychecks that automatically go into the markets, there's seasonality—seasons in the markets like seasons in nature that affect people."

The sweet spot he targets: time frames "too short for a passive investor but too slow for an active trader"—typically 40 to 180 days. "The money is usually hidden in the spots that people aren't looking, and they're not patient enough for it."
