## A Historic Dialogue on Who Really Controls the World

What if the world's power structures aren't what they appear to be? What if the enemies we've been taught to fear are merely actors in a carefully orchestrated play—and the real power operates in ways most of us never see?

This was the central question explored in a landmark episode of Capital Cosmos, where two prominent analysts—Simon Dixon and Simon Micho—came together to compare their models of geopolitics, elite power structures, and the future of global finance. Their conversation, which ranged from commodity markets to central bank digital currencies, from the Strait of Hormuz to the nature of money itself, offered a rare glimpse into how the world's power elite actually operates.

What emerged was neither a simple conspiracy theory nor a dismissal of systemic concerns. Instead, it was a nuanced exploration of competing power factions, their overlapping agendas, and what this means for everyone else.

---

## Two Horses, Two Theories: The Fundamental Framework

Before diving into specifics, Micho outlined his core methodology: he views the world through a "horse race" of competing theories, constantly testing which framework best explains observed reality.

His two primary models:

**Model One: The Civil War Theory**

This theory posits an internal conflict within what he calls the "committee of deep state" or "committee of 300." One faction represents the old global imperial system—essentially the petro-dollar empire—while another represents a new regional power bloc, particularly interested in North and South American resources. The goal of the regional faction, Micho argues, is to systematically destroy the international reputation and operational capability of the global imperial system.

**Model Two: Technofudalism**

Micho has increasingly gravitated toward this second model: a coordinated plan for regional blocks overseen by a technocratic system, implemented through artificial intelligence and data infrastructure, which will replace the old order. This model suggests most of the new system is already built—it simply awaits "uncloaking" once the old system has been sufficiently dismantled.

What makes both theories compelling, Micho notes, is their convergent predictions: "Both of these are going to the the same outcome... We've been funneled to the same basic outcome."

Dixon, for his part, offered a more nuanced framework that attempted to bridge these perspectives while introducing additional layers of complexity.

## Simon Dixon's Model: Following the Money

Dixon begins by rejecting a common oversimplification: the idea that a small group of people engineers the world with perfect coordination. Instead, he sees a more chaotic reality where important influential factions push agendas—and those agendas frequently conflict.

"People fail in their mission," Dixon observes. He points to Operation Ajax 2.0—the recent attempt to replicate the 1953 CIA/MI6 regime change in Iran—as evidence that even powerful actors frequently fail. When plans go awry, financial powers respond with hedging strategies: using the derivatives market to profit regardless of outcomes.

### The Three Industrial Complexes

Dixon conceptualizes the top of the power structure as three interconnected but sometimes competing complexes:

1. **The Military-Industrial Complex** – Tends toward nationalist, patriotic orientations; associates the U.S. military with American identity itself
2. **The Financial-Industrial Complex (FIC)** – Truly global in nature; has no nationalistic loyalty; benefits from controlling the dollar but recognizes its eventual limitations
3. **The Technical-Industrial Complex** – Currently subordinate to capital but developing toward potential dominance

The crucial insight: while these complexes sometimes align (profit from war, finance reconstruction, build surveillance states), they frequently diverge. When they do, the financial industrial complex tends to prevail.

"Because they get to create the currency," Dixon explains. "They got the insurance contracts, they got the financialization and securitization. Even the military companies are just shares in the end, and they need access to capital."

### Nationalism vs. Globalism: The False Dichotomy

One of Dixon's most provocative claims challenges a popular narrative: the idea that Donald Trump is "taking on the crown"—the British Empire's remnant power symbolized by the Rothschilds and London financial centers.

"I think fails as a model from here," Dixon says flatly. "Because I think they've merged into one model. So anything around like Trump taking on the crown, I think has failed as a mechanism of understanding the world."

The real dynamic, in Dixon's view, is between transnational capital—which co-opted both communism and capitalism as Western ideologies—and the various nationalist factions that still believe in country-specific power. But even this framing requires refinement.

"The transnational capital was effectively communism and capitalism were western ideologies," Dixon continues. "They funded both and fascism. At one point you had the Hitler regime, you had the Federal Reserve, and you had the Bolshevik revolution and the creation of the Soviet Union. That's all funded by the same transnational capital that wants to create strategic tension."

## The Cryptocurrency Deception: Bitcoin's True History

Perhaps the most detailed section of the conversation concerned cryptocurrency—and what Dixon reveals should concern anyone who believes digital money represents liberation.

Dixon, who claims involvement with Bitcoin from when it was just $3, offers a nuanced history. He believes Bitcoin originated from intelligence communities—possibly a CIA or NSA project—and points to the suspicious deaths of figures connected to its creation.

"Both of those died under suspicious circumstances," Dixon notes, referring to Len Sassimon and Hal Finney, early Bitcoin developers. "Satoshi Nakamoto and the coins can never be retrieved. But what I think they did is I think they open sourced it. And that wasn't meant to be the project."

The genuine decentralized structure of Bitcoin—open-source code on a decentralized network, with nodes enforcing consensus—created what Dixon calls "a resistance infrastructure." He notes with irony that today, the largest sovereign Bitcoin miners include Iran, Russia, and the UAE—nations outside the Western financial system.

But here's the key: the real project was never Bitcoin itself.

"The real project that they wanted was stable coins and central bank digital currencies," Dixon states. "So now if you look at where we are now, Trump—his primary backer was Elon Musk, a node in the technical industrial complex—they immediately went into Stargate Project. They immediately said, 'We're going to be crypto capital of the world.' They infiltrated the Bitcoin conferences."

The evidence Dixon presents is extensive:

- FBI directors speaking at Bitcoin conferences
- SEC heads appearing alongside Bitcoin organizers
- David Bailey, Bitcoin conference organizer, acquiring Bitcoin media companies and raising finance through Caner Fitzgerald (connected to the Epstein files)
- Justin Sun pardoned despite crypto-related crimes
- Trump family involvement with World Liberty Financial

"The Bitcoin treasury companies, Michael Sailor, Jack Mallers through borrowing against your Bitcoin—all of these I believe are covert infiltration attempts to centralize as much Bitcoin as possible," Dixon claims.

The endgame? A two-tiered system where the public gets stable coins, CBDCs, and Bitcoin held through custodians, while elites maintain exits through self-custody and Bitcoin in its original form.

"You incentivize them to custody it through Bitcoin treasury companies, ETFs, but the elites get the exit of Bitcoin and self-custody when they want to exit the system," Dixon explains. "The end result is integration with artificial intelligence, programmable money, and social credit scores, which effectively is being built as we speak right now."

## The Strait of Hormuz: The Real War Nobody's Talking About

The conversation then turned to immediate geopolitical events—the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and its implications.

Micho presents his analysis: "This could well be my working theory at the moment: this is the event that's synthetic. The whole thing is so in-your-face unnecessary to create a particular outcome, and that outcome is the destruction of the existing system so they can create the next system that can be inserted in its place."

Using actuarial software, Micho modeled two scenarios: immediate reopening versus five-month closure ending July 1st. His conclusion: "Damage is permanent... even if the strait opens tomorrow, we'll still get that."

Dixon unpacks who actually benefits from the ongoing crisis:

**Energy Companies**

- **Shaneer Energy, Chevron, Exxon** – Major Texas refining operations
- **Golden Pass** – 70% owned by Qatar, 30% by Exxon; major LNG facility
- **Halle Burton** and other infrastructure plays

"The winners are going to be those companies," Dixon confirms. "This is a super subsidy for them."

**The Broader Financial Picture**

The entities benefiting include subsidiaries of Saudi Aramco (which owns a refinery in Texas), while Iran still finds ways to move oil to China. Meanwhile:

- The military budget increased from $1 trillion to $1.5 trillion—a "stimulus check" for Lockheed Martin, General Dynamics, Raytheon, Palantir, Boeing
- The financial industrial complex owns shares in all of them

"This is a massive wealth transfer over from the average American to the shareholder class," Dixon summarizes. "Biggest we'll see."

## UAE and OPEC: The Multipolar Chess Game

The announcement of UAE potentially leaving OPEC prompted extensive analysis.

Micho's framework: old control structures—OPEC, in this case—may be "in the way" of new arrangements. "Is it the case that we had control structures that worked a couple of decades ago, but now they're in the way now?"

Dixon offers a more specific analysis. He sees UAE as a "penetration node" for the financial industrial complex in the multipolar world:

- First major economy to join Abraham Accords and normalize with Israel
- Created good cop/bad cop dynamic with Saudi Arabia
- Becoming what Dixon calls "East Israel"
- Hub for crypto capital and surveillance state infrastructure
- Central to Binance's relocation and operations
- Key to circumventing sanctions for Iran

"UAE was the first one that was economically most distressed because of its major inputs which affects all the GCC—tourism and financial services," Dixon explains. "It created crypto capital. It's doing the police and surveillance state."

The connection between UAE and Iran runs deep: Iran is UAE's second-largest trading partner (after China). Sanction circumvention routes for Iran run through UAE and the SIPs system.

"The straight of Hormuz," Dixon suggests, "is just the catalyst for getting those repricing everything."

## Resource Nationalism: The Industrial Reality

Micho's professional background in mining and industrial structures provided crucial grounding to the discussion.

"China, in its own territory, owns and controls directly something like 70% of the industrial network worldwide," Micho reveals. This includes not just domestic production but international ports, railways, and factories through Belt and Road initiatives.

Dixon adds the financial layer: "There's only real two powers in the world. There's the CCP and the financial industrial complex."

The FIC has co-opted every sovereign wealth fund—Norway, the Gulf states, UAE, Singapore—through co-investment. But there's a twist: China funds them all. "China buys all of their commodities, all of their exports. So those are the only two powers."

The BRICS framework represents this negotiation: China with strategic tension from Russia, India, and commodity-driven nations seeking alternative manufacturing bases.

### The Commodity Price Signal

Micho presented a striking analysis of commodity prices dating to 1960:

"January 2005 is when this happened," he explains. "That correlates with... oil production plateaued. And in January 2005, oil production plateaued. And what that was driven by—the Iraq war in 2003 took Iraq offline."

Since then, the system has been in a state of thermodynamic correction:

"What does this chart tell me? The fact that we're seeing blowouts and things are swinging everywhere—this is the system trying to thermodynamically correct before it collapses."

The pattern mirrors 1971: "That's the decoupling from the gold standard. And then after, you've got this blowout—and this blowout was actually more savage."

## The Path Forward: Two Simons, One Uncomfortable Conclusion

Despite their different frameworks, both analysts converged on uncomfortable truths about solutions—or the lack thereof.

### Micho's Community-Based Approach

"I've developed a small-scale example of a series of technologies that we could put together in a small community that could actually make a difference in terms of energy and industry and food production," Micho explains. "The heart of it is an energy system that is small in footprint but high quality: the thorium MSR [molten salt reactor]."

But technology alone isn't sufficient. Micho speaks in terms of sacred collaboration:

"Our society at the moment, our social contract has become deeply unkind to the point where there is no trust. Every human relationship is founded by four pillars: love, trust, respect, and honor—and they don't exist in many modern dialogues anymore."

His vision: localized communities that "don't play along with this narrative," combining technological solutions with spiritual principles of creation versus destruction.

### Dixon's Financial Solution

Dixon takes a different but complementary approach: "I do believe there is a financial solution. If we boycott and we become the next elites that know how to play the market, then the next set of elites are the ones that can change the system from within."

His personal journey illustrates this: "Bitcoin gave me financial freedom. And because it was sovereign, I was able to unwind all my compromised networks—companies and investments that take you down darker paths."

But Dixon identifies a critical problem:

"There are people that benefit from this system, and because their financial interests are aligned with continuing the same system, they don't talk out. And sadly, those are the people with the capital to actually make a difference because they can allocate real capital."

The algorithm, Dixon warns, turns opposition into surveillance credit scores: "You get freedom of speech but not freedom of reach so they can build your social credit score and your police and surveillance state."

His proposed solution: "If we can create more people that are wealthy, that are willing to opt out and use their capital to buy the lobbies, support local politicians that haven't been corrupted, and change the system from within—because you have to beat the system. If you don't beat the system, you're broke."

### The Spiritual Dimension

Both analysts eventually moved beyond economics into deeper questions.

"There's a grand architecture," Dixon reflects. "If you just look at the Federal Reserve system, it ends this way. It's maths, it's code. It's inevitable, predictable, guaranteed."

But Micho counters with a different perspective: "As a man of God, I do believe there is a greater plan, and this is but a human plan."

Dixon responds: "Maybe we don't get to fix the world. Maybe the world is full of problems. And if you're blessed with wealth, then maybe that's a test. The test is: do you do more good with that wealth or do you do more bad with that wealth?"

The conversation ends with Micho observing: "You've got two basic opposing forces. You've got the principle of creation and the principle of destruction. Are you creating stuff that has a way of creating things as it goes forward, or are you supporting actions that will accelerate entropy and erosion?"

## The Final Verdict: Coincidence or Coordination?

Do these two models—the civil war theory and technofudalism—represent genuine alternatives or complementary views of the same phenomenon?

"These two rough theories seem to be digesting each other," Micho observes. "That's why I'm saying it's really hard to tell one from the other."

Dixon agrees: "What's interesting to me is both of these are going to the the same outcome."

Perhaps the most sobering conclusion comes from Micho: "The only way it's not going to work is if human consciousness, as a group, says we're not going to go along with this. Right? That's the only path out of this I can see."

## Key Takeaways

1. **Power operates through competing but often aligned factions**, primarily the military-industrial complex, financial-industrial complex, and technical-industrial complex. While they sometimes conflict, transnational capital increasingly coordinates their activities.

2. **Central bank digital currencies represent the real endgame**, not Bitcoin or cryptocurrency liberation. The infrastructure for a programmable, surveilled monetary system already exists in "wholesale" form (FedNow), awaiting extension to retail through crisis-engineered consent.

3. **The Strait of Hormuz closure functions as a manufactured crisis** designed to justify energy lockdowns, force adoption of new financial systems, and transfer wealth from average citizens to shareholder classes through inflated energy prices and military spending.

4. **Resource nationalism and multipolarity serve as transitional phases** toward a more integrated global technocratic system. Regional blocks (GCC, BRICS) represent penetration points for transnational capital, not genuine alternatives.

5. **Individual and collective action through conscious capital allocation**—supporting local economies, demanding corporate accountability, and building parallel community systems—may offer the only viable resistance, requiring both spiritual purpose and practical financial discipline.
