## Expert economist EJ Antony on free trade, inflation data, and the future of work in the AI era

The American economy is at a crossroads. As geopolitical tensions reshape global trade relationships and artificial intelligence begins transforming the workforce, one prominent economist is sounding alarms about a return to the protectionist policies that defined the 1930s.

In a wide-ranging interview, EJ Antony, chief economist at the Heritage Foundation, offered his analysis on everything from the U.S. withdrawal from USMCA to the accuracy of government inflation data—and his perspective on how workers can survive and thrive in an AI-driven world.

**The Death of Free Trade in North America?**

The interview came just hours before the Trump administration formally declared it would not extend the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA), triggering a six-year review process that could unwind the 32-year-old North American free trade zone.

"NAFTA was certainly done," Antony acknowledged, "but in terms of USMCA, it's not done overnight. This just starts a process of unwinding it, and it would take years for that to happen. In fact, it wouldn't even take effect until Trump is long gone out of office."

Yet Antony expressed concern that beyond the immediate trade agreement, the very concept of free trade in North America may be in jeopardy. "When I said NAFTA, I really meant free trade. The idea of free trade in North America as a block—are we ever going to get that back? I hope so."

The economist criticized the administration's approach to reciprocity in trade, arguing that previous trade representatives like Robert Lighthizer provided more measured guidance than current advisors. "Free trade has been incredibly good for the United States. I think you can have free and fair trade. You can have reciprocity. Unfortunately, this administration's attempts at reciprocity just haven't gone well. We still don't have a truly reciprocal tariff schedule."

**Economic Growth Outlook: A Dramatic Downgrade**

When asked about economic growth projections for 2027, Antony's response revealed a significant shift in his expectations. "I would have given you a much more bullish outlook if you had asked me in February," he said. "But the fallout from the Iran war is very real, significant, and a lot of it—I'd say even the majority—hasn't even hit the economy yet."

The economist expects consumer prices to continue spiking in the weeks and months ahead as supply chain disruptions work their way through the economy. While he previously anticipated growth in the 4-5% range, he now believes the U.S. economy will likely grow at around 2%, with 3% being possible but not probable.

The outlook on inflation remains murky despite falling oil prices. Antony predicted that even if pump prices return to pre-war levels and stay there, consumers should not underestimate the impact of supply chain disruptions that will continue to manifest in "weird spikes" across various consumer categories—from food prices affected by fertilizer costs to products containing plastics and chemicals derived from petroleum.

**The 1930s Comparison: Protectionism on the Rise**

Antony drew a striking comparison between current economic conditions and the era of the Great Depression. "It feels like the 1930s all over again," he said. "It was just beggar-thy-neighbor economics. All these different countries kept putting tariffs on each other, and then they were essentially doing competitive devaluations of their currencies. It really drove up consumer prices without actually making anybody better off globally speaking."

However, the economist was quick to add a caveat: "We're not moving at anywhere near the speed of the 1930s. The total amount of tariffs relative to the size of the American economy is pretty small. Part of that is because in the U.S., the percentage of our economy that comes from international trade is one of the lowest in the country."

**The Problem with Government Inflation Data**

A significant portion of the interview focused on the accuracy of economic data, particularly inflation measurements from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). Antony, who was once nominated to lead the BLS following the firing of Erica Macie, emphasized the need for improved data quality.

"You can look to the fact that the revisions we have seen recently are truly unprecedented—not only in terms of their size but in terms of the fact that they are consistently going in the same direction," he explained. "We would expect statistically these things to be what we call a random walk, where you have one month up, one month down, and that pattern continues. If those revisions are not averaging out to zero, it may indicate that you have a bias."

Antony pointed to specific issues with how the Consumer Price Index is calculated, noting that numerous changes over the years have consistently biased the index downward. "Even things like the hedonic adjustment that tries to control for the quality of products and services improving over time—I think it's very clear that those things have been overestimated. It has artificially reduced the CPI."

He contrasted official BLS data with real-time price aggregators like Truflation, which monitors millions of prices daily, noting that these alternatives consistently showed prices rising much faster than government figures indicated.

**Why Raising Interest Rates Won't Fix Supply Shocks**

When asked about the appropriate Federal Reserve response to inflation caused by supply shocks, Antony's answer was unequivocal: "No. History has told us that it's not the case."

He referenced the Fed's mistake during the 1970s oil crisis, when policymakers attempted to fight rising prices by raising interest rates. "All it did was simply compound the problem because you can't take a monetary tool and try to fix a non-monetary problem. If you need a screwdriver, don't go reaching for a hammer. All you're going to do is break something else."

The economist argued that supply shocks—whether from oil disruptions, fertilizer shortages, or helium constraints—require time to work through supply chains, and raising interest rates only exacerbates the economic damage without addressing the root cause.

**Big Tech Regulation: The Wrong Solution**

The interview addressed recent calls from politicians like AOC and Bernie Sanders to break up large technology companies, with Antony offering a pointed critique.

"These are lovely talking points," he said. "They're very attractive. It sounds nice to want to protect consumers. But when we talk about breaking up big tech companies, these people identify what they perceive to be problems—such as rising prices for technology products—and immediately jump to the conclusion that it's because certain companies are large."

Antony argued that rising technology prices have nothing to do with company size and everything to do with unprecedented demand for computer chips and memory driven by AI development. "The answer then is not to break up companies who have economies of scale and can actually get these chips at lower prices and pass those savings on to consumers because they're placing such massive orders. The answer is to simply produce more chips. Increase supply, and you drive down prices. These proposals make zero sense."

**AI and the Future of Work: Adapting, Not Retreating**

Perhaps the most insightful portion of the interview addressed concerns about artificial intelligence displacing workers. Antony offered a perspective that challenges conventional wisdom: "Nobody loses their job to a new technology. You lose your job to somebody else who knows how to use that technology."

He advised workers to embrace AI rather than fear it, recommending they "learn how to use AI, learn how to be as productive as possible, learn to be the most valuable worker in your workspace. That's your job insurance."

The economist also challenged Keynesian economic theory that technological advancement reduces aggregate demand. "Aggregate demand is infinite. Every single one of us individually has unlimited wants and needs. When a technology makes certain things cheaper, you spend less on that and have more money to spend on other things—which creates demand in other sectors of the economy."

Looking ahead, Antony predicted increased demand for occupations that cannot be easily automated: "AI is not going to do your plumbing or run your electrical for you. AI is not going to be the mason that puts a new brick or stone facade on your home. It may very well increase demand for occupations in the trades."

He also speculated about less obvious consequences, such as whether sophisticated deepfake technology might eventually render video evidence inadmissible in court, potentially reviving demand for security personnel and other verification roles.

**The Human Element in Future Warfare**

Regarding military applications, Antony cited Victor Davis Hansen's book *The Second World Wars* to argue that even in an age of AI and drone warfare, human involvement remains essential. "Air supremacy doesn't automatically mean you win the war because we don't have wings. Nobody lives in the air. If you want to affect the outcome of the war, you have to affect people and where people live, which is on the ground."

The economist suggested future battlefields might feature fewer human pilots in cockpits and more humans piloting drones remotely, but human judgment and presence will continue to matter.

**Looking Forward**

As the interview concluded, Antony remained cautiously optimistic despite acknowledging serious economic headwinds. The key message: while 1930s-style protectionism poses real risks, and supply chain disruptions will continue to impact prices throughout 2027, the fundamental adaptability of the economy—and of workers willing to learn new skills—provides grounds for hope.
