## Introduction
On October 22, global financial markets experienced a significant downturn. The S&P 500 fell by more than 1%, while the NASDAQ dropped 2.5%. Gold declined nearly 2%, and Bitcoin fell 3.5%. While Treasury yields slid on the long end, this was largely driven by safe-haven buying rather than positive economic sentiment. Ole Hansen, Head of Commodity Strategy at Saxo Bank, joins the discussion to dissect the drivers behind this "big down day," focusing on the interplay between escalating oil prices, growing skepticism around the AI trade, and broader commodity trends.

## The Twin Drivers: High Oil and AI Skepticism
According to Hansen, two primary factors are weighing on investor sentiment. First, Brent crude trading above $112 is significantly impacting market psychology. Second, the tech sector has delivered lukewarm earnings responses, prompting investors to question the massive capital expenditures associated with Artificial Intelligence (AI).

The core issue for the AI trade is that "money costs money." Hansen notes that while hyperscalers initially funded their AI arms race with cash on hand, they are increasingly borrowing. With funding costs rising due to higher central bank rates and bond yields, investors are demanding visible returns on these massive investments. This shift marks a departure from the previous "buy every dip" mentality, as the market begins to ask harder questions about profitability and sustainability.

## The Oil Paradox: Flows Return but Costs Soar
A puzzling dynamic has emerged in the oil market. Recent data indicates that oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz have returned to approximately 82% of pre-war levels, with total Middle East exports surpassing previous benchmarks. However, this does not translate into lower prices for consumers.

Hansen explains that while crude oil is flowing, it is doing so at a punitive cost. Due to security risks, including missile and mine attacks on ships, there is a heavy reliance on smaller vessels and ship-to-ship transfers outside the Strait. These logistical complexities add at least $30 per barrel to the final price.

### The Refining Bottleneck
The critical issue is not just crude supply, but refined product supply. While crude flows are recovering, the flow of fuel products (gasoline, diesel) and LNG remains significantly below pre-war levels—potentially close to 40% for fuel products. This is because:
1.  **Refinery Capacity:** Key refineries in the Middle East and Russia are curtailed or offline.
2.  **Risk Aversion:** Ships carrying refined products face higher explosion risks than crude carriers, leading to further transport hesitancy.
3.  **Global Competition:** China, a massive refinery hub, has cut imports to focus on domestic needs due to high costs, drying up its export supply to the global market.

Consequently, distillate markets remain extremely tight. Ultra-low sulfur diesel (ULSD) in New York Harbor is trading above $200 per barrel equivalent, and European diesel prices have remained stubbornly high despite releases from strategic reserves.

## Diesel: The Workhorse Under Pressure
Hansen emphasizes that diesel is the "workhorse" of the global economy, powering heavy machinery, agriculture, aviation, and logistics. The current crack spread (the difference between crude oil price and refined product price) for diesel is around $80, reflecting severe supply constraints.

Despite US efforts to pressure allies into releasing up to 100 million barrels of strategic stocks, Hansen argues this has had limited global impact because these reserves are often locked in local markets rather than being exported globally. Furthermore, the lifting of some Russian diesel export bans may not provide significant relief, as Russia’s ability to ramp up production and exports remains uncertain. With winter approaching, where diesel is also used for heating, any further disruption could force prices even higher.

## Natural Gas: Europe’s Winter Vulnerability
European natural gas prices have surged to their highest levels since 2022, trading at roughly $30 per therm—ten times higher than US prices. Hansen points out that European storage is at record lows for this time of year because the summer arbitrage failed; forward curves were backwardated (winter prices lower than summer), making it unprofitable for traders to buy and store gas.

With LNG supplies from Qatar constrained by regional conflicts, Europe faces fierce competition with Asia for available cargoes. Hansen warns that a deep freeze could trigger significant price spikes, as stored gas accounts for only about 25% of winter consumption needs.

## Commodities: Gold, Copper, and Agriculture
### Gold: Speculative Selling vs. Strategic Demand
Gold has faced speculative selling pressure from hedge funds and CTAs since late August, with net selling reaching 4 million ounces. However, total holdings have increased by 2.3 million ounces, indicating strong strategic demand from central banks and private investors in China and India.

Hansen remains long-term bullish on gold, predicting prices could be 10-20% higher in the coming years due to geopolitical instability and monetary inflation. In the short term, however, technical levels are critical; he identifies $4,230 as a key resistance level that must be reclaimed for a sustained rally.

### Copper: AI Jitters and Tariff Risks
Copper prices have paused their upward trend amid fears of an AI bubble burst. Since data centers require massive amounts of copper for electricity conduction, any downward revision in AI infrastructure spending could dampen demand. Additionally, large visible stockpiles in the US, built in anticipation of tariffs, could flood the global market if those tariffs do not materialize. Hansen maintains a bullish long-term view but acknowledges potential short-term headwinds.

### Agriculture: The Harvest Correction
Agricultural commodities have seen a 14% drawdown in six weeks, reversing August’s rally. This correction is driven by hedge funds exiting one-sided positions and the arrival of harvest supplies for soybeans and corn. However, Hansen warns that El Niño weather patterns pose significant risks to future production, suggesting continued volatility in the agricultural sector.

## European Fiscal Concerns: France
Hansen also highlights growing fiscal uncertainty in France, where the 10-year yield has risen above 5%, widening the spread over German bonds to 170 basis points—a historic high. He compares the situation to Greece’s debt crisis but notes that political resistance to reform (particularly regarding retirement age) makes resolution difficult. The inability to address structural pension deficits poses a significant risk to the European economy.

## Summary
The market selloff on October 22 reflects a broader reckoning with higher costs of capital and energy. Ole Hansen argues that while oil flows are technically recovering, the cost of refining and transporting fuel products remains prohibitive, keeping inflationary pressures alive. Simultaneously, the AI trade faces scrutiny as funding costs rise. While long-term fundamentals for commodities like gold and copper remain supportive, short-term volatility is expected to persist due to geopolitical risks, weather patterns, and technical market shifts.
