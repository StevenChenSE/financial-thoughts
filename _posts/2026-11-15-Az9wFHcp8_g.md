## Introduction

The financial markets are bracing for potential turbulence as the 10-year Treasury yield has climbed back above 5%, driven by hotter-than-expected October consumer price index (CPI) data. With traders now pricing a December rate hike as a near certainty, questions linger regarding how many more hikes remain in this tightening cycle and how far interest rates can climb.

In a recent discussion with Peter Boockvar, Chief Investment Officer of 1.BFG Wealth Partners and author of *The Book Report*, we explored the complex factors driving bond yields higher, the Federal Reserve’s next moves, and the significant implications of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s departure for the broader AI trade.

## The Drivers Behind Rising Bond Yields

A notable shift in market dynamics has been observed: the 10-year Treasury yield is no longer strictly correlated with oil prices. While previous narratives suggested bonds were primarily an "oil story," recent trends show yields soaring even as oil prices fall. Boockvar argues that the current bond sell-off is driven by a combination of factors rather than a single narrative.

### Key Factors Influencing Yields
*   **Inflation and Oil:** Persistent inflation remains a primary driver, though its relationship with oil prices has become more complex.
*   **Debt and Deficits:** Growing concerns about sovereign debt levels and budget deficits are pressuring the long end of the yield curve.
*   **Supply Overload:** The market is saturated with bond supply from two major sources:
    1.  **Hyperscalers:** Massive financing needs for building out the AI ecosystem.
    2.  **Government Issuance:** Increased demand for governments to sell their own bonds to fund deficits.

Boockvar notes that while sentiment was bearish when yields hit 5.30%, a contrarian rally pushed them back to around 4.85%. However, with the recent CPI shock pushing yields above 5% again, he anticipates a short-term peak but believes overall rates will trend higher in the coming years.

## Federal Reserve Policy: December Hike Likely

The hot October CPI number has solidified expectations for a Federal Reserve rate hike in December. According to prediction market Kalshi, there is an 86% chance of a 25 basis point hike. Fed Governor Christopher Waller even suggested that a 50 basis point hike should be considered if inflation data remained hot, which it did.

### Global Central Bank Tightening
The Fed is not alone in its hawkish stance. Boockvar highlights that other major central banks are also poised to act:
*   **ECB (European Central Bank):** Likely to hike in December.
*   **BOJ (Bank of Japan):** Expected to hike in December.
*   **BOE (Bank of England):** Also likely to hike in December.

Despite this, Boockvar characterizes the Fed’s current stance as a "rate tweaking cycle" rather than a massive hiking campaign. He believes the December hike could be the last, contingent on how the AI capex cycle evolves.

## The AI Trade and Capex Slowdown

A critical variable in the interest rate outlook is the pace of capital expenditure (capex) for AI infrastructure. Boockvar predicts that 2027 will likely mark peak annualized capex dollars and rate of change for hyperscalers.

### Why Capex Must Slow
Hyperscalers like Meta, Oracle, Amazon, and Google are spending $200–$300 billion annually on AI infrastructure, often exceeding half their revenue (or nearly all, in Oracle’s case). Shareholders are increasingly questioning when the payoff will materialize. Boockvar argues that for these companies to be profitable, they must eventually slow the pace of spend due to:
*   **Shareholder Pressure:** Demands for profitability over growth at any cost.
*   **Regulatory Hurdles:** State governors imposing moratoriums on data centers due to concerns about water and electricity prices.
*   **Financing Costs:** Higher interest rates making debt more expensive to service.

### Impact on the Economy and Fed Policy
The AI build-out has been a massive contributor to GDP and stock market performance, boosting upper-income spending through equity gains. Fed Chair Jerome Powell and others are sensitive to this dynamic. If AI capex slows significantly, it could threaten economic growth, potentially leading to rate cuts in the short term. However, Boockvar warns that long-term yields might not fall even in a recession scenario. If the US economy contracts due to an AI slowdown, the budget deficit could balloon from 6% to 12% of GDP, requiring massive Treasury issuance that would keep long-term rates elevated.

## Nvidia’s Leadership Crisis and Market Implications

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang recently resigned following scandals involving personal affairs. This event has caused Nvidia’s stock to drop approximately 40% from its early October peak. While former FDIC Chair Sheila Bair noted that Nvidia is not "too big to fail" in a systemic banking sense, the leadership change carries significant weight.

### The Irreplaceable Jensen Huang
Boockvar emphasizes that while Nvidia will continue to produce products customers want, Jensen Huang was a unique CEO who created an incredible business with multiple talents. His departure means:
*   **Market Share Erosion:** Competitors and customers are already seeking to diversify away from Nvidia.
*   **Leadership Vacuum:** CFO Colette Kress is currently interim CEO, but no replacement can match Huang’s specific capabilities.
*   **Reduced Aggressiveness in Financing:** As of Q2, Nvidia held about $100 billion in equity investments and had significant financing obligations. Under new leadership, the company is unlikely to be as aggressive in circular financing arrangements with customers.

### CoreWeave and Demand Dynamics
CoreWeave CEO Michael Intrator recently stated that demand for AI infrastructure outpaces supply, with capacity 10x oversubscribed for 2027. Boockvar agrees that demand is strong but cautions that supply will eventually catch up, leading to lower computing prices. Companies like CoreWeave, which rely on high GPU lease prices to service enormous debt loads, may see their business models squeezed as hardware costs decline.

## Commodity Outlook: Gold, Energy, and Agriculture

Boockvar maintains a portfolio with about one-third allocated to commodities and commodity stocks, including energy, agriculture (fertilizers), precious metals, and uranium.

### Gold’s Headwinds and Tailwinds
Gold has faced headwinds this year due to rising real rates and a stronger US dollar. However, Boockvar points to a critical support level: central bank buying. The World Gold Council reported that central banks purchased 300–320 tons of gold in Q3, continuing a trend seen since 2022. While Western investors may sell due to higher rates, central banks are using the weakness to accumulate. Boockvar suggests that if AI stocks weaken and foreign investors repatriate funds from US tech, the dollar could fall, which would be bullish for gold.

### Inflation Expectations Puzzle
A fascinating divergence exists in bond markets: while nominal yields have risen, 10-year break-even inflation rates are at their lowest since early in the year (2.2%). This suggests that the TIPS market believes current inflation spikes are temporary and will not persist long-term. The rise in yields is therefore driven more by real rates than by inflation expectations.

### Sector Favorites
Beyond commodities, Boockvar favors consumer staples. These stocks have been beaten down by volume and cost challenges but are now trading at attractive valuations. He sees a bottoming in fundamentals and potential for price increases to offset costs, making them a "boring" but profitable long-term hold.

## Conclusion

The current market environment is defined by persistent inflation, a complex bond supply-demand imbalance driven by AI capex, and significant leadership changes in key tech firms. Peter Boockvar advises investors to monitor real rates, the dollar, and the pace of AI infrastructure spending closely. While a December rate hike appears imminent, the long-term trajectory of yields remains uncertain, heavily influenced by whether the AI boom sustains its massive capital requirements or succumbs to economic and regulatory pressures.
