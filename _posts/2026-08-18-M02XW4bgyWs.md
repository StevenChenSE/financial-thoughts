## Economist Steve Hanke analyzes the US Treasury's historic yen intervention and the lessons of the 1997 Asian financial crisis

The recent intervention in the Japanese yen by the US Treasury marks a significant shift in global currency policy, occurring for the first time since the 1998 Asian financial crisis. Economist Steve Hanke, Professor of Applied Economics at Johns Hopkins University, provides a detailed analysis of why the US intervened and what the 1997 crisis reveals about the dangers of monetary policy. Hanke argues that the current instability is rooted in Japan's slow money supply growth and high debt-to-GDP ratio, which continue to pressure the yen despite temporary market support.

### The Mechanics of the Yen Intervention
On July 31st, the US Treasury and the Bank of Japan jointly intervened in the foreign exchange market to prevent a total collapse of the yen. This move was driven by the threat of a "carry trade" unwind, a scenario where investors borrow in low-yield yen to invest in higher-yield US assets. If the yen were to crash, these investors would be forced to sell US Treasuries to repay their debts, driving up US interest rates and destabilizing the global bond market.

Hanke notes that while the intervention caused a temporary "dead cat bounce," the underlying fundamentals of the Japanese economy have not changed. The yen has continued to depreciate due to two primary factors:
*   **Monetary Weakness:** Japan's M2 money supply is growing at a slow rate of approximately 2% per year, which is insufficient to meet the 2% inflation target and leads to stagnant nominal GDP growth.
*   **Fiscal Expansion:** The Japanese government is increasing spending, particularly on defense, which exacerbates an already record-high debt-to-GDP ratio.

Hanke suggests that the current state is an "unstable equilibrium," and further interventions may be necessary to maintain stability.

### Lessons from the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis
To understand the risks facing the yen, Hanke looks back at the 1997 crisis that began in Thailand. He identifies the root cause as the "deadly cocktail" of a pegged exchange rate and discretionary monetary policy. In Thailand and Indonesia, central banks attempted to defend their currency pegs while simultaneously expanding the domestic money supply. This led to a depletion of foreign exchange reserves and a loss of confidence, eventually triggering a currency collapse.

Hanke was personally involved in the Indonesian response as the chief advisor to President Suharto. While the IMF recommended floating the rupiah, which led to a catastrophic 85% collapse and social unrest, Hanke proposed a currency board system. This system, which he had previously implemented successfully in Bulgaria and Bosnia-Herzegovina, would fix the currency to a stable anchor and eliminate discretionary monetary policy.

### The IMF and the Currency Board Controversy
Despite the success of currency boards in other regions, the IMF and the US government opposed Hanke's proposal for Indonesia. Hanke argues that the opposition was not based on economic merit but on political and structural goals. He claims that the IMF, under the influence of the Clinton administration, wanted to push structural reforms that would have benefited US interests and that they viewed the currency board as a way for Indonesia to bypass these requirements.

Hanke points to several contradictions in the IMF's position:
1.  **The "Fake" Exchange Rate:** The IMF claimed the proposed peg rate was too aggressive, a figure Hanke states was a fabrication leaked by opponents.
2.  **Lender of Last Resort:** The IMF argued a currency board would prevent the central bank from acting as a lender of last resort. Hanke refutes this by noting that fiscal authorities can still provide such support.
3.  **Success in Bulgaria:** The IMF eventually admitted in its own reports that the currency board in Bulgaria was a "roaring success," yet they continue to discourage the practice in other countries.

### Why Hong Kong Survived
A key takeaway from the 1997 crisis is the resilience of Hong Kong. Because Hong Kong had a currency board (managed by the Hong Kong Monetary Authority) with its dollar fully backed by US dollar reserves, it was immune to the speculative attacks that destroyed other Asian currencies. George Soros, who famously shorted several Asian currencies, was unable to break the Hong Kong dollar because the system was structurally sound.

Hanke uses Hong Kong as a case study to illustrate that a currency board is one of two viable "free market" solutions for a struggling currency. The other option is a floating exchange rate combined with a strict monetary growth rule, such as the quantity theory of money approach advocated by Milton Friedman.

### Conclusion
The US intervention in the yen market is a temporary measure to address a structural problem. Hanke warns that without addressing the slow growth of the money supply and the high fiscal deficit, the yen will remain under pressure. He emphasizes that the 1997 crisis was a result of policy mistakes that could have been avoided through either a currency board or disciplined monetary growth. For Japan, the choice remains between these two free-market approaches to restore long-term stability to its currency.
