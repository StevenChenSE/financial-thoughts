The recent market turmoil has left many investors reeling, with the worst tech sell-off since the COVID-19 pandemic prompting comparisons to the dot-com bubble bursting 25 years ago. David Cervantes, managing partner at Pinebrook Capital Management, joins the discussion to dissect this "perfect storm" of rising rates, strong growth, and funding stresses that has caused a house of cards to crash down.

## The Perfect Storm: Rates, Growth, and Funding

Cervantes argues that the current market dislocation is the result of a specific cocktail of macroeconomic factors. In the spring, he predicted that rates would rise due to lags in energy markets following supply shocks from geopolitical conflicts like the war in Iran. By September, these predictions materialized as the 10-year Treasury yield hit intraday highs of 5.32%, significantly impacting the cost of capital for leveraged companies.

This spike in rates occurred alongside strong Q2 economic growth and persistent price pressures. As investors looked out on the risk curve, they realized that the cost of funding for highly leveraged tech firms had "moonshot." This coincided with the AI trade taking on a life of its own, where hyperscalers—companies like Meta and Google—are no longer flush with cash but are increasingly reliant on capital markets to fund massive infrastructure projects.

## The Hidden Risks in Tech Financing

A critical concern highlighted by Cervantes is the financing structure of major tech companies. He points to Meta’s special purpose vehicle (SPV), referred to as "Hyperion," which funds its Louisiana AI campus. Despite having an A rating on paper because it is backed by Meta, these bonds were trading like junk. This echoes the pre-global financial crisis era when subprime garbage was labeled AAA, creating a disconnect that markets eventually had to resolve.

The core issue for long-term investors is not necessarily next quarter's EPS, but rather the Return on Invested Capital (ROIC). While top-line revenue growth remains strong—Meta grew 26% year-over-year and Anthropic reported over 90% growth—the massive capital expenditures (CapEx) required to build out AI infrastructure are eroding free cash flow. If investors begin to question whether these monster projects will clear the hurdle rate, assets will re-rate sharply.

## The Fed’s Path: Independence and Hikes

Political dynamics are also shifting the monetary policy landscape. Cervantes notes that with President Trump politically wounded following recent election results, the Federal Reserve is likely to become more independent. This removes the political pressure on Fed Chair Warsh (or other officials) to moderate rates. Furthermore, Fed Governor Lisa Cook, who has faced removal attempts by the administration, may lean hawkish in response.

With nonfarm payrolls adding 165,000 jobs—beating expectations by nearly three times—and unemployment near record lows, the labor market remains tight. Cervantes predicts that inflation, particularly driven by energy costs and housing rents, will keep CPI elevated. Consequently, he expects the Fed to continue hiking rates, with a December hike still very much in play.

## Where Capital Is Rotating

As high-beta tech stocks face headwinds, institutional money has been rotating into defensive sectors. This rotation began in June, signaled by extreme divergence in core correlations within the healthcare sector (XLV). Healthcare and financials have caught bids as investors seek safety. However, Cervantes warns that healthcare is now stretched, trading at three standard deviations relative to the S&P 500, making it a risky short-term play despite its defensive nature.

For those seeking safety abroad, emerging markets have been hit hard due to their exposure to the AI trade (e.g., TSMC, Samsung). However, Europe may become attractive on a valuation basis once inflation pressures ease there. For domestic equity exposure, Cervantes suggests the equal-weight S&P 500 (RSP) as a "layup" trade to avoid concentration risk in mega-cap tech.

## Private Credit and Systemic Risks

A major concern for the financial system is the private credit sector. Post-2008 regulations pushed risky lending out of regulated banks into less transparent areas like private credit funds and private equity. If tech companies default or face liquidity crises, these entities will be left holding the bag. Cervantes bluntly states that funds may get "wiped out" or "zeroed out," but characterizes this as a natural consequence of capitalism rather than a systemic macro event. He advises watching the "trash spread"—the yield difference between the lowest-rated high-yield bonds and the broader index—as a leading indicator of funding stress. Currently, spreads at the lower end have been blowing out since July, signaling underlying weakness.

## What to Watch Next

Cervantes identifies two primary indicators that could change his allocation priorities:
1.  **The Energy Complex:** Not just crude oil prices, but refined products and shipping costs. Since energy is an input for everything from food transport to data center operations, persistent high energy costs will keep inflation sticky.
2.  **The Labor Market:** If unemployment begins to rise significantly (Cervantes expects it could drop to a "three-handle" low due to shrinking labor supply from immigration policies), it would force a reassessment of the hawkish Fed stance.

Ultimately, Cervantes maintains that we are still in a risk-on cycle, albeit one with significant rotations and repricing. The era of easy money for tech hyperscalers is ending, forcing a more disciplined evaluation of return on invested capital and funding viability.
