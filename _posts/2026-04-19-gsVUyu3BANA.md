**Renowned investor warns of coming "Greater Depression," urges preparation through gold, mining stocks, and geographic diversification**

---

The investment landscape is shifting dramatically, and according to Doug Casey, one of the most respected voices in resource investing, the old rules no longer apply.

"You can get a little bit more in the way of interest there," Casey acknowledged during a recent appearance on the Sore Financially podcast, addressing whether bonds might offer better returns than equities. "No, because I think interest rates are headed up, way up."

The founder of Casey Research and author of *The International Man* wasn't mincing words. After decades of artificially suppressed interest rates and unprecedented monetary expansion, Casey believes the financial system is approaching an inflection point that will fundamentally reshape investment portfolios worldwide.

"I think we can all agree that we're not living through normal times anymore," Casey said. "The normal cycle has ended. I think that's obvious."

## A World Gone Mad: Cultural Fragmentation and Its Consequences

Beyond market mechanics, Casey sees a civilization undergoing profound psychological upheaval—one that extends far beyond balance sheets and stock tickers.

"The world is going through a period of mass psychosis," Casey observed from his farm in Uruguay, where he's sheltering from "a lot of the noise out there." He drew historical parallels to the 30 Years War in Europe, the witch hysteria of the 17th century, and the turbulent period from 1929 to 1946.

"What's happening today isn't just in the markets but also in society culturally," Casey explained. "People in large parts of society are not thinking rationally and seem to have gone completely insane."

The most visible manifestation of this fragmentation, according to Casey, is the growing inability of Americans to communicate across political lines—even within families. The country has split into red and blue tribes aligned with Republicans and Democrats, separated by fundamentally different views of traditional values and Western civilization itself.

"I've been saying for at least a decade that the US is headed towards a civil war," Casey stated. "The US had a civil war between 1861 and 1865, but that wasn't actually a civil war. That was a war of secession, which is very different—people just wanted to separate."

Casey predicts this fragmentation will eventually reshape the map itself. "Maybe 100 years from now, the US as a geographical entity won't even exist in its present form. I think it's going to break up into sections. In fact, that's true of most countries in the world. They're artificial constructs. Most countries in the Middle East—including Iran—are artificial constructs. Most countries in Africa, most countries in South America, even in Europe."

The world, Casey suggests, has become like a kaleidoscope: "You can analyze what kind of crystals are in there, what color and composition, so you'd think you'd know how they'd fit together. But you turn the kaleidoscope an eighth of a turn, and it's totally different."

## The Iran Question: A Dangerous Path Forward

Turning to geopolitics, Casey addressed the ongoing tensions between the United States and Iran—a situation he finds both dangerous and diplomatically reckless.

"This really gets back to Trump, at least at the moment," Casey began. "I'd say he might be the most controversial US president in history."

While Casey acknowledged some positive aspects of Trump's agenda—including efforts against "wokism" and opposition to mass immigration—he characterized the current administration as "a loose cannon" that's "substituting its will for large parts of tradition, which makes him very dangerous."

"Iran is a country that Trump's never been to," Casey noted. "I almost went to Iran on a polo trip when I was living in New Zealand. I was a member of the New Zealand Ambassadors Polo Club and was invited to play in three cities in Iran. I couldn't do it because I was injured. But almost nobody's been in Iran in recent years. Nobody really knows what's going on there."

Casey expressed particular concern about the diplomatic approach: "The way Trump is talking, using all kinds of real profanity, is not what you're supposed to do when you're trying to establish diplomatic relations or even have an intelligent conversation with anybody."

The result, Casey believes, has been to unite Iran against what that country views as imperial threats. "This unprovoked sneak attack has made things worse, not better. I got a feeling this thing is not going to end. Not by any means. The prospect of World War is still at our door."

## The Real Reset: Smaller Government, Not Bigger Solutions

When asked about potential unifying figures or events that might heal the nation's divisions, Casey rejected the premise entirely.

"People being united is usually united along the lowest common denominator, and that's dangerous," Casey argued. "The way to solve this problem is not for the president to come up with his solutions, his programs, what needs to be done. Instead, he needs to abolish laws, regulations, and taxes wholesale."

Casey's prescription is fundamentally libertarian: "Each individual can more or less do what he'd like as long as he doesn't impinge upon other people's lives or property. We don't need a president with a program to unify the country. If people are united, they're probably going to be fighting somebody else."

The ideal government, in Casey's view, would be limited to two core functions: "Protect you from invasion from abroad, and protect you from violence from your neighbors. That's all—basically a police force and a court system to allow you to adjudicate disputes without resorting to force or violence."

"The rest of these agencies, ministries, laws, regulations could be abolished," Casey continued. "If that happened, you could radically cut taxes and improve people's standard of living and ability to create more wealth."

Casey expressed skepticism about the current administration's capacity for meaningful reform. "Trump is not a free market guy. He's a deist, as the French would say—he thinks he knows how to order things and is going to impose his will on the market or on the world if he can. He's much better than what the Democrats would have come up with. But it's like saying a heart attack is better than cancer."

## The Greater Depression: What It Means and How to Prepare

Casey has long predicted what he calls "the Greater Depression"—a downturn he describes as "much worse and much different and probably much longer-lasting than the unpleasantness of 1929 to 1946."

Defining his terms carefully, Casey explained: "A depression is a period of time when most people's standard of living drops significantly. A lot of the high standard of living we've had in the West has been because of debt. You borrow money, and if I borrow a million dollars now, I can live high off the hog for the next year—except I got to pay it back. So my standard of living goes way up when I borrow money. But then when I pay it back, it's going to go way down. Worse than that, I got to pay interest on it."

"And that's what's happened to the whole world," Casey continued. "The world is unbelievably, incredibly in debt. Either that debt is going to be paid back—which will reduce a lot of people's standard of living—or defaulted on—which is also going to reduce a lot of people's standard of living."

For individual investors, Casey's guidance is clear: forget traditional safe havens.

**On stocks:** "The stock market is really overpriced. By all conventional ways of judging what's cheap and what's dear, it's at extremes."

**On bonds:** "No. Because I think interest rates are headed up, way up. They're eventually going back to the levels we saw in the early 1980s, when even the US government was paying 15-16% for its money. People can't fathom that today. I think it'll happen again."

**On real estate:** "The problem with real estate is that it floats on a sea of debt. People can't buy it or sell it unless they can borrow money. That's a real problem when interest rates are going up."

Instead, Casey points to harder assets and strategic positioning.

## Gold, Silver, and Mining Stocks: The Case for Hard Assets

"I've been a gold bull for basically my entire investing life, and I've never sold any," Casey revealed. "Goes up, goes down, doesn't matter. I just kept buying more. Same with silver."

While Casey acknowledges that gold is no longer cheap by traditional metrics—"by any traditional parameters, gold is overpriced relative to the price of a car, a house, a suit of clothes, or a meal"—he sees compelling reasons for continued gains.

"Most people will think, 'Oh, that's ridiculous,'" Casey said of his price targets. "But I think it's true: Gold is going to be reinstituted as actual money, usable day-to-day money in the future. And governments are going to be forced to do it."

The catalyst, according to Casey, is the declining acceptance of the US dollar globally: "Nobody wants to accept the dollar anymore. If you're a foreign government or whatever, you don't want to accept the dollar—you realize you're holding the fiat currency of a bankrupt entity. And nobody wants to hold rubles or yuan—they're just fiat currencies by equally untrustworthy governments. So what are you going to use as money? If you're going to have a civilization, you need money. Gold has proven to have unique characteristics. It's the best money."

Beyond precious metals, Casey sees significant opportunity in mining stocks—a sector he describes as perpetually unloved despite its potential.

"Mining is a terrible business, incidentally," Casey admitted with characteristic candor. "But it's treated me well because it's so very volatile, and if you specialize in it—which nobody does—nobody's interested in playing in the dirt with big yellow machines. But it's good to be contrarian."

"I think we're in a bull market for mining stocks now, and I think that's going to last for quite a while."

## Energy: The Foundation of Civilization

Shifting to broader economic considerations, Casey emphasized the critical role of energy in maintaining—and improving—standards of living.

"Without cheap and abundant energy, we cannot advance," Casey stated. "Any society that has done really well over the last 100 or 150 years had access to cheap and abundant energy."

He drew attention to Europe's current predicament: "I'm based in Europe, generally in Germany, and we're far from cheap energy and abundance of energy right now. We can see our standards of living declining, slowly but surely."

Casey's energy thesis is unambiguous: nuclear power represents "by far the best solution to mass power generation."

"Nuclear, especially the new technologies of nuclear that are now coming online, is by far the safest, the cheapest, and the cleanest form of mass power generation," Casey asserted. "I don't think there's any question about that."

While Casey doesn't oppose solar and wind—"they're fun kids' toys, great for isolated or special situations"—he dismissed the notion that they can power an industrial economy: "You can't run an industrial economy on sporadic, sometimes energy that's also extremely expensive."

This leaves hydrocarbons as the practical foundation: "Oil and gas, and especially with further advances in drilling technology, will remain essential. It's concentrated energy, and you need that."

Casey expressed particular interest in coal: "Nobody's thinking about coal, but there's lots and lots of coal. Good coal stocks, especially in Australia. There's a lot of them. Also South Africa, which makes things dangerous because that country's going down the toilet. But you can get 8, 10, 12% in current dividends, and coal prices are going much higher along with oil and gas prices because of the political situation and because of the green hysteria. Those two things work together, actually."

## Technology and the Singularity: A Counterbalancing Force

Despite his grim economic outlook, Casey remains optimistic about technology's long-term trajectory.

"Technology will keep advancing, and that will stand to improve people's standard of living," Casey predicted. "I'm a big technophile, and ultimately, technology will save the day. The longest trend in human history is the advance of man—which is basically technology and learning how to control the world. It started with learning how to make fire, then the wheel, and things are advancing very quickly."

He cited futurist Ray Kurzweil's concept of the singularity with approval: "All these technological trends—computer science, biological science, robotics, space, nanotechnology—are coming together at once."

However, Casey tempered this enthusiasm with a crucial caveat: "Nothing's guaranteed, including in technology. You need a lot of capital to keep technology going, and a lot of the technology we have today is quite fragile. There are a lot of things that can derail technology for a decade or for a century."

He also took gentle issue with enthusiasts like Peter Diamandis, who predicts universal prosperity from robotics and AI: "He's a bit too enthusiastic in the short run."

## Practical Advice: Community and Self-Sufficiency

For listeners wondering how to position themselves for the coming turbulence, Casey offered practical guidance.

"When things get tough, which they're going to, you don't want to be in close proximity to a bunch of other people because they can go crazy," Casey advised. "In good times, it makes sense to be in a city because you can make connections and do things. It's easier to be creative. In bad times, it's better to be away from a city."

For those who can't relocate—bounded by family connections, social ties, or practical constraints—Casey emphasized the importance of community with like-minded individuals.

"I like hanging out with friends," Casey said. "The question is, what kind of people are your friends? I prefer people that believe in individualism and free markets and free thought and self-improvement."

"In the days of the American West, one individual could be the Lone Ranger, but that's not a long-term solution for survival. You need to find people that think the way you do or we do. That's important—to form a community with them."

Casey also stressed the importance of becoming valuable: "Put yourself in a position so that you're of value to yourself and to other people."

He returned to his latest book, *The Preparation*, which he wrote with Matt Smith: "It's about what a young man should do instead of going to college, which is a huge mistake—costly, corrupting, unnecessary. It's what you should do during those four critical years of your life, instead of being polluted and corrupted. And you should do it at any age."

The book currently holds 4.7 stars on Amazon.

## Looking Forward: Cautious Optimism

As the conversation drew to a close, Casey addressed the host—who is based in Germany—with direct advice.

"If I were you, I would make the chicken run," Casey said. "Why stay in Germany? You're surrounded by socialists. The country is rearming. All of Europe is rearming, doubling so-called defense budgets. Taxes are high, going higher. I'd bail out of Europe to start with."

Yet Casey maintained his characteristic wit and philosophical perspective: "Staying active is saying these things, not that it does any good to the world at large—but you have to amuse yourself, don't you?"

The host agreed, noting that these conversations help make sense of what's happening: "It helps put things in order. It forces me to think about stuff so we can have educated conversations."

"It's also a question of good karma," Casey responded. "You want to do things that you perceive as good and constructive."

Asked to end on a positive note, Casey summed up his worldview: "The best thing is to go back to what the US was founded on. A government that protects you from invasion and violence. The best thing for individuals is freedom—to do what you want as long as you don't harm others."

"We've drifted very far from that. But if we get back there, we'll be fine."
