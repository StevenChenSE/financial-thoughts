## Introduction

In a compelling interview on *Gabelli Talks*, host Danny sits down with Andy Hoese, founder of Finding Value Finance, to dissect a financial landscape that resembles a "dream setup" for commodities but a nightmare for traditional financial assets. Hoese argues that we are witnessing an unprecedented alignment of macroeconomic forces: severe supply constraints in oil, natural gas (LNG), and coal, colliding with a monetary system forced to print money to manage soaring national debt. This convergence is not merely cyclical; it represents a structural shift where the inability to transition between energy sources due to simultaneous shortages will trigger inflationary impulses that devalue currencies and reprice hard assets like gold, silver, and uranium.

## The Bond Market Breaking Point

The foundation of Hoese’s thesis lies in the current state of the US bond market. With the 10-year Treasury yield hovering above 5%, we have crossed a critical historical threshold. Historically, when yields surpass 5%, financial assets such as the S&P 500 begin to diverge from their previous upward trajectories, facing significant downward pressure.

Hoese projects that yields are likely to continue climbing, potentially reaching 6.3% to 6.4%. While there is speculation that the Federal Reserve and the Treasury might intervene through yield curve control (YCC) or quantitative easing (QE)—buying long-end bonds to artificially suppress rates—Hoese remains skeptical of their ability to sustain this control indefinitely. If they do succeed in holding yields down, the pressure will simply transfer to the currency markets, resulting in a weaker US Dollar (DXY). This dynamic creates a binary outcome for investors: either rates spike and choke the economy, or they are suppressed, leading to currency debasement. Both scenarios favor hard assets.

## The Great Rotation into Emerging Markets and Commodities

A key indicator of this shift is the movement in commodity currencies. Hoese points to breaking patterns in the Canadian Dollar (CAD), Australian Dollar (AUD), and Brazilian Real against the US Dollar. These are not isolated events but signals of smart money rotating out of dollar-denominated financial assets and into emerging markets and commodities.

This rotation mirrors the period between 2001 and 2011, where a weakening dollar fueled a massive commodity bull market. Currently, ratios such as Emerging Markets versus the S&P 500 are forming inverted head-and-shoulders patterns, suggesting a bottom is in place for international equities relative to US stocks. Hoese highlights specific opportunities, noting that Brazilian ETFs (EWZ) have broken out of long-term consolidations. The investment logic here is twofold: investors gain exposure to the underlying asset appreciation and benefit from the currency exchange rate differential as the dollar weakens against commodity-linked currencies.

## Energy Supply Shocks: Oil, Gas, and Coal

The core driver of this macro shift is a multi-front energy crisis. Hoese describes a scenario where global markets are short on all major forms of energy simultaneously, preventing easy substitution between fuels.

*   **Natural Gas (LNG):** European inventories are critically low ahead of winter. Furthermore, supply constraints in the Middle East due to damaged facilities are expected to persist for 3–5 years. TTF Dutch Natural Gas futures have already broken out, signaling tightness in the global LNG market.
*   **Coal:** With gas expensive and scarce, markets are reverting to coal. Newcastle coal futures show a classic inverted head-and-shoulder breakout pattern. Crucially, Hoese notes that China’s coal output is declining in volume terms, tightening supply further. When viewed against gold, coal is historically cheap, suggesting significant upside potential for energy equities.
*   **Oil:** Oil prices are sitting at the precipice of a major nominal breakout. Hoese utilizes fractal analysis to project that if oil follows the percentage moves of previous secular bull markets (such as those in the 1970s or early 2000s), prices could reach $650 per barrel by the 2030s, with some extreme models suggesting even higher figures.

The critical insight is that these shortages are interlinked. A shortage of LNG drives up coal; a shortage of coal and gas increases demand for oil. Because all three are constrained, there is no "off-ramp" to cheaper energy. This structural deficit ensures that energy prices remain elevated, driving inflationary pressure into the broader economy.

## Precious Metals: The Ultimate Hedge

While rising interest rates typically create headwinds for gold and silver in the short term, Hoese argues that the current environment is different. Gold and silver are currently undergoing a retest of previous breakouts, forming potential double-bottom patterns.

Hoese draws parallels to the 1970s, where precious metals consolidated while interest rates spiked, before exploding higher once the Fed was forced to intervene with money printing to save the bond market or stimulate a slowing economy. He posits that if the Fed implements yield curve control or aggressive QE to manage the debt spiral (now exceeding $40 trillion), the dollar will collapse. In this scenario, gold and silver will not just participate; they will "rocket."

Furthermore, Hoese notes an interesting correlation between oil, uranium, and silver. He suggests that as energy prices surge, these assets may move in tandem. If oil reaches $500–$600, silver and uranium could see similar nominal price levels due to their shared status as scarce, industrial-critical commodities.

## Housing Market Resilience vs. 2008 Crash

Contrary to the fear of a 2008-style housing crash, Hoese argues that the current market dynamics are fundamentally different. The 2008 crisis was driven by forced selling from homeowners with little to no equity who were underwater on bad loans. Today, homeowners have significant equity. Even with slowing sales and rising rates, there is little incentive for mass walk-aways or foreclosures because most owners are not financially trapped in the same way they were two decades ago.

Therefore, while a slowdown in home sales and construction is likely due to high rates, it will not result in a 30–40% collapse in home prices. Instead, if the Fed lowers rates to combat an economic slowdown driven by energy costs, Hoese predicts a boom in housing demand as buyers rush to refinance or purchase at lower rates, further devaluing the dollar and boosting asset prices.

## Conclusion: The Asymmetric Opportunity

Andy Hoese concludes that investors are currently facing a rare "alignment of moons." We have the tailwinds of a commodity bull market, monetary expansion due to debt constraints, and severe physical supply shortages in energy. This setup favors hard assets—energy stocks, gold, silver, uranium, and emerging markets—over US financial assets.

The strategy is clear: position for a world where money loses purchasing power because the real economy (energy) cannot keep up with monetary creation. Whether rates rise (causing deflationary pressure on stocks but inflationary pressure on commodities via supply shocks) or are suppressed (devaluing currency and boosting all hard assets), the long-term trajectory points toward significant gains in the commodity and precious metals sectors. As Hoese notes, "We win either way."
