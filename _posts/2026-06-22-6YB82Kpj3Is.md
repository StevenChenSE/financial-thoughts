## Thomas Lamb on the Future of Precious Metals and the Hidden Forces Shaping Commodity Prices

The global commodity markets are experiencing unprecedented dynamics, and for investors holding gold, silver, and copper, understanding the underlying forces has never been more crucial. Thomas Lamb, CEO of J2 Metals—an exploration mining company focused on three of the world's most important metals—shares his insights on where these markets are heading and the factors most investors are overlooking.

---

## The Gold Market: Beyond the Textbook

Gold has served as humanity's primary hedge against inflation and store of value for thousands of years. Yet according to Lamb, the metal's traditional relationship with economic indicators has fundamentally changed since around 2010.

"Gold should go up when inflation heats up and interest rates come down," Lamb explains. "It should also perform well in times of trouble. But I'm wondering if the store of value thesis has now disconnected."

Lamb's perspective comes from unique experience. During his career, he sat on the board of Uzhuralzoloto (U Gold), Russia's second largest gold producer, gaining firsthand exposure to how major producers view and manipulate the gold market.

"Being on the front lines of this decoupling, you see interesting dynamics," he notes. "On one side, Bitcoin has emerged as an alternative for some investors. Producers are also leaning into demand, selling gold to capture profits while prices are high. Meanwhile, Russia's significant gold reserves—potentially being sold to fund conflicts—are possibly suppressing prices."

Despite these current headwinds, Lamb remains in contact with European economic experts from the Austrian school of economics who maintain absolute conviction that gold's day is coming.

"We've been through this before," they tell him. "In Europe, we've experienced World War II and hyperinflation before that. We really believe gold is coming."

---

## Silver: The Industrial Metal Poised for Dramatic Gains

Unlike gold, silver's bull case rests not on monetary fundamentals but on industrial demand—and that case is exceptionally strong.

"Silver is an industrial metal now, principally," Lamb states. "And you cannot dial up production of silver. There are not that many silver primary mines in the world. It's a co-product often."

The supply constraint meets surging demand, particularly from China, where solar panel manufacturing, battery production, and electric vehicle adoption are accelerating rapidly.

"You see demand really going up," Lamb observes. "But supply cannot follow. So then you see the prices go up."

Lamb works with traders on the front lines of commodity markets who share an extremely bullish outlook on silver.

"They really believe silver has nowhere to go except up dramatically," he says with evident conviction.

The recent price decline puzzles even these market veterans, highlighting the mysterious forces at play in current markets.

---

## Copper: The Electrification Metal

Copper demand is surging as the world electrifies, and Lamb sees the price continuing to rise—but with important caveats.

"The world is electrifying," he explains. "You have data centers and AI centers. The world continues to build cities. You've got fast-growing and advancing population in India, electrification there, and big build-outs in China."

However, the economics of copper production are becoming increasingly challenging. High-grade, large-scale copper mines have largely been discovered and exploited. What remains are the "halo" deposits—lower-grade material surrounding existing mines.

"The price of copper has to come up for us to go get it," Lamb acknowledges. "That requires higher and higher copper prices. So everybody in our sector is very bullish on copper."

---

## Oil: The Hidden Cost Killing Mining Margins

Perhaps the most underappreciated factor affecting mining profitability is energy costs, specifically diesel prices.

"Oil has spiked dramatically since the end of February, from $60 to $120, now back down to $76," Lamb notes. "But diesel has doubled. Maybe even gone up by two and a half times."

For mining operations in remote locations—from the Sahara Desert to Eastern Congo—diesel isn't just an expense; it's often the primary operational constraint.

"Remote places need diesel," Lamb emphasizes. "Everyone installs solar and other alternatives as much as possible, but definitely diesel gets extremely expensive, especially in really remote areas."

The real killer, according to Lamb, is volatility. Mining projects require bank financing of $100 million to $1 billion, with complex structures that demand accurate oil price forecasting. When oil volatility is high, planning becomes nearly impossible.

---

## The Information Warfare Problem

Lamb raises a troubling concern that extends far beyond traditional market analysis: the breakdown of reliable information in an era of disinformation and propaganda.

"Imagine being a mining company trying to price your diesel," he illustrates. "You have to understand whether Hormuz is blocked. Some tankers are getting through, it turns out, but you don't know how many."

During the Gulf War, journalists on the ground provided relatively accurate battlefield reporting. Today, that transparency has vanished. Tankers transiting the Strait of Hormuz have their transponders disabled. The US has made secret deals with various nations, creating a fog of uncertainty around basic supply facts.

"If you say there's a blockade and it's closed, oil should be $130-$140," Lamb calculates. "But it's not, for some reason. We're getting very inaccurate information right now. This makes it really hard to price."

This information problem isn't limited to the Middle East. From Africa to authoritarian jurisdictions worldwide, the inability to verify ground-level conditions affects every aspect of resource sector planning.

"This is why fact-based political environments and reality are so important," Lamb argues. "This impacts the mining sector across the globe."

## How J2 Metals Navigates the Chaos

As a smaller exploration company on the TSXV with approximately $8 million market cap, J2 Metals faces these global challenges differently than major producers.

"At our stage, the exercise is acquire very high-quality prospective projects and then reduce your cost of capital to explore them and advance them," Lamb explains. "We don't have to navigate energy prices per se. We encounter second and third order effects."

Those second-order effects are significant. When oil prices spike due to Middle East conflicts, the cascade is predictable: global economic slowdown, layoffs in the Indian subcontinent, transportation costs consuming African workers' daily income, and ultimately, precipitous drops in commodity prices.

"So all these knock-on effects have results," Lamb observes. "You end up with a precipitous drop in commodity prices."

Yet despite the short-term volatility, the long-term thesis for precious metals and copper remains compelling. As Lamb's European contacts remind him, history suggests these metals have an important role still to play—and the fundamental supply-demand dynamics for silver and copper suggest the bull case may be just beginning.
