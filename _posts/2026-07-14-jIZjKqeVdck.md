## Gavekal CEO Discusses Commoditization of AI, Energy Margins, and Geopolitical Shifts

**Subtitle:** Gavekal CEO Louis Gave joins Dan Nathan and Peter Bookbar to discuss the commoditization of AI, the explosion of refining margins, structural risks in bond markets, and the geopolitical shift toward strategic stockpiling.

### Introduction

In a wide-ranging discussion on the *Risk Reversal Podcast*, Louis Gave, CEO of Gavekal, shared his insights with hosts Dan Nathan and Peter Bookbar. Gave, whose firm has operated in Asia since 1997, dissected the evolving dynamics of the global economy, focusing on the divergent AI strategies between the US and China, the new scarcity in energy refining, the structural bear market in bonds, and the reconfiguration of geopolitical power. Gave emphasized that while the US builds "fortresses" in technology, China is forced by chip bans to build "Dubai," fostering open-source ecosystems that threaten to commoditize AI models and semiconductors.

### AI Strategy: Fortresses vs. Dubai

Gave highlighted a fundamental divergence in AI development philosophies. US companies like **OpenAI** and **Anthropic** are building "medieval fortresses" with closed ecosystems, targeting high-end, exclusive models akin to selling Ferraris. In contrast, China, blocked from accessing advanced chips by US export controls, has adopted an open-source approach. Gave likened China's strategy to building Dubai: an open environment where everyone can enter, exit, and modify the technology.

This shift was necessitated by the US chip ban. Historically, Chinese companies relied on throwing capital and human resources at problems. Unable to do so in AI hardware, they turned to open-sourcing models and leveraging global software developers to improve products. Gave noted that Chinese AI models are rapidly gaining ground, with 60% of top models now originating from China. The strategy is to deliver reliable, cost-effective solutions—"Toyotas" or **BYDs**—rather than premium "Ferraris," appealing to the global market, especially bootstrapped startups and emerging economies.

Gave pointed to companies like **CXMT** and **Yangtze Memory Technology** as threats to US semiconductor dominance, noting that Chinese firms prioritize market share over profit, which pressures margins for companies like **Micron**. He also warned about the sustainability of US hyperscaler spending. While **Alibaba** guided for $70 billion in capex over three years, US hyperscalers are spending $700 billion annually. Gave argued that the market is not pricing in the commoditization that typically follows Chinese entry into a sector. Furthermore, he expressed concern over **OpenAI** deferring its IPO to 2027, noting the company is "too big to fail" within the ecosystem but faces a critical need for equity capital as private funding dries up.

### Energy and Refining: The New Scarcity

Turning to energy, Gave described the current environment as an "inflationary boom" driven by massive government deficits and scarcity. While crude oil prices have stabilized, with China acting as a swing buyer at prices below $65 and pausing purchases above $100, the real story lies in refining. Crack spreads have blown out due to multiple factors:

1.  **Refinery Destruction:** Continuous bombing of refineries in Russia and Ukraine, combined with attacks during the Iran conflict that took offline major facilities in **Bahrain** and **Jaffa**.
2.  **China's Export Halt:** China has stopped exporting refined products, retaining them for domestic use and leveraging its massive storage capacity, estimated at 1.8 billion barrels.
3.  **Lack of New Capacity:** Global refining capacity has stagnated for two decades due to the perceived transition to electric vehicles.

Gave noted that refiners like **Marathon Petroleum** are trading at all-time highs with enormous free cash yields, funding massive share buybacks rather than new capacity. He argued that refining is the new scarcity play, following energy and semiconductors, and that the "happy days" for energy companies persist within a $65 to $100 oil price range.

### Geopolitics and the Dollar

Gave offered a critical view of recent geopolitical maneuvers, including a peace deal signed at **Versailles**, which he viewed skeptically given the historical failures of treaties signed there. He argued that the US removal of Iranian leadership fragmented command structures, empowering regional commanders and the **IRGC**, complicating negotiations and increasing the risk of asymmetric attacks.

On the currency front, Gave challenged the reliance on the **DXY** (Dollar Index), calling it an outdated benchmark weighted heavily against the Euro and Yen. He observed that the **Renminbi** has been strengthening steadily, while Asian currencies are increasingly managed against the Yuan rather than the Dollar. Gave highlighted China's progress in developing its own payment system, **CIPS**, and settling over half its trade in its own currency. He also noted that the Hong Kong peg serves China as a "tokenized" US dollar, offering flexibility in trade negotiations without downside risk.

Gave suggested that the **Mag 7** stocks have effectively become a global reserve asset, propping up the dollar. If the AI trade rolls over, he believes the dollar could face significant downward pressure, particularly as foreign ownership of US assets remains high but vulnerable to shifts in sentiment.

### Bond Markets and Fiscal Risks

The discussion shifted to the "structural bond bear market," driven by persistent inflation and fiscal indiscipline. Gave pointed out that the US is running budget deficits of 7% of GDP at full employment, a unsustainable trajectory that risks an "inflationary bust" if shocks occur.

A major risk factor is the "demographic sword" of Japanese investors. Japan holds $3.5 trillion in foreign assets, equivalent to 80% of GDP. As **JGB** yields rise and the yen weakens, Gave warned that Japanese investors may eventually repatriate capital, triggering a sell-off in global bonds. He emphasized that the true danger in debt crises is not debt-to-GDP ratios but the percentage of debt owned by foreigners. Countries like France and the UK, with high foreign debt ownership, face greater vulnerability than the US, though the US is not immune to shifts in foreign demand.

Gave predicted a steepening yield curve, with central banks keeping short rates low to manage debt rollovers while the long end sells off. This environment favors financials, which have been breaking out globally, over stretched tech valuations.

### Commodities and Strategic Stockpiles

Gave argued that the lessons from recent conflicts are reshaping how nations approach security. The US no longer controls global sea lanes due to the cost asymmetry between billion-dollar ships and $10,000 drones. This reality is forcing countries to prioritize strategic stockpiles of critical commodities.

India's need for fertilizer during the Gulf crisis highlighted the vulnerability of relying on treasuries and gold when physical supply chains are disrupted. Gave noted that countries are now recognizing the need to stockpile essentials like fertilizer, oil, rare earths, lithium, and natural gas. This shift is inflationary, as rebuilding inventories and constructing storage facilities requires massive amounts of steel, plastic, and other materials.

Gold remains a hedge against the erosion of the rule of law, as evidenced by the seizure of Russian assets. However, Gave is more bullish on physical commodities, citing the US Department of Defense's interest in stockpiling lithium and Germany's plans for a natural gas reserve. The perception that allies may not always be reliable is driving a return to self-sufficiency and strategic reserves.

### Market Outlook

In conclusion, Gave outlined his market preferences based on fundamentals, momentum, positioning, and valuations. He views tech as stretched with extreme positioning and stalling momentum. In contrast, he favors **financials** due to the steepening yield curve and attractive valuations, and **cyclicals** which benefit from a potential weakening dollar. He advised against consumer cyclicals, which lack momentum.

Gave also noted the divergence between **S&P 500** earnings and national income, attributing the gap to "other income" from investments and semiconductor gains. He warned that under the hood, earnings growth is more modest than headline figures suggest. Looking to Japan as a monetary policy trailblazer, Gave expects central banks to drag their feet on rate hikes, keeping short rates low to support debt management, further supporting the case for financials and cyclicals in the current macro environment.
