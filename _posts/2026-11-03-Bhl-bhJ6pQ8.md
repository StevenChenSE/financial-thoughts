In a recent episode of *Capital Calls*, host Danny spoke with Chris Macintosh from the *Capitalist Exploits Insider* newsletter about the turbulent economic landscape facing global markets. With WTI oil prices exceeding $100 and geopolitical tensions rising, Macintosh issued a dire warning regarding the impending "meltdown" driven by stagflation. Central to his thesis is a massive consolidation in the offshore drilling sector—specifically the merger between Transocean and Valaris—which he argues heralds the start of a significant bull cycle in energy infrastructure.

## The Stagflationary Reality: Why Deflation Is a Myth

Macintosh challenges the prevailing market narrative that expects a deflationary outcome. Instead, he posits that the global economy is firmly entrenched in a **stagflationary** environment. This condition is characterized by two simultaneous forces:

1.  **Monetary Debasement:** Central banks are inflating the money supply to manage debt levels that the underlying economy cannot support.
2.  **Supply Chain Disruption:** Shortages of minerals and goods are driving up prices for underlying commodities.

Macintosh explains that while the broader economy may weaken, prices for essential goods will continue to rise. He draws a parallel to Zimbabwe in the 2000s, where a collapsing economy coexisted with the best-performing local stock market because investors fled into assets of value to escape currency devaluation. Governments cannot allow true deflation because it would overwhelm their ability to service existing federal debt; therefore, currency devaluation is the inevitable policy response.

## Maslow’s Hierarchy and Consumer Prioritization

A key component of this stagflationary thesis is how consumer behavior shifts when paychecks fail to keep up with inflation. Macintosh references **Maslow’s hierarchy of needs**, noting that consumers will cut discretionary spending first.

*   **Discretionary Cuts:** Spending on restaurants, disposable goods, and non-essential services will decline sharply.
*   **Essential Retention:** Consumers will prioritize "food, shelter, and energy." People may skip a night out, but they will not turn off their lights or stop taking hot showers.

This structural shift creates a powerful tailwind for the energy sector while creating headwinds for consumer goods and leveraged assets like housing and private credit.

## The Transocean-Valaris Merger: A Consolidation of Power

The focal point of Macintosh’s current investment thesis is the acquisition of **Transocean** by **Valaris**. This merger represents a dramatic consolidation in the offshore drilling industry.

*   **Market Share:** The combined entity will own roughly **40% of all global offshore rigs**.
*   **ETF Analogue:** Macintosh describes this new company as effectively acting like an "ETF" for the entire offshore rig space, providing investors with direct exposure to rising day rates and tightening supply.

Both hosts disclosed that they currently hold positions in these companies, emphasizing that their conviction is based on long-term structural changes rather than short-term speculation.

## Supply Constraints and Aging Fleets

The offshore energy sector faces a unique set of constraints that limit the ability of competitors to respond to rising demand:

1.  **Capital Intensity:** The industry requires massive capital deployment over 20-30 year timeframes, traditionally financed by debt.
2.  **Debt Market Closure:** ESG narratives and punitive measures against fossil fuels have starved the sector of capital for years.
3.  **Rising Interest Rates:** Uncertainty regarding long-term interest rates makes financing new rigs prohibitively difficult.
4.  **Aging Infrastructure:** The average age of a **jackup rig** is now 22 years, approaching the end of its typical 30-year lifespan. Scrapping these aging assets creates a desperate need for new supply.
5.  **Low Order Books:** New build order books are at their lowest levels since 2014.

Despite these constraints, **day rates** (the daily fee for renting a rig) are soaring. This indicates that the existing fleet is highly profitable, yet management teams—scarred by the previous bear cycle—are reticent to deploy capital into new builds or acquisitions, preferring to return cash to shareholders or reinvest conservatively.

## The Decline of Shale and the Rise of Offshore

For the past four decades, U.S. shale has been the primary source of additional global oil supply. However, Macintosh notes that **shale basins are now in decline**, with tier-one assets largely drilled out. As shale production wanes, offshore energy becomes relatively more attractive.

There are significant known offshore resources that were stranded during the price crashes of 2014–2016. Many of these assets can be developed at breakeven costs of **$20–$40 per barrel**, offering huge profit potential in a high-price environment. However, unlocking these resources requires deploying expensive rigs and offshore support vessels (OSVs), which remains constrained by the capital issues mentioned above.

## Geopolitical Risks and Resource Nationalism

The global energy war is intensifying. Macintosh highlights risks such as:
*   **Iranian Retaliation:** Potential attacks on undersea cables or Gulf energy infrastructure, which would be inherently inflationary.
*   **European Conflict:** The ongoing war with Russia has severe implications for currency units and debt markets in Europe.
*   **Resource Nationalism:** Governments are increasingly viewing critical resources as matters of national security, leading to potential nationalization of production assets.

However, Macintosh argues that drilling contractors and service providers offer a hedge against some of these risks. Unlike oil producers whose wells are fixed in specific jurisdictions (e.g., Iran or Saudi Arabia), drill ships and pipeline manufacturers are mobile and less tied to single geographic locations. Even state-owned entities like Venezuela’s **PDVSA** rely on private contracting firms like Chevron for technology and execution, suggesting that service providers remain resilient even under nationalist regimes.

## Strategic Positioning: Trading Houses and Warehousing

Beyond direct energy plays, Macintosh discusses the importance of **trading houses** and **warehousing**. In a world shifting from "just-in-time" to "just-in-case" inventory management due to supply chain fragility, stockpiling becomes essential.

*   **Trading Houses:** Act as brokers matching buyers and sellers (e.g., copper mines in Chile with demand in Asia), often insulated from direct operational risks via insurance contracts.
*   **Warehousing:** Benefits from the need to store excess inventory when delivery times stretch from weeks to months.

Macintosh emphasizes that while the thesis for these sectors is sound, valuation remains critical. Investors must assess whether assets are trading at reasonable multiples or if they are already overvalued (e.g., 100x earnings).

## Conclusion

The consensus view among Macintosh and his team is that the world is moving toward a severe energy shortage exacerbated by monetary debasement and geopolitical conflict. The **Transocean-Valaris merger** serves as a catalyst for the offshore drilling sector, which stands to benefit from soaring day rates, constrained supply, and the decline of U.S. shale. While risks such as nationalization and global instability exist, the structural asymmetry in the offshore energy market offers compelling opportunities for those positioned ahead of the curve. As Macintosh concludes, "Wars are always inflationary," and the market has yet to fully price in the stagflationary reality facing the global economy.
