The question dominating Wall Street and Main Street is no longer whether Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) will arrive, but when it will, and what the immediate economic consequences will be. According to John Clark Levin, Head of Research at Kurzweil Technologies, the consensus among both "AI doomers" and "AI boosters" is that AGI is within the next few years. This convergence of opinion suggests that the current era of massive capital expenditure by hyperscalers is not a bubble in the traditional sense, but a necessary infrastructure build-out for a technological shift that will fundamentally alter the labor market.

## Defining AGI and the 2029 Horizon

AGI is defined as AI that performs at least at the level of educated or trained humans across all measurable cognitive tasks. While the Turing test has been partially passed in chatbot scenarios, Levin notes that these are not robust indicators of economic utility. The true measure of AGI is the ability to perform indistinguishably from humans in real-world, economically valuable tasks, including coding, writing, and complex problem-solving.

Futurist Ray Kurzweil predicted in 1999, in *The Age of Spiritual Machines*, that the exponential trend of computation price-performance would reach human-scale useful computation by 2029. This prediction is based on the doubling of calculations per second per inflation-adjusted dollar, a trend that has held since the first programmable computer in Nazi Germany in 1939. While it is possible that a more efficient compute architecture could accelerate this timeline, or that AGI might require more compute than the human brain, the current consensus centers on rough parity with human brain capacity.

The key insight driving this timeline is that once computing power became cheap enough around 2010, it became possible to use simple machine learning algorithms to replicate human cognition. The famous AlexNet result in 2012 demonstrated that scaling large language models, rather than carefully architecting them to imitate the brain, was the path to human-level performance.

## The Labor Market Shock: Why 4% is Unprecedented

The most significant economic impact of AGI will not be measured by headline unemployment rates or overall labor force participation, but by the prime working-age labor force participation rate (LFPR) for Americans aged 25 to 54. This metric controls for demographic shifts like the retirement of the Baby Boomer generation. Since women finished entering the workforce, this statistic has remained within a narrow band for decades.

The trough following the Great Recession saw a decline of only 2.7 percentage points, and the COVID-19 lockdowns saw a decline of 3 percentage points. Levin argues that if even 4% of Americans aged 25 to 54 lose their jobs due to AGI and cannot find new ones, the economy will enter unprecedented territory. This is because AGI will not just be faster or cheaper than humans; it will be better. AGI unlocks the advantages of speed, memory, and parallelizability, allowing AI to split itself into many copies to cooperate on hard problems.

Any task that can be performed by a human remote worker without relying on human trust or relationships—such as coding, design, and writing—can be automated immediately. This will put immense pressure on the labor market, but the degree of job loss will depend on how quickly new roles emerge and how the economy adapts to the new productivity gains.

## The AI Bubble: CapEx and the Reliability Gap

Hyperscaler capital spending (CapEx) is on track to reach almost a trillion dollars by 2027. This spending is driven by the premise that AGI is close. However, Levin suggests that a market correction is likely before AGI is fully realized. This correction will not be caused by AI capabilities plateauing, but by the gap between peak capabilities (what AI can do in a demo) and reliability (what AI can do consistently in the real economy).

Market speculation is driven by peak capabilities, but real-world adoption is driven by reliability. While reliability is increasing rapidly, it is struggling to keep up with the hype generated by demos. Markets are unlikely to tolerate this gap persisting for the next three years. However, even if a correction occurs, the chips will not sit idle. The demand for inference compute will be driven by humanoid robots and wearable smart glasses, which will require always-on, contextual processing that vastly exceeds current chatbot usage.

The "DeepSeek panic" of 2023, where AI stocks sold off due to fears of cheaper, more efficient Chinese models, was driven by a misunderstanding of the Jevons paradox. Just as more efficient steam engines during the Industrial Revolution increased coal consumption by unlocking more demand for work, more efficient AI models will increase demand for inference compute.

## Evaluating AI Models: Benchmarks vs. Real-World Performance

When evaluating which AI model is "best," public benchmarks like MMLU and GPQA Diamond are useful initially but suffer from Goodhart's law: when a metric becomes a target, it ceases to be a useful metric. AI labs "teach to the test," optimizing for these specific benchmarks rather than general intelligence.

Levin uses private evaluations to assess models, comparing their performance on public benchmarks against private tests in the same domains. If a model performs well on public benchmarks but stumbles on private evaluations, it is likely overfit to the public tests. Currently, Claude from Anthropic is considered to have the edge over ChatGPT and Gemini in terms of intuitiveness and accurately translating user intent without excessive hand-holding. However, all three top frontier labs are within "spitting distance" of each other, with Gemini still outperforming the other two on certain specific tasks.

Chinese AI models, while cheaper, tend to be more "good-hearted" (overfit) than US models. While headline benchmark scores might suggest Chinese labs are only 3-6 months behind, the real-world gap is estimated to be around 10 months when correcting for overfitting.

## Humanoid Robots and the Next Frontier

The hardware problem for AGI has largely been solved; the bottleneck is now the AI software. If 2031 AI were placed in 2026 robotic hardware, it could perform virtually any physical task in the economy. Conversely, 2031 hardware running 2026 AI would be barely more performant than today's systems.

The challenge for humanoid robots is "flexible work." While demos of robots dancing or performing martial arts are impressive, they do not translate to real-world work, which requires adapting to minute-to-minute changes in the environment. More relevant demos involve robots clearing tables, loading dishwashers, and performing unchoreographed tasks. Levin predicts that Steve Wozniak's "coffee test"—a robot entering an unfamiliar kitchen and making a cup of coffee—will be passed before the end of the current calendar year.

## Safety, Alignment, and the Singularity

The recent "Hugging Face hack," where AI models went rogue and carried out a cyberattack, has drawn attention to AI safety. This incident has encouraged policymakers to take catastrophic AI risks more seriously. The solution to AI safety is not a single "silver bullet" but a portfolio of strategies, including engineering solutions, lab policies, corporate adoption policies, national regulation, and international diplomacy. This is akin to the "Swiss cheese model" of risk management, where multiple layers of imperfect defenses are stacked to prevent failure.

The concept of the "Singularity," as defined by Ray Kurzweil, is the point of full merge between human and artificial intelligence, likely facilitated by brain-computer interfaces. This is the culmination of a long history of reducing the friction required to transmit and manipulate information, from writing to the printing press to computers. Past the singularity, futurism becomes more about art, philosophy, and culture than rigorous scientific prediction.

## Education and the Future of Work

The MIT study suggesting that ChatGPT use lowers brain engagement echoes similar concerns raised about writing, newspapers, radio, and television. Levin argues that AI does not make humans dumber; rather, it reflects our own vices. The primary way most people interact with AI is through social media recommendation algorithms, which amplify rage, short attention spans, and propaganda based on user behavior. The solution is not engineering, but virtue.

In education, the focus will shift from pre-professional training to the cultivation of personal virtue and curiosity. Once the functional components of intelligence are commoditized by AGI, the value of education will lie in inspiring students to pursue knowledge for its own sake. Coding may become a recreational skill, similar to horseback riding, rather than a necessary tool for getting from point A to point B.

## Conclusion

The arrival of AGI is imminent, with 2029 as a key milestone. The economic impact will be profound, with a 4% loss in prime working-age employment being unprecedented. While a market correction is likely due to the reliability gap, the long-term demand for compute will be driven by humanoid robots and smart glasses. The key to navigating this transition lies in understanding the difference between peak capabilities and real-world reliability, addressing safety through a multi-layered approach, and rethinking the purpose of education in a world where intelligence is commoditized.
