## Introduction

In a recent discussion on *Capitalist Exploits*, host Danny and guest Chris Macintosh, publisher of the *Capitalist Exploits Insider* newsletter, dissected the current state of the energy markets. While much of the market’s attention is fixated on artificial intelligence and tech valuations, Macintosh argues that the most compelling asymmetric investment opportunity lies in a sector many have written off: offshore drilling. With the recent merger news involving Valaris and Transocean, Macintosh contends that we are witnessing the beginning of a new supercycle characterized by supply constraints, margin expansion, and significant capital return potential.

## The Dynamics of Mergers and Acquisitions in Market Cycles

To understand why the current moment is unique, one must first understand how mergers and acquisitions (M&A) function within market cycles. Macintosh identifies two distinct phases where M&A activity typically peaks:

1.  **At the Top of the Cycle:** Here, equity valuations are overhyped. Management teams, often compensated via stock options tied to share price rather than operational profitability, utilize inflated equity to acquire other companies. This behavior is driven by narrative and "bullshittery" rather than fundamental cash flow generation. A classic example cited was the Time Warner era dynamics where acquisitions were rewarded by the market despite questionable underlying value creation.
2.  **At the Bottom of the Cycle:** Conversely, at the trough, shareholders are disillusioned after significant losses, and debt holders may have become equity holders through recapitalizations. Despite this pessimism, fundamentals often begin to improve—revenues rise, margins expand, and profitability returns—but the market remains skeptical due to past trauma.

The current offshore drilling scenario fits squarely into the second category. After a brutal period of bankruptcy and capital destruction from 2016 through 2020, the sector is now seeing consolidation not because valuations are inflated, but because it makes strategic sense for remaining players to combine forces as fundamentals improve.

## The Valaris-Transocean Merger: Creating a Giant

A pivotal event signaling this shift is the reported merger between **Valaris** and **Transocean**. Macintosh describes this union as creating "easily the largest offshore driller" in the world, controlling roughly **40% of the drill rigs on the planet**.

This consolidation represents a critical inflection point. In previous cycles, management might have been incentivized to build new rigs at the top of the market. Today, however, no one is adding significant supply. The industry has been deleveraged through intense capital destruction, and there is currently **no order book** for new rigs. This lack of new capacity creates a powerful moat around existing players, granting them immense pricing power in a market where demand is recovering but supply remains constrained.

## Supply Constraints and the Shale Pivot

The offshore sector’s current strength is also underpinned by changes in the broader energy landscape. During the last downturn, capital fled offshore projects in favor of **shale oil**, particularly in the Permian Basin. Shale was favored because it offered a shorter cycle—energy could be extracted more rapidly and at lower costs than deepwater alternatives.

However, that narrative has shifted. Macintosh notes that:
*   Tier-one shale assets are largely drilled out.
*   Major basins are entering decline phases.
*   The market is facing an energy crisis that requires new supply sources.

With shale growth slowing and global demand for energy persisting, the reliance on offshore drilling has returned. Yet, because the industry spent the last decade deleveraging and writing down assets, there are very few participants left. This scarcity of operators means that those who remain—like Valaris, Transocean, and a handful of others—are positioned to capture disproportionate value as day rates increase.

## The Tanker Stock Parallel: A Case Study in Patience

Macintosh draws a direct parallel between the current offshore drilling opportunity and the recent performance of tanker stocks, specifically **Frontline (FRO)**. *Capitalist Exploits* began accumulating positions in tanker stocks around 2019, a time when the sector was deeply unloved.

The journey to profitability was not linear. Investors had to stomach:
*   A roughly **50% drawdown** starting around 2020.
*   Another **30-40% drawdown** after July 2024, leading many to believe the high-water mark had passed.

Despite these volatility spikes, the thesis held true. Frontline and other tanker stocks have delivered returns in the range of **8.5x to 9x**, or approximately **8,000%** when dividends are reinvested. The key to capturing this return was not timing every dip, but maintaining a position size that allowed for psychological comfort during drawdowns and having the patience to let the cycle play out over several years.

Macintosh suggests that offshore drillers will likely follow a similar trajectory. While he does not predict immediate gains ("Doubt it" happens tomorrow), he posits that over the next five to six years, the sector offers massive upside potential for those who can tolerate short-term noise and volatility.

## Asymmetric Investing and Portfolio Management

The core of Macintosh’s advice centers on **asymmetric investing**—limiting downside risk while seeking significant upside. He emphasizes that this is not a "put all your money in" recommendation. Instead, it requires careful portfolio management:
*   **Position Sizing:** Allocating 5-15% to a high-risk/high-reward theme makes drawdowns psychologically manageable compared to a 30-40% allocation.
*   **Patience:** The edge lies in long-term views that contradict short-term market sentiment. Short-term price movements (over months or half-years) are considered noise.
*   **Dividend Reinvestment:** Compounding returns through dividend reinvestment is crucial for maximizing gains in these cyclical sectors.

While *Capitalist Exploits* looks for 10%+ dividends, Macintosh notes that offshore drillers may not reach those specific yield targets immediately. However, the current valuations are "extraordinarily cheap," and he anticipates that post-merger management will likely focus on **buybacks and dividends** to return capital to shareholders, as building new rigs is not currently necessary or financially prudent given the existing capacity constraints.

## Conclusion

The convergence of a completed deleveraging cycle, a lack of new supply (no order book), improving fundamentals, and strategic M&A activity creates a unique setup for offshore drillers. As capital potentially rotates out of the overheated AI sector into value-oriented industries like energy and industrials, the offshore drilling space presents a compelling asymmetric opportunity. For investors willing to endure volatility and maintain patience, the merger of Valaris and Transocean may mark the beginning of a supercycle that offers returns comparable to those seen in the tanker market over the past five years.
