## The Critical Vulnerability in US Defense Supply Chains

November 27 represents a critical deadline for America’s defense sector. This is the date when China’s temporary suspension of its antimony export ban to the United States expires. Antimony is an essential mineral used primarily in ammunition, military equipment, and flame retardants. Despite its importance, the US relies on imports for approximately 91% of its supply, creating a significant vulnerability in national security infrastructure.

With antimony prices having more than doubled since last year, renewed Chinese restrictions could trigger another severe supply shock. To understand the implications of this deadline, we spoke with Rodney Stevens, CEO of NSJ Antimony, a company developing projects that could become vital to North American critical mineral independence.

## What Is Antimony and Why Does It Matter?

Antimony is classified as a critical metal due to its indispensable role in military applications. According to the USGS, approximately 40% of US antimony usage is associated with lead and ammunition, while another 49% goes toward metal products including flame retardants. While demand is growing in the solar photovoltaic sector, Stevens notes that military use remains the dominant driver of consumption.

A unique characteristic of antimony is its lack of recyclability. Unlike copper or zinc, antimony used in bombs and flame retardants does not return to the supply chain; it effectively disappears once deployed. This one-way flow means there is minimal secondary supply, making primary mining production essential for maintaining stockpiles.

## The Geopolitical Leverage: China’s Export Restrictions

China implemented export restrictions on antimony in 2024, citing dwindling domestic mining supplies and the need to maintain internal capacity for its own strategic needs. This move shocked global markets, revealing a massive supply gap. Currently, the world faces an annual shortfall of approximately 30,000 tons of antimony outside of China.

When China first blocked exports, it provided a temporary "lifeline" for military use to maintain geopolitical leverage. However, this exemption is set to expire on November 27. Stevens warns that if this lifeline is removed, it will likely cause another price rally as users scramble to secure remaining inventory.

## Market Dynamics: The Mini-Cycles Within a Super Cycle

The antimony market is not traded on major exchanges like the LME, making it less liquid and transparent. Investors often use stocks like US Antimony Corp (NYSE) as a proxy for metal prices. The chart for US Antimony Corp illustrates the volatility: a long bear market from 2021 to 2024 was followed by a sharp spike when China restricted exports.

Stevens explains that the current price dip reflects a temporary correction where users stopped buying at peak prices and began drawing down stockpiles built during the initial shortage. However, this is part of a larger "super cycle." As inventories dwindle, demand will resurge. Stevens predicts a long-term bull market lasting five years or more, characterized by mini-cycles of inventory building and restocking.

The timeline for new supply is lengthy. Due to ESG regulations and permitting hurdles, it takes a minimum of five years to bring new mines into production. Furthermore, antimony deposits are typically small. A single mine might produce only 3,000 tons per year, meaning roughly ten such mines would be needed to close the global gap.

## Government Response: Aggressive US Support for Critical Minerals

The US government has recognized the strategic urgency of antimony independence. Unlike Canada, which tends to offer financial support later in a project's life cycle (near permitting or feasibility stages), the US is aggressively funding projects at earlier stages, sometimes even upon initial resource estimates.

This support includes loans, bursaries, and grants directed at companies capable of securing domestic supply. For example, Perpetua Resources recently unlocked financing after the government identified its antimony potential. Stevens notes that while the US provides this capital, it does not strictly demand exclusive rights to the metal in return, indicating a broader strategic goal of diversifying global supply chains rather than monopolizing them.

## NSJ Antimony: A Bite-Sized Project for North American Security

Rodney Stevens leads NSJ Antimony (formerly NSJ Gold), operating in New Brunswick, Canada. The company is exploring the historic Lake George region, a former antimony producer that shut down due to low prices.

### Why Not Restart Lake George?
Although Lake George has an existing mill and known mineralization, restarting it is not feasible. Over the past five years, the mine filled with water containing high levels of arsenic, creating significant environmental liabilities. Consequently, NSJ Antimony is focusing on staking new ground nearby to drill for fresh ore bodies that can be processed at a central mill.

### Project Status and Strategy
NSJ Antimony has raised $4 million to fund an initial drilling program of 11 holes. The goal is not necessarily to discover a massive mine immediately, but to find an "ore grade intercept" that justifies further exploration. If successful, the company plans to expand resources with an 80/20 rule: 80% of drilling will define known zones, while 20% will be used for wildcat exploration.

Stevens emphasizes that antimony projects are "bite-sized." A typical mine might require $100 million in CAPEX and produce 3,000 to 5,000 tons per year with a cash cost half the current spot price. This scale allows junior miners to advance projects without the massive capital requirements of major mining houses.

If the US fails to secure alternative antimony sources, Stevens starkly warns that it could compromise its ability to sustain defense operations. However, the combination of aggressive government funding, high prices, and strategic projects like NSJ Antimony suggests a pathway toward resilience.
