## The Long End Is the Foundation for the Price of Money

As of Tuesday, September 8th, financial markets are experiencing a broad-based decline, with the S&P 500 down 32 basis points, the NASDAQ down 30 basis points, and gold down nearly 1%. However, the most critical development is occurring at the long end of the yield curve. Brett Heath, CEO of Metalla Royalty, argues that the long end of the curve is the foundation for the price of money, affecting everything from 30-year mortgages to global shipping costs.

The U.S. is currently in a "tough spot" with no clear ability to exit the escalating conflict with Iran. The conflict, which includes attacks on Saudi oil facilities by Iran-backed Houthis and U.S. strikes on Iranian oil tankers, is driving oil and diesel prices higher. Since most of the world's goods are moved by diesel-powered ships, these energy costs are filtering into the cost of everything, contributing to persistent inflation.

## The Shift in U.S. Debt Buyers

The core of the debt crisis lies in a fundamental change in who is buying U.S. Treasuries. For years, central banks and sovereign wealth funds were the "price-insensitive" buyers of U.S. debt. However, a long-term trend has seen these institutions diversify heavily into physical gold to reduce their exposure to U.S. Treasuries and duration risk.

According to the Office of Financial Research, hedge funds now hold over $2 trillion in U.S. Treasuries, backed by approximately $3 trillion in repo funding. This means the "most price-insensitive buyer" has been replaced by the "most price-sensitive buyer"—leveraged hedge funds. Because the U.S. must roll over nearly a third of its debt annually (the highest frequency among the G7, compared to an 11% average for the rest of the group), this frequent price discovery is driving long-term rates higher.

Heath notes that the U.S. came to market 444 times last year, and with hedge funds as the marginal buyer, rates at the long end are under significant upward pressure. This contrasts with Japan, which, despite a higher debt-to-GDP ratio, only rolls over about 10% of its debt annually, making it less vulnerable to rate shocks.

## Gold as a Sovereign Hedge

Gold has become the "largest, safest currency of the world" that cannot be debased. The shift began when the U.S. began to "weaponize the dollar" through sanctions and the seizure of foreign assets, particularly after the freezing of Russian assets. This led central banks to view gold as a necessary reserve asset.

Last year, for the first time in history, the nominal value of gold held in foreign reserves exceeded the nominal value of U.S. Treasuries. With approximately $9 trillion in U.S. Treasuries still sitting on foreign central bank balance sheets, there is significant room for further diversification. Heath believes the $4,000 price level for gold acts as a floor, supported by record central bank buying, and that the metal is likely to continue higher.

## The Copper Squeeze and the AI Power Paradox

While gold is consolidating, copper is breaking new all-time highs. Heath identifies copper as the "binding constraint" of the AI story. While the market focuses on AI chips, the real issue is power, and power runs on copper. Data center power demand is expected to double by 2030, equaling almost all the electricity Japan consumes annually.

The supply side of the copper market is severely constrained. Building a major copper mine takes 18 years, and only a dozen companies globally have the resources and skill to do so. Even if copper prices reach $10 a pound, new supply will not come online for 5 to 10 years. This supply-demand imbalance, combined with the fact that copper makes up less than 1% of data center capex, means the market is not fully pricing in the potential for a significant copper squeeze.

## Implications for Royalty Companies

For royalty and streaming companies like Metalla, the rising cost of debt presents an opportunity. As debt becomes more expensive for mining companies, royalty financing—where the operator gives up a percentage of production in perpetuity—becomes a more attractive part of the capital stack. Metalla, which has a portfolio of over 100 assets, is positioned to benefit from this shift.

Metalla recently reported its best quarter in company history, with record revenue, adjusted EBITDA, and net income, even as gold prices fell from their highs. The company’s strategy is to buy high-quality assets from high-quality operators in stable jurisdictions (the Americas and Australia) at a discount during development, allowing them to "free carry" the assets until they reach production.

Heath emphasizes that the mining sector is in its healthiest place ever, with margins at 31% compared to a 17% average for other sectors, yet trading at a decade-low P/E ratio of 20x. The gold-to-XAU (mining index) ratio has broken out of a 15-year consolidation, suggesting mining equities are positioned to outperform the metal itself.

## Conclusion

The U.S. debt crisis is a "nowhere to hide" scenario. With the Fed potentially forced to raise rates to combat oil-driven inflation and hedge funds demanding higher yields on Treasuries, the cost of money is rising across the board. Central banks are diversifying into gold, and the market is beginning to recognize the supply constraints in critical metals like copper. For investors, this means a shift in strategy is required: focusing on assets that are insulated from debt costs, such as royalty companies, and understanding the long-term structural shifts in global reserves and commodity supply.
