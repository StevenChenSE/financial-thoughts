### The Bond Market Has Taken the Wheel

On Wednesday, August 19th, the United States Treasury announced a significant shift in fiscal strategy: it would double its buybacks of long-term government bonds to cap the long end of the yield curve. The market reaction was immediate and volatile. Gold jumped 3% to $4,500 an ounce, Bitcoin rose more than 5%, and the 30-year Treasury yield fell back from its highest level in nearly two decades.

However, the relief proved short-lived. By Thursday, the bond market had taken back all of the gains, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell more than 700 points. As of this morning, the 30-year yield sits just under 5.3%, as if the announcement never happened. Gold is holding near $4,600, a three-month high, while Bitcoin trades at around $77,000, up more than 22% on the week.

## The Intervention: A "Game of Chicken"

The world's largest borrower has stepped into the market to support the price of its own debt. This marks the second intervention in three weeks, following the Treasury’s move on July 31st to prop up the Japanese yen—the first American intervention in the yen since 1998.

The context for these moves is stark. The US national debt is crossing the $40 trillion mark, with interest payments alone running north of a trillion dollars a year—more than the entire defense budget. Just last week, the government sold $25 billion of 30-year bonds at the highest rate in 25 years.

Danielle DiMartino Booth, CEO of QI Research, described the move as "Operation Twist," but run out of the Treasury instead of the Federal Reserve. "It is a very controversial thing to do, to be intervening in our Treasury market," Booth said. However, she noted that she would rather have the Treasury taking a direct role than leaving the onus on the Federal Reserve to step in with quantitative easing one day.

The intervention is also a surprise to risky asset investors. "Hedge funds have record positions in the US Treasury market right now," Booth explained. "This is probably a huge surprise to them that [Treasury Secretary Scott] Bessen came in to do this." There have been rumblings for some time about taking long maturity out of the Treasury market and shifting that into Treasury bills. This program, started under Janet Yellen, has now been doubled to $4 billion a month.

## The Missing Buyers

Adrian Day, president of Adrian Day Asset Management, argues that the intervention is aimed at a deeper problem: the investors who are supposed to buy America’s long-term debt have stopped showing up.

"Clearly, if the bond market is weak, and evidence of that would be yields moving up, that just makes it so much more difficult for them to sell those bonds," Day said. He emphasized that higher yields do not always attract more buyers; rather, they often reflect weakness in the underlying currency and economy.

Day pointed out that while foreign participation in auctions has gone up, much of that is from the Cayman Islands—hedge funds buying trades. "It's not the buyers we want in Treasuries," Day said. "Long-term people who are going to buy it and hold it for 30 years, which is primarily pension funds and insurance companies... and foreign governments... they're just not interested."

## The Yen Connection

Professor Steve Hanke of Johns Hopkins University explained why the yen rescue and the Treasury market are the same story. Investors have borrowed cheap yen for years to buy Treasuries. A collapsing yen would force them to dump those Treasuries to cover their losses. Therefore, defending Japan's currency is also a defense of America's bond market.

Hanke warned that the 30-year yield could easily go up another 50 basis points. He cited three major factors: the war in Iran and the disruption of the Gulf and Red Sea, the fact that the inflation genie is still out of the bottle in the US, and the general fiscal problem of an increased deficit that must be funded by the issuance of more Treasury paper.

## Chart Patterns and the 2007 Parallel

Chris Vermeulen, chief market strategist at technicaltraders.com, sees a historical parallel in the charts. "The last time we saw the 30-year trade this long, this many times in a year, was back in 2007 just before a financial crisis," Vermeulen said.

Vermeulen noted that the monthly chart is pointing to an 8% move in bond prices (which corresponds to a significant rise in yields). "They're literally trying to just hit the brakes and try to slow that down because if this pops, we are going to have that big financial crisis that is pending," he said.

## The Fed's Loss of Control

When the Federal Reserve skipped a rate hike at its July meeting, Chair Kevin Warsh pointed to the bond market, stating that the Fed hadn't done much in 42 days, but the markets had done quite a bit.

Gareth Soloway, president of Verified Investing, says this admission raises a question nobody at the Fed wants to answer. "What's scary about that is that if the market just does what it wants anyways, then what's the relevancy of the Federal Reserve?" Soloway asked.

Soloway warned that getting above 5% on the 10-year yield is going to get scary. He cited the housing market, noting that in places like Florida, business is "as bad as '08." "When you have 40 trillion in debt, how high can these things go before the amount that we're paying on interest... is just going to break the whole economy anyways?" Soloway asked.

Clem Chambers, founder of a NewFangled, goes a step further, suggesting that the liquidity holding the market up is not coming from the Federal Reserve at all, but from the Treasury. "They've got plenty of levers to pull and press and twist," Chambers said. "They're not, inverted commas, independent, are they?"

## The $40 Trillion Rubicon

Danielle DiMartino Booth highlighted the two numbers hanging over the market: 5% on the 10-year yield and $40 trillion of debt. "I think you hear most commonly the idea of a 5% US 10-year Treasury yield as being kind of Defcon 1 where things would really start to break across the system," Booth said.

The host compared the situation to a frog in a pot of water being slowly heated. "Debt's been rising, but it's not like all of a sudden, you know, we turn the boiler on," the host said. "It's like slowly simmering and at some point we're going to boil and die, but we haven't gotten to that point yet."

Peter Boockvar, Chief Investment Officer at BFG Wealth Partners, read the bond market's reaction to the buyback announcement as a direct answer to the Treasury. "When you start when you pick a fight with someone that's much bigger than you, you better bring enough ammo to that fight and the bond market is much bigger than him," Boockvar said.

Boockvar warned that if the dollar weakens, it creates inflationary issues and could facilitate foreign selling of US assets. "The bond market is telling Mr. Bessen, is that all you got?" Boockvar asked. When asked if the Treasury has enough firepower, Boockvar replied, "He does not. I think it goes higher."

## The End of the Road

Mark Skousen, economist at the Oxford Club, cited the Wall Street saying: "The stock market is never never commit suicide, but it can be murdered." Skousen warned that a fiscal crisis is approaching as the Treasury has to refinance $7 trillion every year.

"I'm reminded of the time in Sweden in 1992 when they had their own crisis. They had to raise rates to 500% on a short-term basis in order to keep people from flooding or leaving the Swedish krona," Skousen said. "The United States, of course, is the world's currency. So, there's no place to hide except to go to gold or go to the Bitcoin."

Skousen noted that central banks are buying gold "hand over fist" and now own more gold than Treasuries. "They're planning on trouble that you know, you couldn't get your $17,000 an ounce for gold if you had a treasury collapse and a no bid on treasuries," he said.

Adrian Day also sees a breaking point coming. "At some point we will reach a breaking point where you know, long-term bonds of Britain or Germany or US don't sell," Day said. "We'll reach a point where governments have to postpone repayment of debt. We're not there yet. We're a long way from there."

## Conclusion

Our eight guests came at this from different angles and they all arrived at the same uncomfortable place. The Treasury can shuffle which bonds it buys back and it can double again if it has to. It cannot change, however, the $40 trillion of debt outstanding and it cannot force pension funds and insurance companies to buy 30-year bonds they do not want.

Kevin Warsh admitted that the bond market is doing the Fed's job. The real question is what happens when the bond market turns that same power on the Treasury, demanding a higher rate at every auction until the government cannot afford to borrow. The number to watch is 5% on the 10-year yield. Below it, the game of chicken continues. Above it, things start to get really scary. Prediction markets put the odds of crossing that line by year end at one in three.
