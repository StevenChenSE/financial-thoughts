## Inverted Market Correlations: The "Quagmire"

A central theme of the discussion is the inversion of traditional market relationships, which Wagner calls a "quagmire." Historically, higher inflation expectations acted as a bullish tailwind for gold. However, in the current environment, this relationship has flipped 180 degrees.

### Why Gold Sold Off During High Inflation
When oil prices spiked (reaching above $100/barrel due to geopolitical tensions with Iran), inflation expectations rose. Traditionally, this would boost gold. Instead, gold declined. Wagner explains this paradox through the lens of liquidity and Federal Reserve policy:
*   **Fed Reaction:** Higher inflation signals led markets to anticipate aggressive rate hikes by the Fed. Rising rates are poison for non-yielding assets like gold.
*   **Margin Calls:** Institutional traders with leveraged positions faced margin calls as equities sold off. To cover these calls, they liquidated liquid assets, including gold, rather than holding it as a safe haven.

### The Oil-Gold-Dollar Dynamic
Recently, oil prices have fallen (dropping from over $100 to the 80s), and long-term yields (10-year and 30-year) have also declined. Wagner observes that gold is rising in tandem with falling oil and yields, suggesting a return to more traditional correlations where lower inflationary pressure or easing financial conditions support gold prices.

## Investment Strategy: Dollar Cost Averaging

For investors who purchased gold near recent highs and are currently underwater, Wagner advises a long-term perspective over short-term trading. He recommends **dollar cost averaging (DCA)**—buying small incremental amounts over time.

*   **Rationale:** In a market that is ultimately bullish but prone to deep peaks and valleys, DCA reduces the probability of loss and maximizes the chance of acquiring positions at better average prices.
*   **Personal Approach:** Wagner describes his own strategy as "set it and forget it," buying small amounts consistently. This ensures that high-priced purchases constitute only a small percentage (6-8%) of the total portfolio, mitigating risk.
