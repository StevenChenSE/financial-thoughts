**Michael Gad, founder of the Lead Lag Report, warns that private credit is this cycle's version of subprime mortgage debt—while making the case that AI will divide society into "architects" and "the unemployed."**

---

In a wide-ranging interview covering everything from the Iran nuclear situation to the future of work, Michael Gad delivered a provocative thesis: AI isn't just an investment theme—it's a fundamental restructuring of the economy that will force governments to implement universal basic income (UBI) or risk social collapse. Meanwhile, Gad argues that private credit, the much-lauded alternative to traditional bank lending, is hiding the same kind of systemic risk that blew up the financial system in 2008.

"Private credit is this year, is this cycle, subprime," Gad stated bluntly. "Nobody understood what I was saying until very suddenly it got into the headlines with some of these blowups."

The interview touched on markets, macroeconomics, and the existential question of what happens to human workers when AI becomes capable of performing cognitive tasks faster and cheaper than any human could. It's a conversation that should make investors think carefully about where they're putting their capital—and whether their own skills will still be relevant in five years.

---

## The AI Paradox: Bullish Until It's Bearish

Gad's investment thesis on AI is nuanced. He's invested heavily—$40,000 over the past 78 weeks—building AI automation systems for his media business and research operations. He's witnessed firsthand what he calls the "agentic AI" revolution: AI systems that don't just answer questions but actually complete tasks autonomously.

"I'm very much all in on AI," Gad said. "This stuff is going to really replace a lot of industries."

But here's the twist: Gad believes AI is a paradox for investors. It's initially bullish because it boosts productivity and company earnings. However, it becomes bearish when the unemployment consequences materialize—millions of people losing jobs faster than they can reskill.

"The world's going to be divided into two kinds of people: the architects and the unemployed," Gad warned. "The people that can actually figure out how to use AI and go all in—and everybody else that's basically going to be replaced by that output."

Gad's concern isn't theoretical. He's spent months building over 70 different agentic AI automations, and he's convinced the technology is moving faster than people's ability to adapt. "AI is much faster than most people's ability to think, which means they're not going to be able to reskill," he explained.

### Why UBI Might Be Inevitable

The investment implications of mass AI-driven unemployment are significant, according to Gad. Without intervention, the collapse in consumer discretionary spending power would crush the economy—and ultimately, equities.

"The last thing you want is people sitting around at home without an income," Gad said. "That's how you have a surge in crime."

His proposed solution? Tax the AI companies, call it an "efficiency dividend," and distribute it to those displaced by automation. "You're going to need universal basic income because the reality is so many people are going to be unemployed as AI keeps on taking over things."

Gad pointed to recent data from the UK as a warning sign: for the 2025-26 financial year, the government will spend $333 billion on welfare—more than the $330 billion collected in income taxes. "Politicians are very good at finding different words," Gad noted. "They're not going to call it tax. They're going to call it dividend. They're not going to call it welfare. They're going to call it abundance."

## Private Credit: This Cycle's Subprime

Beyond AI, Gad's most controversial claim concerns private credit. While traditional markets have rallied to new highs, the private credit space has been quietly imploding—much like subprime mortgage debt did before the 2008 financial crisis.

"I have been very loud for the last year saying private credit is this cycle's subprime, and nobody understood what I was saying until very suddenly it got into the headlines," Gad recalled.

The connection to software stocks is direct. Business Development Companies (BDCs), which lend heavily to technology firms, have gotten crushed alongside the software sector. The iShares Expanded Tech-Software Sector ETF (IGV) is down 20% year-to-date, while the S&P 500 sits at all-time highs.

"Once you start getting deeper and deeper into agentic AI and seeing the capabilities, you start realizing that a lot of companies on the software side will not make it," Gad said. "A large reason for why these stocks have gotten crushed is because there's been a lot of lending on the software side on the private credit end."

### An Opportunity in the Wreckage?

Interestingly, Gad sees the carnage as a potential opportunity. "I myself think there's an investment opportunity now in some of these private credit public equity plays," he said. "Because there's an overreaction on the idea that no companies will exist because of AI."

He drew a parallel to the 2007-2008 period: "Bare thorns happened—what, six, seven months before Lehman? There were rallies in between that. You had opportunities to make money in those very beaten-down areas that ultimately did go bust, just with longer time."

Gad acknowledged that the fundamental thesis—that AI will cause companies to default—remains valid. "If it hurt private credit, it's going to hurt public junk debt. And while it might be an overreaction now, the reality is there's no pricing default risk there."

## Trump's Iran Strategy and Market Numbness

Shifting to macro, Gad offered a counterintuitive take on the Middle East situation: President Trump has successfully gotten the market "totally numb" to Iran concerns.

"Clearly that's why we've had this rip the last three weeks in markets," Gad observed. "The deal—they're going to make a deal. I don't know who the hell he's negotiating with, but he's going to make a deal with somebody. The market doesn't care anymore about Iran."

This numbness has allowed markets to ignore geopolitical risks that would have caused significant volatility in previous eras. Gad's explanation is straightforward: "Historically, wars don't seem to matter all too much unless you get a super spike in oil—and clearly you're not going to have that reverse carry trade play out."

### Where to Hide if War Escalates

Despite his skepticism about immediate risks, Gad was clear about where investors should park capital if the situation deteriorates:

"If war escalates, I still think it's long duration Treasuries. I mean, it's a thing nobody wants to use as a hedge, probably becomes the best hedge."

He also acknowledged gold as a traditional safe haven, though his enthusiasm was tempered. "Gold in general became a momentum trade, and it seems like for now at least momentum may have broken."

## The Disinflationary AI Thesis and Treasury Yields

Gad's long-term thesis on AI has significant implications for bond markets. If AI is inherently disinflationary—or even deflationary—then long-duration Treasury yields should decline substantially from current levels.

"Flat out, long-duration yields really should drop to reflect that disinflationary pressure," Gad argued. "If it doesn't, I'd argue we have bigger problems to worry about, because if 10-year yields do not track inflation expectations, then it's tracking default risk in the US."

The connection between AI, disinflation, and Treasuries has become a popular trade. Gad referenced a circulating thesis: "What if the real way to play AI is long-duration Treasuries? Because if AI is disinflationary or deflationary, then yields at these levels will come down."

Gad is explicit about his positioning: "I like long duration, actually now more from an investment perspective than a trading perspective."

### Why High-Yield Credit Spreads Are Dangerous

At the same time, Gad is wary of credit markets. "There's no juice in the high-yield space," he said. "In the junk debt space, which still has higher yield, the reality is there's no pricing default risk there."

This isn't a traditional bond bull market call—it's a trade based on AI's deflationary impact combined with potential credit dislocation. "I do believe there's going to be a credit dislocation at some point," Gad acknowledged. "Spread widening happens when you're at the narrowest credit spreads in history, like we are right now."

## Deregulation: The Trade Nobody's Talking About

While AI dominates headlines, Gad sees significant opportunity in the deregulation theme—a shift he believes is being underappreciated by markets.

"I really think the deregulation thing is real," Gad said. "That's why I launched the Free Markets ETF (FMKT)—investing in companies that benefit from deregulation. I think there's so much untapped potential that deregulation unleashes into the broader economy."

### Key Deregulation Plays

Gad identified several specific sectors:

- **Cannabis:** "As you have an aging population, older people want to smoke. They want their cannabis." Recent news around federal cannabis reclassification has brought the sector back into focus.

- **Psychedelics:** "There's the legit health benefits and physical benefits with some of these treatments." Gad noted FDA movement toward potential approvals for psychedelic-based therapies.

- **Nuclear energy:** "Nuclear is not going anywhere. The nuclear play is very real."

- **Cryptocurrency:** "I do actually think the Bitcoin crypto play is real."

## The Dollar and Emerging Markets

Gad sees a secular shift underway that could benefit neglected asset classes. The dollar's multi-decade strength may be ending.

"The momentum on the downside for the dollar is real, because the momentum in emerging markets on the upside is real," Gad explained. "If you continue to see people chasing emerging markets, they're going to sell dollars to buy the emerging market currency to buy emerging market stocks."

This isn't just a relative value trade. Gad sees fundamental drivers:

- **Commodities and EM:** "There's a direct link between emerging market outperformance and commodity strength and commodity demand."
- **Robotics and physical labor:** "If you're going to have robotics taking over physical jobs—which is already starting—that's a commodity-driven dynamic. Commodity-driven dynamic benefits emerging markets."
- **Valuations:** "Emerging markets, relative to the S&P, it's had a big move, but it's underperformed the S&P for more than well over a decade and badly."

## The Lumber-to-Gold Ratio: An Indicator for a New Era?

Gad also addressed his controversial lumber-to-gold ratio, which he developed based on the theory that housing drives the business cycle. However, he's acknowledged that the current cycle isn't about housing—it's about AI.

"I launched that into a cycle where it's not about the housing market—it's about the AI tech cycle," Gad said. "Some major sell-side research firm said the S&P is like 40% AI in terms of what's driving the performance. It's like, alright, that's it. The business cycle is no longer your house. It's digital."

He closed the RORO ETF that used the signal. "Unfortunately, I didn't think that in 2021 AI would ever be a thing and that it would drive the economics of the world basically."

## Fed Policy and the Money Printer

Gad addressed the elephant in the room: the Federal Reserve's apparent willingness to print money to finance deficits.

"They'll crash stocks to save bonds," Gad said, referencing a popular market theory. "If you got to refinance, the best thing to do is scare the pants off equity investors to force them into bonds as they're releasing bonds to finance."

He's skeptical that the Fed can easily pivot away from money printing. "Policy makers just love printing money, man. I don't know what else to tell you. They just... anybody thinks that they can't, that they have to stop at some point? It's like..."

The key insight: expanding money supply doesn't automatically mean the S&P 500 goes up. "Money supply expanding may finally actually benefit emerging markets, may finally benefit small caps, may finally benefit BDCs, may finally benefit software stocks. It doesn't have to just go into the market, which is the thing everyone is already long—which is passive large-cap S&P type stocks."

## The Human Edge: Charisma and Relationships

In a poignant closing, Gad offered practical advice for individuals worried about AI replacing their jobs:

"Everybody that's watching this, if you want to do anything to better yourself to survive, you better sure as hell be charismatic and be able to develop relationships, because AI might get you leads, it might get you prospects—you still got to close."

He acknowledged that even he has been surprised by AI's capabilities: "I've been one of the most skeptical guys on AI. I was very wrong, dead wrong for a couple years on Nvidia. This is different than just doing a prompt. Now that it actually does things... as long as you have the right mindset for it, you start realizing, wow."

1. **Private credit carries hidden systemic risk** similar to subprime mortgages in the last cycle, particularly due to heavy lending in the software sector.

2. **AI will create a bifurcated society** between "architects" (those who can leverage AI) and "the unemployed" (those who cannot adapt quickly enough).

3. **Universal basic income may become inevitable** to prevent consumer spending collapse as AI displaces workers—representing a potential "efficiency dividend" taxed from AI companies.

4. **Long-duration Treasuries offer a strategic opportunity** if AI proves as deflationary as Gad anticipates, while high-yield credit spreads currently lack adequate default risk pricing.

5. **Deregulation themes (cannabis, psychedelics, nuclear, crypto) represent underappreciated opportunities**, alongside potential rotation from the S&P 500 into neglected areas like emerging markets and small caps.

6. **Human skills—charisma, relationship-building, closing ability—will become the irreplaceable differentiator** in an AI-driven economy where cognitive tasks can be automated.
