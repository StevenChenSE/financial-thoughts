## The Young Canadians Considering a Bold Escape

A striking new poll reveals that four in ten Canadians aged 18 to 34 would vote to become American citizens—if only asset conversion to USD was guaranteed. This startling finding from Ipsos Public Affairs captures something deeper than mere frustration: it's a profound crisis of confidence in Canada's economic future.

"They feel like they're on a knife's edge," says Daryl Bricker, Global CEO of Ipsos Public Affairs. "A couple more cuts from that knife and it will bring them down."

The numbers paint an increasingly dire picture of Canadian household finances. According to the latest Ipsos research, 43% of Canadians report being within $200 or less of not being able to meet their monthly financial obligations—a figure that has climbed two points from just the previous quarter. Nearly one-third (29%) say they already don't earn enough to cover their bills and debt payments. The average amount of money Canadians have left at the end of the month has plummeted to just $1,000, down dramatically from $97 last quarter.

These aren't abstract statistics. They represent millions of Canadians lying awake at night, wondering whether next month they'll be financially viable. "This is how they feel," Bricker explains. "They feel like they're on a knife's edge when it comes to their financial situation, and they feel a couple more cuts from that knife will bring them down."

## The Perception-is-Reality Economy

What's driving this widespread anxiety? While economists parse the nuanced details of supply chains and commodity pricing, everyday Canadians are experiencing a straightforward reality: everything costs more, and they feel it every time they fill their gas tank or walk through a grocery store.

"The point for the public is: perception is reality," Bricker states. "They are experiencing the increase in fuel prices both directly and indirectly. They're experiencing it when they go to the gas pump and when they go to the grocery store. So things that need to travel in order to bring them the goods they consume every day."

The timing has been brutal. Gas prices have soared due to instability in the Middle East, compounding affordability concerns that predated the current conflict. For most Canadians who drive to work, seeing the numbers at the pump is immediate, personal, and impossible to ignore.

"Unless there's some considerable relief from the government, this upward pressure on inflation from rising fuel costs and groceries will continue," Bricker warns.

## A Government Responds: Carney's First Moves

Prime Minister Mark Carney's government—having achieved the first transition from minority to majority in Canadian history—has wasted no time addressing the crisis. Among his first acts was suspending the federal fuel tax starting next Monday through Labor Day (September 7th), a measure Carney called "a responsible temporary measure that will also reduce costs for truckers and businesses."

The move reflects a broader shift in how Carney's government approaches policy. "They seem to be better attuned to the public opinion environment than their predecessors were," Bricker observes. "It's indicative of what Mark Carney said when he was at Davos—you have to deal with the world as it is rather than the world that you'd want it to be."

Rather than pushing policies Ottawa believes Canadians should embrace, the new government appears more willing to listen to what Canadians actually want. This pragmatic approach stands in contrast to the previous administration, though Bricker notes that Conservative leader Pierre Poilievre would likely have taken similar action on fuel prices had he won the election.

## What Canadians Actually Want: The Top Five Concerns

When Ipsos asks Canadians what issues matter most, the answers reveal a nation grappling with interconnected crises:

1. **Affordability** — The number one concern, encompassing everything from groceries to fuel
2. **Healthcare** — Canadians increasingly fear the future of publicly-funded healthcare
3. **Housing** — Access to the housing they aspire to remains out of reach for many
4. **Immigration** — But notably, concerns focus on system management, not immigrants themselves
5. **Relations with the United States** — A dramatic shift from viewing America as an "uncomfortable neighbor" to viewing it as a "threat"

On immigration specifically, Canadians have made an important distinction from their American and European counterparts. "It's not actually about the immigrants themselves," Bricker explains. "It's more about how the system is being managed and whether it's being managed effectively. Canadians have concluded that immigration becoming out of control is having an effect on cost of living issues, healthcare, and the credibility of our immigration system."

The cross-party consensus on immigration that once existed "has started to fall apart," particularly among voting groups, though it remains at the official political level.

## The Housing Market Reckoning

Perhaps nowhere is Canada's economic transformation more visible than in its housing market. Condominium prices in major cities like Vancouver and Toronto have fallen dramatically. More remarkably, rent is now actually negotiable downward—something unprecedented in recent memory.

"The interesting thing is the condo market," Bricker notes. "People looked at it as investment opportunities rather than living opportunities, and the chickens are coming home to roost in that market."

But there's a deeper cultural shift at play. When Canadians are asked what type of housing they'd actually like to live in, condominiums rank near the bottom. "Condos are the housing you endure rather than the housing you want," Bricker says. "The housing you want are generally single family homes—that's what people are looking for."

This presents serious challenges for developers who built with young up-and-comers in mind. "Probably not the young up-andcomers that the condo makers may have thought they were building for," Bricker observes. "Younger people—it's the housing they endure. It's not the housing they aspire to."

The demographic picture only complicates matters.

## The Demographic Time Bomb

Last year marked the first year in Canadian history where the overall population actually shrank. While immigration policy tightening contributed to this decline, the underlying driver is far more fundamental: Canadians aren't having enough children.

To simply replace the population that dies each year, Canada needs a fertility rate of 2.1 children per woman—the so-called "replacement rate." Today's fertility rate stands at just 1.3.

"We're almost a whole kid short to just replenish our population," Bricker says. "Without immigration, we would probably be tipping into greater population decline than we have now."

This has major implications for the housing market. With fewer children being born and immigration being curtailed, the demand dynamics that drove Canada's housing boom face fundamental challenges.

"I don't know that people are really taking this into account and thinking about it," Bricker warns. And Canada isn't unique in this regard. "This is happening in most of the world, except in places like Africa or the Indian subcontinent. Even places like Latin America are going through a very similar thing where their fertility rate has collapsed."

## The Interest Rate Wildcard

For Canadians carrying variable-rate mortgages—which are the norm in Canada unlike America's 30-year fixed-rate system—the Bank of Canada's decisions carry enormous weight.

"The Canadian central bank has been pretty stable over time," Bricker notes, "but with the degree of uncertainty that exists in the marketplace, things will probably remain stable."

That stability, however, masks competing pressures. Rising fuel and grocery costs create upward inflationary pressure, while a softening real estate market could push mortgage rates downward as lenders compete more aggressively for fewer borrowers.

"I think to say that anybody could predict what the Bank of Canada is going to do in the next six months or the next year is hard to say," Bricker admits, "particularly given what's happening in the Middle East right now."

The painful memory of 2022—when interest rates essentially doubled for many Canadians—remains fresh, raising the question of whether similar disruption could occur again.
