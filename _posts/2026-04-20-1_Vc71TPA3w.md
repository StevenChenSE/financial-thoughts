## A Deep Dive into Canada's Economic Crisis with Daryl Bricker, CEO of Ipsos Public Affairs

---

Four in ten Canadians are living on the financial edge—and they know it. According to the latest polling data from Ipsos, 43% of Canadians report being within $200 or less of not being able to meet their monthly financial obligations. Nearly one-third say they already don't earn enough to cover their bills and debt payments. This isn't just a number; it's a national crisis that is reshaping politics, policy, and the Canadian identity itself.

Daryl Bricker, global CEO of Ipsos Public Affairs, sat down to discuss the state of the Canadian economy, the surprising resilience of the Toronto Stock Exchange amid global uncertainty, and what Prime Minister Mark Carney's new majority government means for the country's future.

---

## The Perception Is Reality: How Canadians Are Experiencing Economic Pain

When asked whether living standards in Canada have worsened dramatically since early 2025, Bricker offered a straightforward answer: for the Canadian public, perception is reality.

"Regardless of what the experts say about the cost of living—the expansion of prices for corn or other commodities—what matters is that Canadians feel it every day," Bricker explained. "They experience it directly at the gas pump and indirectly when they go to the grocery store. Things that need to travel to reach consumers are getting more expensive, and people are feeling it."

### Key Findings: Canadian Financial Vulnerability

The numbers tell a stark story:

- **43%** of Canadians are within $200 or less of not being able to meet monthly financial obligations (up 2 points from last quarter)
- **29%** say they don't earn enough to cover bills and debt payments
- **Women and younger adults** remain among the most financially vulnerable demographics
- Average finances left at month end have climbed to a record **$1,000**, up from $97 last quarter

### The Psychological Toll

"This is how they feel," Bricker emphasized. "They feel like they're on a knife's edge when it comes to their financial situation, and they feel like a couple more cuts from that knife will bring them down."

This financial anxiety extends beyond simple household budgeting. It's affecting:

- **Optimism about the future**—especially among younger Canadians
- **Beliefs about upward mobility**—parents don't believe their children will do better than them at the same stage of life
- **Daily emotional well-being**—described as an "overcast" weather hanging over people's thinking

## The Energy Paradox: Why Higher Oil Prices Are Actually Good for Canada

There's a counterintuitive element to the current economic situation. While gas prices soar due to instability in the Middle East, higher oil prices actually positively impact the **TSX (Toronto Stock Exchange)**. When comparing the TSX to the American S&P 500, the Canadian market has actually outperformed since last year by a wide margin.

### Why This Doesn't Help the Average Canadian

Despite this market strength, Bricker notes that there's "pretty close equivalence between Canadians and Americans right now in terms of their economic optimism." The difference lies in **who gets blamed**:

- In the **United States**, people are taking out their frustration on the national government and Donald Trump personally
- In **Canada**, Mark Carney isn't receiving the same level of blame—Canadians tend to point fingers at external factors, including Donald Trump

This suggests a critical political advantage for the Carney government: the ability to redirect economic frustration outward rather than inward.

---

## Carney's First Moves: Fuel Tax Suspension and a New Approach to Governance

After securing Canada's first-ever transition from a minority to a majority government between national elections, Prime Minister Mark Carney has made his priorities clear. His first major act was suspending the federal fuel tax from March through September 7th (Labor Day), calling it "a responsible temporary measure that will also reduce costs for truckers and businesses."

### Listening to Canadians: A New Governing Philosophy

According to Bricker, this represents a fundamental shift in how the Liberal government operates:

"Mark Carney said at Davos that you have to deal with the world as it is rather than the world you'd want it to be. It seems like he's doing the same thing when it comes to Canada. They're not using the public policy process to make Canadians eat their broccoli every day. They seem to be more open to the idea that they should be listening to Canadians and leading them in directions they want to go, rather than trying to change things as much as the previous government was."

### The Conservative Parallel

Bricker notes that Pierre Poilievre, had he won the election, would likely be doing something very similar on fuel policy. This suggests a rare area of bipartisan economic consensus.

## What Canadians Actually Care About: The Top Five Issues

When Ipsos surveys Canadians about the most important issues facing the country, the results reveal a clear hierarchy:

### The Top 5 Issues in Order of Priority

1. **Affordability** – The cost of daily living expenses
2. **Healthcare** – Concerns about the future of publicly paid healthcare
3. **Housing** – Access to affordable middle-class housing
4. **Immigration** – How the system is being managed
5. **Dealing with the United States** – Relations with the neighboring superpower

### The Affordability-Housing Connection

These issues are deeply interconnected. Affordability and housing are "obviously very interlinked," Bricker noted. Canadians want action on both daily expenses and longer-term housing access.

### Healthcare Anxiety

"There's a feeling in general that the future of publicly paid healthcare is not assured," Bricker explained. "The level of service people feel they're getting from the healthcare system is definitely in decline. They're very worried about what the future will bring."

### Immigration: A Nuance Difference from the U.S. and Europe

Unlike in the United States or many European countries, the immigration debate in Canada is not primarily about the immigrants themselves. Instead, Canadians are concerned about:

- How the system is being managed
- Whether immigration is contributing to cost of living issues
- The effect on healthcare services
- The credibility of the immigration system itself

"Many Canadians have concluded that immigration has become out of control, and this is having an effect on those cost of living issues," Bricker said. "The consensus across all parties still remains at the official level, but not necessarily among the voting groups."

## The Housing Market in Crisis: Condos as the New Sacrifice Zone

For those watching Canadian real estate, a dramatic shift is underway. Housing prices—especially condominiums—in major cities like Vancouver and Toronto are falling dramatically. Even rents are becoming negotiable downward, something not seen in decades.

### Why Condos Are Falling Out of Favor

Bricker explained a fundamental preference shift:

"The condo market was seen as an investment opportunity rather than a living opportunity. But the reality is that condos are not at the top of the list for most Canadians. Condos are the housing you *endure* rather than the housing you want. What people actually want are generally single family homes—probably like the one you grew up in, David."

### The Forces Working Against Condos

Several factors are converging against the condo market:

- **Remote work flexibility** – People no longer need to live downtown
- **Commuting options** – Workers can live further from city centers
- **Aspiration for single-family homes** – The preferred housing type remains elusive for many
- **Demographic shifts** – Young up-and-comers are no longer the target buyers

"Who's still buying condos? Probably not the young up-and-comers the condo developers thought they were building for. It's likely people downsizing—older people moving into that type of accommodation. But younger people? It's the housing they endure. It's not the housing they aspire to."

## The Demographic Time Bomb: Why Canada's Population is Actually Shrinking

Last year marked the first year in Canadian history where the overall population actually shrank. This isn't a temporary blip—it's a structural crisis that will reshape the country's future.

### The Birth Rate Crisis

To simply replace the number of people who die in Canada every year, the country needs a fertility rate of 2.1 (meaning every woman, on average, has two children during her lifetime). Today, Canada's birth rate stands at just **1.3**—almost a full child short of what it needs just to maintain current population levels.

### Immigration Was the Lifeline—But It May Be Gone

"The thing that kept the population growing was the fact that we had very large amounts of immigration," Bricker noted. "Many people would argue it was too much immigration. So until we get that straightened out, we're going to see the Canadian population continue to decline because we're simply not having enough kids."

### What This Means for the Housing Market

The implications are profound:

- Without immigration, the population would already be in decline
- Government crackdown on immigration is ongoing
- Housing demand will be affected long-term
- This is not unique to Canada—it's happening across most of the developed world

"Even places like Latin America are going through a very similar thing where their fertility rate has collapsed," Bricker added. "This is a global phenomenon."

## The Bank of Canada: Predicting Interest Rates in Uncertain Times

Canadians have variable rate mortgages rather than the 30-year fixed mortgages common in the United States, making the Bank of Canada's decisions critically important to household finances.

### The Current Outlook

Bricker was measured in his predictions:

"The Canadian central bank has been pretty stable over time. They haven't been making huge changes or movements. With the degree of uncertainty that exists in the marketplace, I think things will probably remain stable."

### Competing Pressures

The bank faces a difficult balancing act:

- **Upward pressure on inflation** from rising fuel costs, groceries, and transportation
- **Downward pressure on housing demand** as the real estate market softens
- **Competition among lenders** may lead to some mortgage rate softening

"The mix of things happening all at the same time makes it hard to predict what the Bank of Canada will do in the next six months or year, particularly given what's happening in the Middle East."

### Could 2022 Happen Again?

In 2022, interest rates roughly doubled for many Canadians. When asked about the likelihood of that happening again, Bricker was cautious:

"If the costs of goods in the country continue to remain high—the transportation of them, access to fuel, home heating and all the rest—unless there's some considerable relief from the government, there could be upward pressure. But predicting this is very difficult given the current uncertainty."

## Regional Tensions: Alberta's Quiet Revolution

Bricker expressed particular concern about Alberta, where approximately **30% of residents say they would vote for Alberta to become its own country** in North America. While this doesn't represent a majority, it's a significant signal of regional alienation.

"People's view of the United States has moved from being an uncomfortable next-door neighbor to something they believe is a threat," Bricker noted, explaining the shifting Canadian perspective on its southern neighbor.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **Canadian financial vulnerability is at a crisis point** — 43% of Canadians are within $200 of not being able to meet monthly obligations, reflecting deep anxiety about daily financial survival.

2. **Perception drives political reality** — Whether or not economic fundamentals justify the concern, how Canadians *feel* about their finances shapes their voting behavior and policy preferences.

3. **Mark Carney's government is adapting** — The new majority is prioritizing immediate affordability concerns (fuel tax suspension) and listening to public sentiment rather than pushing ideological agendas.

4. **The housing market is undergoing a fundamental reset** — Condos are declining in value and desirability as remote work enables Canadians to pursue their preferred housing: single-family homes.

5. **Canada faces a demographic reckoning** — With a fertility rate of 1.3 (well below the 2.1 replacement rate) and immigration policies tightening, the country is facing population decline with profound implications for housing, healthcare, and economic growth.

6. **Regional grievances are growing** — Significant portions of Alberta are open to separation, and the consensus on many issues—including immigration—is fraying at the voting group level even if it holds at the official political level.
