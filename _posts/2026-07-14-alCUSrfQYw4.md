## Adam Taggart Discusses AI Valuation Risks, Buildout Bottlenecks, and Macro Shifts

In a special segment from the Royal Symposium, financial commentator Adam Taggart of *Thoughtful Money* joined David Lin to analyze the current state of the AI trade and broader macroeconomic landscape. Taggart highlighted a growing divergence between market enthusiasm and insider behavior, noting that many technology elites are actively de-risking their portfolios despite the ongoing AI boom. With AI and AI-adjacent companies comprising 45% of the S&P 500 market capitalization, Taggart argued that physical constraints, valuation extremes, and social tensions pose significant risks for the remainder of the year.

### Elite De-Risking and Insider Sentiment

Taggart shared anecdotal evidence from an elite gathering in Lake Tahoe, attended by figures like Google co-founder Sergey Brin and neighbors of Michael Milken and Steve Wynn. He observed that many tech millionaires and billionaires, rather than doubling down, are selling tech assets and moving into Treasury bills. One attendee reported holding 65% in T-bills. Taggart emphasized that this behavior represents "complete de-risking" rather than profit-taking for personal needs, signaling deep concern among those most exposed to the industry. He compared the current sentiment to the dot-com era, questioning whether the market is in late 1997 or early 2000, noting that insiders are increasingly acknowledging a bubble while remaining uncertain about the timing of the top.

### Physical Constraints Threaten Buildout Projections

While capital remains available for AI infrastructure, Taggart identified physical limitations as the likely bottleneck. He cited issues such as land availability, permitting delays, water scarcity, and component shortages like memory chips and capacitors. He pointed to a recent moratorium on new data centers in Reno, Nevada, and the withdrawal of Blackstone from a major data center project as evidence that the aggressive buildout schedules modeled by Wall Street may not be feasible. Taggart suggested the actual buildout could fall to 75-80% of forecasts, potentially triggering a significant repricing in the AI complex. Unlike previous tech cycles where infrastructure like fiber optics retained value, data center chips depreciate rapidly, with upgrade costs reaching two-thirds of the initial build, complicating the path for second-generation companies.

### Market Concentration and Correction Risks

Taggart noted that the "Magnificent Seven" hyperscalers are beginning to lag, while the semiconductor sector remains overheated. This dynamic has narrowed the market trade further, increasing vulnerability given that semiconductors are highly cyclical. With AI-related stocks making up nearly half the S&P 500, a cooling in this sector could drag the broader market down. Taggart referenced bearish signals from analysts like Darius Dale, who anticipates a 1998-style correction, and Jeremy Grantham, who predicts a bubble collapse within two years. Taggart himself forecasted a potential 10-15% market correction or a flat finish for the year, advising investors to take gains and implement hedges. He echoed John Hussman's warning that in bubble markets, investors must choose whether to "look like an idiot now or look like an idiot later."

### The K-Shaped Economy and Social Instability

Beyond markets, Taggart discussed the "K-shaped" economy, where the bottom demographic faces rising debt, declining savings, and job displacement, while the top maintains spending. He warned that the breaking point for the economy might be social rather than financial. Citing political scientist Peter Turchin's work on regime change, Taggart highlighted the risk of "excess elites" and disaffection among the lower classes, drawing parallels to the rise of the Bolsheviks. He expressed concern over the growing political appeal of socialist policies driven by emotional grievance rather than economic models, noting that the US ranks high on Turchin's list for potential societal collapse.

### AI, Robotics, and Labor Displacement

Taggart referenced Nicholas Eberstadt's book *Men Without Work* to describe the cohort of working-age men exiting the labor force. He argued that the combination of AI and robotics threatens to displace both white-collar and blue-collar workers at an exponential rate. While AI may create opportunities for a small number of "solopreneurs," Taggart questioned how the bottom 80-90% of the workforce would be repurposed, noting a lack of concrete plans to absorb displaced labor. He warned that the social cost of rapid displacement could outweigh the economic benefits of the technology if alternative productive uses for labor are not found.

### Geopolitics and Economic Models

The conversation touched on the clash between Eastern and Western values, with Taggart suggesting that internal educational shifts and external support from nations like China and Russia aim to dismantle Western capitalism. He discussed the success of certain command economies like Singapore and South Korea, attributing their prosperity to the integration of free market principles within a rigid, multi-decade plan. Taggart argued that Western capitalism requires a return to genuine free markets with fair regulatory "referees" to address cronyism and restore confidence, rather than abandoning the model for systems with a poor historical track record.

### Investment Opportunities

On the investment front, Taggart suggested that precious metals may have bottomed as pressure from high oil prices eases. He also highlighted copper as a long-term bullish opportunity, essential for the ongoing AI infrastructure buildout despite potential slowdowns in pace. Taggart concluded that while AI remains transformative, the current market pricing reflects an unrealistic buildout rate, making this a prudent time for caution and risk management.

### Summary

Adam Taggart's analysis points to a market at a potential zenith, driven by extreme valuations and insider de-risking in the AI sector. Physical constraints threaten to curb infrastructure growth, while macroeconomic and social risks loom large. Investors are advised to exercise caution, secure profits, and hedge against a likely correction in the coming months.
