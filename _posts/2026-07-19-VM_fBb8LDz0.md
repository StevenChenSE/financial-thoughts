## Demystifying the Legal Status of Virtual Currency for Individual Investors

For ordinary people in mainland China, the question "Is buying Bitcoin illegal?" often sparks two extreme online opinions: one claiming that any contact with cryptocurrency is a crime, and the other asserting that personal trading is completely safe and legal. Both views are overly simplistic.

The reality is more nuanced. Current regulations do not explicitly define an individual casually buying a cryptocurrency as a criminal act. However, they also do not issue a "legal pass" for personal transactions. There is a significant legal distinction between holding your own assets and acting as a payment channel or matchmaker for others. Based on the latest official documents released in 2026, this article clarifies the five critical boundaries that individuals must understand.

### 1. The Regulatory Baseline: The 2026 Notice
It is crucial to base discussions on current law. On February 6, 2026, the People's Bank of China, along with eight other departments, issued the "Notice on Further Preventing and Handling Risks Related to Virtual Currency" (Document No. Yin Fa [2026] 42). This document, effective immediately upon release, superseded the previous 2021 notice. Therefore, any legal assessment regarding cryptocurrency in China today must rely on this 2026 framework, rather than outdated information from previous years.

### 2. Boundary One: Virtual Currency Is Not Legal Tender
The 2026 Notice explicitly states that virtual currencies such as Bitcoin, Ethereum, and Tether (USDT) do not possess the legal status of state-issued legal tender. They lack "legal tender status" (法偿性).

In plain terms, this means:
*   They are not recognized as RMB or any other national currency.
*   They cannot be used as a medium of exchange for market settlements.
*   You cannot compel others to accept them as payment.
*   While they have market prices and liquidity, they are not equivalent to bank deposits and do not enjoy the same regulatory protections as traditional financial products like stocks or funds.

Investors must understand that the presence of a market price does not equate to legal protection. Even tokens named "stablecoins" like USDT are not protected under banking deposit insurance.

### 3. Boundary Two: Personal Use vs. Business Activities
This is the most critical distinction. The 2026 Notice strictly prohibits "business activities" involving virtual currency within the country. These prohibited activities include:
*   Exchanging legal tender for virtual currency and vice versa.
*   Exchanging virtual currencies for other virtual currencies.
*   Acting as an intermediary or matchmaker for transactions.
*   Providing pricing, financing, or issuing tokens.

**The Key Distinction:**
*   **Personal Use:** Using your own funds, your own accounts, and bearing the results yourself is not classified as a business activity.
*   **Business Activity:** Frequently exchanging coins for others, finding buyers/sellers, charging fees or spreads, and using your accounts to process funds for third parties constitutes illegal financial activity.

The law looks at behavior rather than self-declaration. If you receive money from a friend to buy USDT, facilitate trades in a chat group for commissions, or lend your bank card and exchange account to others, you are likely engaging in illegal business operations, regardless of how you label it.

### 4. Boundary Three: Disputes and Self-Borne Risks
A common misconception is that transactions are protected by civil law if there are contracts or chat logs. The 2026 Notice clarifies that investment in virtual currency and related financial products, if it violates public order and good morals, renders the related civil legal behavior invalid. Consequently, losses are borne by the investor themselves.

This does not mean every contract is automatically void, but it signals that crypto disputes are not handled like standard banking or securities disputes. Investors must assume responsibility for:
*   Sending funds to wrong addresses.
*   Entrusting funds to "gurus" who disappear.
*   Project failures or platform bans.
*   Market volatility.

Promises of "guaranteed returns" or "no loss" are essentially worthless in the event of a dispute. The principle is clear: your position, your risk.

### 5. Boundary Four: The Dangers of Fund Sources and Usage
Beyond market price volatility, the most severe risks involve the source and use of funds. The Notice highlights that virtual currency is frequently used for:
*   Money laundering
*   Fraud and illegal fundraising
*   Illegal business operations
*   Illegal cross-border fund transfers

**High-Risk Scenarios to Avoid:**
*   Allowing strangers to send money to your bank card, which you then convert to USDT and transfer out.
*   Converting RMB to foreign currency via crypto to earn spreads (potentially illegal foreign exchange trading).
*   Participating in "quantitative arbitrage" or "steady profit" projects that centralize funds.

While a single interaction with suspicious funds does not automatically make one a criminal, liability depends on knowledge, transaction patterns, and abnormal profits. The safest rule is to never lend bank cards, payment codes, or exchange accounts, and to refuse all "payment channel" or "money mule" jobs.

### 6. Boundary Five: Pre-Transaction Due Diligence
Before engaging in any transaction, individuals should verify three critical factors:
1.  **Fund Source:** The money must be your own, and you must be able to explain its origin clearly.
2.  **Fund Usage:** Are you buying for yourself, or are you acting as an agent for someone else? If you are helping others buy, transfer, or process funds, pause and reconsider.
3.  **Account Ownership:** Use only your own real-name verified accounts (bank, payment, and exchange). Never lend them or accept third-party account substitutions.

If any of these three points are unclear, do not proceed out of fear of missing out. Market opportunities will return, but entanglement in financial disputes can have long-lasting consequences.

### Summary: The Core Takeaway
For ordinary individuals in mainland China, the 2026 regulatory framework establishes a clear boundary:
*   **Holding or occasional personal trading is not a crime.**
*   **However, it is not legally protected.** All risks, including scams, contract liquidations, and fraud, are borne by the individual.
*   **Business activities are strictly prohibited.** You cannot act as an intermediary, payment channel, or matchmaker.

If you decide to proceed, ensure your funds are clean, your accounts are yours, and you understand that you are operating in a space where the legal system will not bail you out.
