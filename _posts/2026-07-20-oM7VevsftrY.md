## A Market Built on Promises and Parabolic Dispersion

Jim Chanos, founder and president of Kynikos Associates, recently joined the *Risk Reversal* podcast to discuss the current state of the AI boom. Known for his bearish views during the dot-com crash, Chanos argues that the current AI bubble is structurally more dangerous than the late 1990s tech frenzy. He points to extreme stock dispersion, record issuance, and a fundamental mismatch between long-term capital expenditure and short-term spot pricing.

### The Three Legs of the Current Bubble

Chanos describes the current market as a bull market that puts a "premium on promises" rather than a discount on reality. He identifies three key characteristics that signal a potential turning point, reminiscent of the first half of 2021:

1.  **Rich Valuations:** Stocks are trading at historically high multiples.
2.  **Retail Speculation:** Increased participation from retail investors driving volatility.
3.  **Heavy Issuance:** A surge in IPOs, secondary offerings, and insider selling.

Chanos notes that while the S&P 500 remains near all-time highs, there is tremendous dispersion under the surface. Large-cap tech stocks can move 5% to 10% in a single day based on flows and sentiment rather than fundamental news. This volatility is concentrated in individual stocks while broader market volatility indices remain low, creating a fragile environment.

### The Dot-Com Comparison: Scale and Leverage

When asked to compare the current AI build-out to the dot-com era, Chanos states it is "much worse." During the late 1990s, the entire fiber-optic and competitive local exchange carrier (CLEC) industries spent approximately $100 billion over five years. In contrast, individual companies today are raising and spending hundreds of billions on AI infrastructure.

*   **Dot-Com Era:** Spending was driven by profitable enterprises like Coca-Cola, GE, and Merrill Lynch networking their computers. When the bubble burst, these companies cut orders, causing earnings to collapse, but the spenders themselves remained profitable.
*   **AI Era:** Spending is driven by hyperscalers, but a vast ecosystem of unprofitable "neo-clouds" and data center firms is raising massive amounts of VC money and debt. The capital expenditure relative to the size of the economy dwarfs the 1999–2000 period.

### The Danger of Spot Pricing and "Construction in Progress"

A central theme of Chanos’s analysis is the fallacy of building long-term assets based on short-term spot prices. He argues that companies are committing to 20-year data center projects based on current high rental rates, which may not persist.

*   **Accounting Illusions:** Chanos highlights the "construction in progress" line item on balance sheets. Hyperscalers are capitalizing billions in chips and infrastructure that are not yet plugged in or generating revenue. Because these assets are not yet depreciated, they do not hit the income statement, artificially inflating earnings per share (EPS).
*   **Obsolescence Risk:** Tech cycles are short. Chanos notes that GPUs and DRAM become technologically obsolete rapidly, yet companies are using depreciation models of 5–6 years, or even deferring depreciation for 18 months after purchase. Chanos’s own models use a 10-year depreciation schedule, yet he still struggles to pencil out the economics for many data center projects.

### Valuation Disparities and the Nvidia Benchmark

Chanos emphasizes that Nvidia is the primary "gatekeeper and price maker" in this boom. He asserts a simple valuation rule: no company dependent on Nvidia chips should trade at a higher valuation than Nvidia itself. Yet, many data center firms and memory suppliers like Micron trade at premiums.

*   **Micron Example:** Chanos questions the market’s faith in Micron’s long-term agreements (LTAs) and order books. He points out that Micron had negative gross margins just three years ago, yet the market is pricing in sustained 80% gross margins. He argues that if Nvidia’s margins eventually decline due to competition, the valuations of dependent companies will face severe downward pressure.
*   **Neo-Cloud Pivot:** Chanos cites Nebius, a major neo-cloud player, which recently pivoted to an asset-light model. After previously advocating for heavy capital intensity as a moat, Nebius now plans to manage assets owned by others. Chanos views this as an admission that the heavy capex model is flawed.

### Declining Returns on Invested Capital (ROIC)

Chanos warns that the return on incremental invested capital for hyperscalers is falling rapidly. He presents data showing that the return on marginal capex for the group (Google, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, Oracle) has dropped from ~40% a year and a half ago to ~20% today. If spending continues at current rates, this return could fall to 10%, making it difficult for CEOs to justify trillions in future capex when risk-free Treasury yields offer comparable returns with less risk.

Oracle, in particular, is highlighted as having the worst ROIC metrics, with its stock down significantly from highs despite massive deal announcements. Chanos suggests that other hyperscalers are watching Oracle as a cautionary tale.

### Energy Constraints vs. Economic Reality

Addressing the narrative that AI growth is limited by power availability, Chanos is skeptical. He notes that power costs represent only 5–6% of data center revenues, making it the smallest cost component. He argues that the US is not short of power, and while transmission and regulatory issues exist, they are solvable. He believes political backlash may be a larger threat than physical energy scarcity.

### Conclusion: A Splash of Cold Water

Chanos concludes that while AI will undoubtedly create wealth and produce huge winners, the current market is valuing everything as if it has already succeeded. He compares the situation to the dot-com era, where demand was assumed to be infinite. He predicts a "splash of cold water" when the laws of economics reassert themselves, order books collapse, and the market shifts from rewarding promises to discounting reality.

"In bull markets, you put a premium on promises, and in bear markets, you put a discount on reality," Chanos said. "And we're clearly in the former right now. Do we get to the latter? I don't know. But the signs are there."
