*An exclusive interview with the co-founder of Allegiance Gold on the $5,000 milestone, inflation, and the path to $10,000+ gold.*

---

**Charlotte Mloud, Investing News:** Today I’m joined by Alex Ebrahian, co-founder of Allegiance Gold. Alex, thank you for being here.

**Alex Ebrahian:** Thanks for having me, Charlotte.

### From Wall Street to Gold: A Personal Journey

**Charlotte:** Let’s start with your introduction. How did you come to the gold industry?

**Alex:** I started my career at Smith Barney, which was acquired by Morgan Stanley. I was a banker for about 12 years, financing publicly traded companies, private equity, and large real estate projects. Over the last decade, I’ve been building Allegiance Gold.

It’s unconventional to move from finance into the physical gold world. My perspective comes from personal experience. I was born and raised in Beirut, Lebanon, and at a very young age, I experienced civil war and currency fluctuation. I vividly remember my parents exchanging a portion of their currency into physical gold.

The 2008 crisis was a stark reminder that you can’t just depend on the system. Even with a background in finance and statistics, I couldn’t anticipate how bad the economy was or how my parents were impacted. That was a lesson learned. I went back and started asking: *What is money, and why isn’t it taught in school?* Wall Street doesn’t want you to know a lot about it—I remember at Smith Barney, banks really discouraged people. And obviously, governments don’t want you to own a lot of it.

That catalyst began my questioning of conventional wisdom. Today, Allegiance Gold is a full-service physical precious metals dealer. We’ve been in business for almost a decade, focusing on education, relationship, and full transparency.

### Gold’s Position in the Cycle: The Fourth or Fifth Inning

**Charlotte:** Let’s begin with the big picture. Where do you see gold in the cycle right now?

**Alex:** Anytime you have a bull market in gold—if you study history from the 1970s to early 2000s—it’s typically a 10 to 12-year cycle. For me, the last starting point was 2020. At that point, every government overprinted money to support people during the pandemic. Essentially, we sacrificed the currency to save the economy.

Then in 2022, another catalyst emerged when the US and Western banks seized Russian assets. The world started paying attention: if you’re not fully aligned with the US, there’s national risk. Thereafter, you have central banks—the “insiders of the money game”—deciding interest rates and money printing. We’ve seen central bank gold buying jump from an average of 400–600 tons to over 1,000 tons in recent years. In 2025, it was roughly 900 tons.

I pay attention to their direction. A World Gold Council survey last June showed almost 79% of central banks said they are on a path to hold more gold. Why? Because it’s a neutral asset. Once you have it in your jurisdiction, it can’t be seized by Western banks or the US government.

If the cycle started around 2020, with gold’s low around $1,500, and today we’re about $5,000, we’re in the fourth or fifth inning of this bull market. The second half of the cycle moves faster than the first. The fundamentals are the same: unprecedented deficit spending, growing debt, pressure to lower interest rates, and central banks quietly buying more gold.

### Geopolitical Turmoil & Gold: Why the Price Isn’t Higher

**Charlotte:** A big question from our audience: with conflict in the Middle East, why isn’t gold—a safe-haven asset—higher right now?

**Alex:** Gold is not responding to war. It’s responding to the fact that the Federal Reserve might not lower interest rates as anticipated. Let me explain.

You have a trifecta affecting inflation:
1.  **Higher oil prices.** Oil went from about $50 a barrel at the start of the year to over $100. While the US has intervened by releasing reserves and removing sanctions on Russian oil, oil is a global commodity. Sustained high prices have a triple effect on inflation.
2.  **Increased food costs.** One-third of fertilizers comes from the Strait of Hormuz. If fertilizer costs rise in February and March—key buying months for farmers—it trickles down to production and food costs.
3.  **Prolonged war spending.** Originally forecast at $50–255 billion, the war continues. We are printing money to pay the deficit. Our $2 trillion deficit has interest payments representing nearly half—about a trillion dollars. Does it make sense to borrow more just to pay interest?

These three components point to much higher inflation. That will force the Fed’s hand *not* to cut rates. The market, including oil forecasts, had factored in lower rates. So, gold is responding to higher anticipated inflation, which deters rate cuts. That’s why we’ve seen gold trade between $5,000 and $5,200 recently.

### Inflation in 2026: The Hidden Reality

**Charlotte:** Looking ahead to 2026, with the Fed struggling to hit 2% inflation, where could inflation go?

**Alex:** It depends on how long energy prices stay high. Estimates suggest a $10 increase per barrel of oil results in a 0.1% to 0.3% rise in inflation. We’ve gone up about $50. That implies a 0.5% to 2% increase.

But remember, the government’s inflation measure only looks at the last 12 months. February’s numbers came in at about 2.4%, on par with expectations. The average consumer knows inflation is much higher. If a price goes from $100 to $150, and then to $195, reported inflation appears to be decelerating, but you’re still paying more.

I don’t pay much attention to the official percentage. Since 1980, how it’s measured has changed—it could be manipulated. The same goes for GDP. We just revised down February job numbers to 92,000 and Q4 GDP from 1.4% to 0.7%.

I look at the disconnect between the real economy and the stock market. The market is down only about 2% year-to-date, but we expect more. When job numbers drop, consumer confidence and spending drop. The US economy is roughly 70% consumer spending.

Higher oil prices affect corporate earnings. We’re also seeing cracks in the banking system. Recently, BlackRock and Morgan Stanley said they are minimizing redemptions in the private credit market. That market is about $1–3 trillion. When defaults rise because consumers can’t pay, it has a triple effect.

The bond market is signaling weakness, especially in BBB-rated bonds where companies keep borrowing despite unhealthy balance sheets. If the entire system depends on more borrowing, there comes a point where it won’t function properly.

In 2008, oil was $140 a barrel, but we were only producing about 5 million barrels. Today, the market can’t withstand that—corporate earnings would drop, bond ratings would fall, and banking liquidity would be affected.

Don’t look at gold pulling back from $5,200 to $5,000 and think it’s a falling knife. Gold is still up 17% year-to-date and about 65% in the last year. Gold’s intention isn’t for the short term; it’s for the mid to long term. Understand the fundamentals of debt, money printing, interest rates, and where we are in the economic cycle. Goldman Sachs just increased its recession probability from 20% to 25%. People need to ask: how do I position myself so that if something happens, I have something outside the system?

### Stagflation & The Economic Outlook

**Charlotte:** A word surfacing again is *stagflation*. Do you see that scenario unfolding?

**Alex:** Yes and no.
*   **No**, because the government can influence GDP numbers. War spending on the military complex boosts large goods production.
*   **Yes**, because if we continuously see GDP revisions downward, the math fails. The premise of recent policy was that deregulation, increased debt, and lower taxes would spur growth to compensate for higher debt servicing. If that doesn’t work, it will have a big impact.

Midterm elections are around the corner. Affordability will be a key issue. President Trump allowed Russia to sell oil because he understands high oil prices come back home—impacting corporate earnings and consumers, which has political implications.

I’m watching the Q1 GDP numbers due in April/May. They’ll give consensus on the Supreme Court’s trade decision impact. The Strait of Hormuz is a battlefield not just for oil, but for currency dominance. Iran has sent mixed signals about allowing oil exports only in yuan. This geopolitical aspect is crucial—everything is connected: the dollar, oil, gold, and markets.

If Q1 GDP drops and inflation hikes, you’ll see that baked into the gold price.

### The Fed, Banking Cracks, and What’s Next

**Charlotte:** The next Fed meeting is this week. What will you be watching?

**Alex:** The CME forecast shows a 99.9% chance nothing happens this meeting. The Fed may not have enough data, and it’s almost a political decision. Jerome Powell may be replaced by Kevin Warsh, who is known to dislike inflating the balance sheet. But his conviction will be tested because the market and President Trump are pushing for lower rates.

In an environment where inflation might be increasing, you actually want higher rates, not lower. Conventional central banking wisdom says to bring down inflation, you raise rates—something Powell did previously.

I’m paying attention to their rhetoric on trade and job numbers. Are they seeing higher volatility or concerns we haven’t seen yet? The banking sector is where cracks will show. A bank sits between depositors’ money and its loans. If loans have problems or impairments, it affects lending and underwriting.

The central bank is asking: can we continue on this path? They’ll look at GDP, which the government can influence. If you think of the US government as a corporation, don’t just look at the $38–39 trillion national debt. Consider unfunded liabilities and all debt compared to GDP—it’s three times worse than reported. As long as we can increase GDP on paper and keep inflation at bay, the status quo continues.

I don’t anticipate much movement from this meeting. I do anticipate political rhetoric from President Trump calling out Powell for not lowering rates. It’s premature for the Fed to cut when they anticipate inflation rising. Remember, reported inflation and unemployment are backward-looking. Consumer spending and corporate confidence will be key forward indicators.

### Price Targets: Gold to $10,000-$12,000, Silver to Challenge $100

**Charlotte:** Do you have a price target for gold in 2026?

**Alex:** Short-term, expect volatility with about 20–25% standard deviation on both sides. Our forecast at the start of the year was $6,000+. I see gold in a 10–12 year bull cycle starting in 2020. Historically, when gold enters a bull market, you see a 7–8x move from its low to its high. My personal forecast is by 2030–2032, we could see prices between $10,000 and $12,000.

**Charlotte:** What about silver?

**Alex:** I think silver this year will challenge the three-digit level. We could see another $100 level. The move from $30 to $40 was methodical, but $40 to $60 and $80 to $120 was very fast. The fundamentals are still there. This pullback is healthy to solidify support.

Mid-to-long term, our forecast for the next year is around the $100 level. Remember, government buying for the military complex is an unknown, unreported demand factor. The Silver Institute doesn’t include it. We have a structural deficit, demand exceeding supply, and allocated silver on the CME has dropped from about 124 to 80 since the start of the year.

There are many indications that the physical and paper silver markets should decouple. In due time, we will see that again. The bid-ask spread in physical has narrowed. When silver peaked between $110–120, that gap widened. I see that not as a penalty, but as a signal some are taking profits.

Silver is indispensable with growing demand. It’s not about timing short-term volatility; it’s about positioning. In metals, you need time. Who would have thought at the start of the year we’d be at war in February? Maybe President Trump knew, but the public didn’t factor that in.

**Charlotte:** On that decoupling of physical and paper silver—what would be the trigger?

**Alex:** Look at the macro picture. It wasn’t a surprise the US engaged with Venezuela. On the supply side, Mexico and Peru are top producers. China has had significant influence in South America’s mining sector. If you combine growing industrial demand (including unreported government demand) with a tightening physical supply, the conditions for a decoupling intensify. It’s a matter of when sustained physical demand overwhelms the paper pricing mechanism.
