## Introduction

For decades, financial markets have operated under the assumption that central banks, particularly the Federal Reserve, hold the reins of economic destiny. The prevailing question for investors was always: "When will the Fed cut or raise rates?" However, Michael Every, Global Strategist at Rabo Bank, argues that this paradigm is obsolete. In a recent interview with Capital Cosm host Danny, Every posits that the global economy is transitioning from a liquidity-driven system to one dictated by physical scarcity and geopolitical control. As oil prices surge and supply chains fracture, central banks are discovering that while they can print money, they cannot print molecules.

## The Middle East Flashpoint: Beyond the Strait of Hormuz

The immediate catalyst for this shift is the escalating conflict in the Middle East. While much of the market focus remains on the Strait of Hormuz, Every points to a more complex and dangerous reality unfolding in Yemen and the Red Sea.

Recent military clashes between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis have resulted in strikes on Saudi oil facilities. More critically, the Houthis have captured the key Red Sea port of Mokha. This move places them on the cusp of controlling the Bab-el-Mandeb Strait, a second global choke point for energy transport.

"The Houthis are not independent actors," Every notes. "They are pretty much funded... and directed and encouraged by the Iranians."

The potential damage extends beyond maritime chokepoints. Speculation is mounting that the Houthis may have damaged the Saudi East-West pipeline. This pipeline is crucial because it allows oil to bypass the Strait of Hormuz by running to the west coast port of Yanbu. If both Hormuz and Bab-el-Mandeb are compromised, the primary route for Middle Eastern oil to reach Asia is severed. Oil would then have to travel north through the Suez Canal on smaller vessels and around Africa, adding weeks to shipping times and drastically increasing costs.

## The Refined Product Crisis

A common misconception among investors is that high oil prices are solely a function of crude supply. Every emphasizes that the more critical issue is the shortage of refined products: diesel, jet fuel, and bunker fuel.

"Central banks... how are they going to magic up diesel? How are they going to magic up bunker fuel or jet fuel? They're not," Every states bluntly.

The global refining capacity is under severe strain due to two main factors:
1.  **Ukraine’s Campaign:** Ukrainian drones have effectively targeted Russian oil refineries, reducing global refining output.
2.  **Domestic Deficits:** The US and Europe lack sufficient refinery capacity to process crude into usable fuels, even if crude is available.

Diesel prices in the US have hit nominal highs of $6 per gallon, with reports of $9.99 in some California markets. With stocks vastly below their 15-year averages, the physical shortage of energy inputs is driving inflation in a way that monetary policy cannot address.

## The Failure of Traditional Monetary Policy

In this new environment, the tools of the Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank (ECB) appear inadequate. Liquidity injections or interest rate adjustments cannot create physical energy.

Every highlights the ECB’s recent stance, noting that the bank is aware that crushing demand through higher rates is a "self-inflicted wound." It is akin to cutting off a limb to cure gangrene. While it may stop the infection (inflation), it causes massive damage to the body (the economy).

The market currently prices in a 25 basis point rate hike by the Fed, with expectations of rates reaching only 4.5% to 4.75% by October 2027. Every argues this is a "drop in the bucket" compared to the real inflation driven by energy costs. Interestingly, Rabo Bank’s house view, led by US strategist Philip Morray, actually anticipates two rate cuts, arguing that if higher rates don't solve the supply-side problem, lower rates channeled into productive capacity might help more than tightening belts.

## Geopolitical Realignment: The "Mecca Alliance" and Hard Power

The energy crisis is also reshaping alliances. The recently announced "Mecca Alliance" between Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan has been tested immediately by the Houthi threat. However, Every describes the agreement as a "nothing burger" in its current form.

Pakistan and Turkey have declined to invoke defense clauses, citing "pre-existing conditions" in the conflict. This reveals a stark truth: Saudi Arabia lacks hard power. Despite its economic weight in the GCC, it cannot militarily secure its own energy infrastructure. Meanwhile, Turkey, possessing significant military strength, is hesitant to engage due to historical baggage and tensions with Israel.

This power vacuum forces the US into a difficult position. With munitions stocks depleted—particularly precision-guided missiles that allow for risk-free engagement—the US is reluctant to intervene. "If the message is we refuse to fight any wars where there is any risk of having a fighter jet shot down, then yeah, the US is short," Every warns. This perceived reluctance undermines the US position as a global hegemon.

## The US-China Bond Yield Divergence

A clear indicator of this geopolitical shift is the widening spread between US and Chinese 10-year bond yields. US yields are spiking near 5%, while Chinese yields remain below 2%.

Every attributes this divergence to structural differences:
*   **China:** Faces deflationary pressure due to overcapacity and acts as a non-free market with capital controls and state-directed buying.
*   **US:** Faces inflationary pressure from supply shortages and operates as a free market where yields reflect genuine risk and inflation expectations.

The US Treasury is increasingly intervening in bond markets, buying back long-maturity debt to suppress yields. Every views this as the US beginning to mimic aspects of China’s state-capitalist economic statecraft to compete effectively. "You cannot geostrategically try and go toe-to-toe with a state capitalist... economy like China... and say let freedom rule. It won't work," he argues.

## Conclusion: Territory Over Liquidity

The overarching theme of Michael Every’s analysis is that "territory, not liquidity" is now the primary driver of financial markets. Bond yields are being dictated by energy prices, which are in turn driven by realpolitik and military outcomes on the ground.

Central banks are "prisoners" to this dynamic. They cannot solve physical shortages with monetary policy. As the world faces the potential for a combined economic and military threat from Russia, Iran, North Korea, and China, the US and its allies are struggling to maintain the old order.

For investors, the implication is clear: following the Fed is no longer enough. Understanding the flow of physical commodities, the stability of geopolitical alliances, and the realities of hard power is essential for navigating the new financial landscape. As Every concludes, the era of assuming central banks control the narrative is over; the era of controlling the physical world has begun.
