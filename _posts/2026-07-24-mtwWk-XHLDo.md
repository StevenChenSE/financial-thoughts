## The Illusion of Perpetual Growth
Michael Hudson argues that the US stock market is currently experiencing a "slow crash," a reality that many investors are reluctant to accept. For 80 years, from 1945 to 2025, Western economies enjoyed a continuous upward trajectory. However, Hudson contends that this trend has reached its structural end. The hesitancy to recognize this shift is profound, as many market participants still attempt to project past trends into the future, ignoring the fundamental changes in the global economic landscape.

## Pension Fund Capitalism and Wealth Inequality
A core driver of the current market dynamics is what Hudson terms "pension fund socialism" or "pension fund capitalism." In the United States, social security is not paid out of the public budget on a pay-as-you-go basis. Instead, the system requires the American labor force to pre-save for retirement by investing in the stock market. This is enforced through heavy taxes on labor, approximately 15% of paychecks, which are then funneled into the market.

This mechanism creates a stock market boom for asset holders, exacerbating wealth inequality. The top 10% of the American population owns nearly 90% of all stocks. Consequently, pension funds are used to push up stock prices, capital gains, and financial fortunes for the wealthy elite, particularly the top 1%. Families are encouraged to save for retirement by buying stocks, but this links their financial security directly to a market rigged for asset price inflation.

## Insider Selling and the Shift to Safety
While retail investors hold on, large investors and insiders understand that the game is over. Hudson notes that major investors have already sold out of the stock market, moving their capital into government bonds. The goal is no longer to make a profit or capital gain but to preserve principal safety against impending losses.

The market is vulnerable to a sudden break in the chain of payments, which could trigger a rapid crash. This break could occur in various sectors, such as transport companies, airlines, or cities facing bankruptcy. Hudson highlights the rise of "crapification" and "shitification" of the economy, driven by private capital companies. These entities often borrow up to 90% of the funds needed to buy corporations, then lay off workers and sell off assets to enrich themselves, leaving the economy in ruins.

## Global De-Dollarization and Sovereignty
The impact of this economic squeeze will be most severe in countries that cannot print their own currency to pay foreign debts, including nations in the Global South, Europe, and Asia. The United States has increasingly acted as a "pirate state," confiscating foreign currencies and reserves. Examples include seizing Iran's savings, confiscating $300 billion of Russia's reserves via Euroclear, and grabbing the assets of Iraq, Libya, and Venezuela.

This behavior has accelerated de-dollarization. Countries are realizing that holding reserves in US dollars or keeping gold in London or the New York Federal Reserve basement poses a significant risk. They are now reclaiming their gold and asserting sovereignty over their trade relationships. De-dollarization represents a shift where nations decide whom to trade with, what currencies to use, and where to keep their reserves, rejecting US attempts to control their economic policies through sanctions and financial coercion.

## Iran, China, and the New Geopolitical Reality
Iran exemplifies this shift. Facing pressure from the US, Iran has announced it will control the Strait of Hormuz, potentially charging tolls or service fees to guide ships through mine-infested waters. While the US has offered a 60-day window for Iran to accept dollars without confiscation, Iran remains skeptical of US promises.

Instead, Iran is selling most of its oil to China and accepting payment in Chinese currency. This strategy provides geopolitical leverage, as China has a vested interest in protecting Iranian tankers from US seizure. The US is unlikely to risk a military showdown with China over confiscated oil. This dynamic underscores the broader trend: nations are moving away from the dollar to protect their assets and maintain economic independence, recognizing that the US uses its financial power to enforce political control and seize wealth.
