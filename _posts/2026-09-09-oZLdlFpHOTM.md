### Escalating Middle East tensions and US diesel export policies drive energy prices higher, driving bond yields and fueling a "super spike" scenario.

## Introduction
Oil markets are once again surging on the back of renewed geopolitical escalations in the Middle East, with crude prices approaching the $100-per-barrel mark. As of September 8th, the situation has been further complicated by a shift in the nature of the conflict: the Houthis have struck a key Saudi refinery, and Iran has reportedly fired sophisticated missiles at US warships. These events have spooked global markets, but the more immediate domestic impact in the United States is the surge in diesel prices. US diesel has hit an all-time high of $5.90 a gallon, a figure that is critical because over 76% of commercial trucks in the US run on diesel, directly affecting the cost of transporting goods, including groceries.

## The Geopolitical Flashpoint
The recent escalation involves a complex web of actors, including the Houthis, the Saudi government, and the US military. The US recently hit three Iranian tankers in retaliation for Iranian missiles being fired at US warships. While US Central Command denied claims that a US carrier and a guided missile destroyer were directly hit, the mere fact that missiles were fired and missed has heightened tensions.

Doomberg, head writer of the Doomberg Substack, notes that the market is pricing in the risk of "uncontrolled escalation." If the escalation ladder escapes the control of the main actors and critical assets are knocked offline for years, a "real super spike" in oil prices could occur. The attack on the Saudi refinery is particularly significant because it shifts the chokepoint in the supply chain from crude oil to refined products. Crude is worthless until it reaches a refinery, and if refineries are targeted, the lack of diesel reserves compared to crude reserves makes the economic damage more severe.

## The Diesel Spread and US Export Dilemma
A major driver of the current price pressure is the "diesel crack spread." The US is the world's largest net exporter of diesel, a fact that complicates the domestic price picture. While the US produces enough diesel for its own needs, refiners are running at near-maximum capacity and exporting the surplus to Europe, Japan, and other markets to take advantage of higher global prices. This is a geopolitical choice by the Trump administration, which has allowed domestic prices to rise while allowing refiners to "print money" and stabilize global markets.

Doomberg points out that the US could easily lower domestic diesel prices by implementing export controls or an export tax, a power the President has had since the war began but has not yet exercised. The total competitive diesel market is roughly 7 million barrels per day. The US exported about 1.3 million barrels last year but has increased this to 1.9 million to help fill the gap left by Russia, which has closed off diesel exports to preserve its domestic supply.

## Canada’s Leverage and Trade War Risks
The trade war with Canada adds another layer of risk. Canada holds "escalation dominance" in this specific argument because it can limit exports of heavy crude from Alberta to the US. This heavy crude is sold at a significant discount and is a key input for US refiners. Doomberg argues that while the US military is far stronger, the threat of a military conflict is not a credible escalation path in a trade dispute. However, the threat of an export tax on Albertan crude is a direct and potent economic weapon that could further strain US energy supplies.

## Bond Yields and the Petrodollar
The surge in energy prices is also having a profound effect on financial markets, particularly US Treasury yields. There is a near-perfect correlation between WTI crude oil prices and the 10-year US Treasury yield. The explanation is straightforward: countries that must buy oil in US dollars must sell their US Treasuries to generate the cash needed for the purchase. This creates a demand for shorter-duration cash and reduces demand for long-term debt, pushing yields higher.

This dynamic challenges the narrative that the "petrodollar" is on its way out. While the US dollar's hegemony is declining, the dollar remains a powerful conduit for energy transactions. The ability of the US to weaponize the Treasury system through sanctions further underscores the strength of the dollar, even as the neutrality of US assets is eroded.

## The Role of China
China has emerged as a critical variable in the oil market. By overbuilding its energy infrastructure—including coal-to-liquids, LNG capacity, and electric vehicles—China has created a buffer that allows it to buffer a sudden drop of 5 to 6 million barrels per day in crude imports. This "flex capacitor" has prevented oil prices from spiking to the $150 or $200 levels that some analysts predicted when the Strait of Hormuz was threatened. However, China can also use this power geopolitically, re-entering the market aggressively to drive prices up if it suits its interests, as seen by the recent premium on Shanghai crude.

## Conclusion
The current energy landscape is defined by a combination of geopolitical instability, US export policies, and the strategic maneuvering of major powers like China and China. The "super spike" scenario is a real possibility if the conflict in the Middle East escalates further, particularly if more refineries are targeted. For the US, the decision to continue exporting diesel at the expense of domestic prices is a significant political and economic trade-off. As the Federal Reserve navigates the impact of energy prices on inflation and interest rates, the interplay between oil, bond yields, and the petrodollar will remain a central theme in the global economy.
