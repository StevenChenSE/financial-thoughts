Despite intermittent headlines about recovering oil transit flows and ongoing ceasefire negotiations, global crude and refined product markets remain severely strained. With crude oil hovering near high levels and diesel prices touching record peaks, consumers and investors face persistent cost pressures. 

In a detailed discussion, Rory Johnston, founder of Commodity Context, breaks down the core mechanics driving this energy squeeze, the geopolitical realities in key chokepoints, the distillate crisis, and why quick relief remains elusive.

## 1. The Strait of Hormuz: Recovery vs. Reality

While recent reports highlight that crude exports through the Strait of Hormuz have rebounded to roughly 14 to 17.5 million barrels per day (mb/d), the market is far from returning to pre-war normality. 

* **The Lingering Deficit:** Even with current throughput improvements, roughly 7 mb/d of typical pre-war transit remains disrupted. In any standard market environment, a multi-million-barrel shortfall represents an extreme supply shock.
* **Massive Cost Overheads:** Moving crude through contested waters carries immense financial friction. Shipping, insurance, and security convoys now push transit costs to upwards of $30 per barrel compared to the pre-war baseline of around $2 per barrel. To attract tankers willing to navigate these risks, producers such as Iraq have had to offer extraordinary discounts (sometimes $30 to $50 per barrel) to clear inventory.
* **Data Nuance and Dark Transits:** Tracking actual vessel traffic requires moving beyond daily Automatic Identification System (AIS) snapshots. With ships turning off transponders for dark shuttle transits and conducting ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman, analysts rely on rolling averages and satellite data to map the true pace of recovery.

## 2. Stock and Flow: Why High Prices Persist Despite Higher Flows

A common question among market observers is why prices remain elevated if flows have improved compared to earlier crisis troughs. The answer lies in the fundamental stock-and-flow model of commodity markets:

1. **Depleted Buffers:** The market operated in a persistent flow deficit for over six months. During that time, commercial inventories, operational buffers, and Strategic Petroleum Reserves (SPRs) were heavily drawn down.
2. **The Need for Prolonged Surplus:** Prices cannot reliably decline merely because supply nears equilibrium; the global system must generate a sustained surplus over a prolonged period to rebuild depleted storage cushions.
3. **Severe Backwardation:** The crude futures curve remains steeply backwardated—where prompt barrels command a steep premium over forward contracts. This structure reflects immediate, acute physical demand rather than long-term optimism, illustrating how tight physical balances still are on the ground.

## 3. The Acute Crisis in Diesel and Middle Distillates

While crude oil attracts the majority of headlines, the distillate market—particularly diesel—presents the most acute bottleneck in the global energy system. Crack spreads have widened to historic highs, with diesel trading at roughly double the price of crude.

### Core Drivers of the Distillate Shortage

* **Regional Refinery Damage:** Conflicts and attacks across key refining facilities in the Middle East and Red Sea region have removed significant product export capacity, skewing regional throughput heavily toward raw crude rather than refined fuel.
* **Disruption of Russian Exports:** Ukrainian strikes on Russian energy infrastructure have knocked substantial domestic refining capacity offline. As Russia—historically the world's second-largest exporter of diesel and gas oil—curtailed and banned exports, 1.5 to 2 mb/d of global diesel supply disappeared from the market.
* **High U.S. Refinery Utilization:** U.S. refiners are operating at over 92–95% capacity to capture margins and meet export demand, leaving virtually no spare domestic capacity to expand output further.

## 4. The Dilemma of U.S. Diesel Export Restrictions

With domestic diesel prices crossing historic highs above $6.50 per gallon, political pressure in Washington has raised the prospect of export restrictions ahead of upcoming elections. However, policy interventions carry significant structural tradeoffs:

* **Short-Term Domestic Impact:** A full blanket ban might trap excess Gulf Coast product domestically, providing temporary retail relief in the short run.
* **Global and Structural Repercussions:** Severing U.S. exports—which currently run near 1.5 mb/d—would instantly spike international prices. Domestically, collapsing refining margins would cause refiners to reduce throughput, inadvertently cutting the production of gasoline, jet fuel, and other critical coproducts.
* **Likely Policy Path:** Rather than a catastrophic blanket ban, policymakers are far more likely to consider modest, targeted quotas or licensing adjustments that limit political friction without collapsing refining operations.

## 5. The Role of China as the Global Swing Refiner

Given that Western and Russian refining systems are constrained or disrupted, China represents the primary source of underutilized global refining capacity capable of altering the supply balance.

* **The "Beijing Swing":** Earlier in the crisis, China shielded global balances by drastically cutting crude imports (by over 5 mb/d at the trough) and drawing down domestic crude and unobservable refined product stockpiles to meet domestic demand.
* **Export Quota Potential:** If Beijing chooses to loosen export restrictions and grant larger quotas to independent and state refiners—similar to late 2022—it could introduce hundreds of thousands of barrels per day of middle distillates to the global market, providing immediate downward pressure on distillate crack spreads.

## Summary and Outlook

The broader commodity complex remains tightly tethered to the energy market. Because diesel is an essential input across agriculture, logistics, manufacturing, and raw material extraction, high fuel costs act as a broad tax across the real economy. 

Until sustained physical supply surpluses emerge—either through comprehensive conflict resolutions or a significant release of global refining capacity—energy markets are expected to remain tightly wound, with risk skewed toward elevated prices and broader economic headwinds.
