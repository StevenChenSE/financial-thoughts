In an era when conventional wisdom dictates that soaring gas prices crush consumer spending and throttle economic growth, one veteran energy investor is flipping the script. Josh Young, Chief Investment Officer at Bison Interests, argues that the ongoing Strait of Hormuz crisis—and the higher oil prices it has unleashed—may ultimately prove beneficial for American workers, particularly those at the lower end of the income spectrum. His thesis hinges on a counterintuitive but economically grounded observation: when oil prices rise, wages tend to follow.

"The sort of inflation that you're actually looking for, which is people getting paid more, tends to be very highly correlated with oil prices," Young explained during a recent interview. "In the short term, it's terrible. In the medium term, it's fantastic."

This perspective challenges the knee-jerk assumption that energy price spikes automatically translate into economic pain. Instead, Young sees the current crisis as a potential catalyst for wage growth—particularly for workers who have historically struggled to gain economic traction.

## The Strait of Hormuz Standoff: What's Really Happening

The Strait of Hormuz, through which approximately 20% of the world's oil flows, has been effectively closed for approximately two and a half months. This single geopolitical flashpoint has created a massive bottleneck in global energy markets, with significant implications for producers, consumers, and investors alike.

### The China Factor

Recent diplomatic overtures between the United States and China have raised hopes that the strait might reopen. President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping reportedly agreed during talks in Beijing that the waterway must remain open. However, Young expresses deep skepticism about the substance behind these headlines.

"My read on this is that the Trump administration asked China to stop supplying Iran with weapons," Young said. "What they do supply Iran with are the precursors to these weapons, and they have not agreed to stop supplying those."

Young points to a telling detail: recent tankers that managed to leave the Strait of Hormuz were almost exclusively Chinese-owned or operated, bringing oil and oil products directly to China. This pattern suggests Beijing has been a net beneficiary of the crisis—politically, through a weakened U.S. presence in the region, and economically, through preferential access to constrained oil supplies.

"I don't think they want anything to change," Young concluded. "I don't see anything from this meeting that was indicative of any sort of progress towards ending the Iran war."

### A Complicated Picture for American Policy

For investors wondering whether the Strait will reopen, Young advocates caution. His firm maintains a diversified approach: the bulk of the portfolio is positioned conservatively to survive various price environments, while a smaller "risk basket" is allocated toward scenarios where the strait remains closed indefinitely.

The complication, according to Young, is that even a sudden reopening won't immediately crash oil prices back to pre-crisis levels. The bottleneck created by 2.5 months of closure will take weeks or months to clear. More significantly, some production across the Persian Gulf may never fully restart—certain fields require massive capital investment and extensive service operations to bring back online.

## Why Oil Prices Haven't Hit $200—Yet

Despite the strait's prolonged closure, Brent crude has hovered around $118, and WTI has remained stubbornly above $100. This raises a natural question: why hasn't the crisis pushed prices to the $200 level Young forecast months ago?

### The Storage Buffer

Young identifies two primary factors suppressing prices. First, global oil inventories remain substantial. Each day the strait stays closed, approximately 10 million barrels exit storage, and there's a high correlation between falling inventories and rising prices. Eventually, the math becomes inevitable.

"There's sort of this combination of suppression through narration and through sort of news control," Young explained, "as well as just it taking some time for inventories to fall to send prices higher."

### Volatility and Speculation Suppression

The second factor is more nuanced. Fake ceasefires, premature peace proposals, and erratic headline risk have created extreme oil price volatility. This volatility has crippled speculative activity, particularly from commodity trading advisors (CTAs) and hedge funds that trade oil futures.

"These speculators' budgets aren't actually in dollars like you or I might invest in a stock," Young noted. "Their budgets are in value at risk. So, by increasing the volatility dramatically through these sort of fake news statements and one-step-forward, two-step-back headlines, what we've seen is a suppression of speculation and investment in oil futures."

The result: price suppression through narrative management. But this effect is wearing off. Each subsequent "peace breakthrough" headline has diminishing impact, and as volatility normalizes, speculation will return—likely pushing prices significantly higher.

## The Counterintuitive Wage Growth Thesis

Perhaps Young's most provocative claim is that higher oil prices may actually boost real wages, especially for lower-income workers. This isn't idle speculation—it rests on empirical observation and a crucial structural change in the American economy.

### The Bernanke Connection

Young's thesis draws on research by former Federal Reserve Chair Ben Bernanke, who observed a strong correlation between higher oil prices and a booming U.S. economy. The relationship isn't coincidental; it's rooted in macroeconomic mechanics.

But there's a critical update since Bernanke's original research: the United States has transformed from a net oil importer to a net oil exporter. This shift, which occurred around 2021-2022, fundamentally changes the calculus.

"You can do the very simple Econ 101 math," Young explained. "If you're a net seller of a thing instead of a net buyer of a thing, as you sell it for a higher price, you do better."

### The K-Shaped Consumption Pattern

Data showing that lower-income consumers have cut gas spending more sharply than high-income households might seem to contradict this thesis. But Young sees this as a transient shock phenomenon, not the long-term pattern.

"Certainly in a shock, poorer people do worse. They have less savings and less ability to absorb it," Young acknowledged. "But those people need to go work at Walmart or need to go work at Kroger. Those places need their workers to show up. And so, they start paying them more."

The historical record supports this pattern. In past oil bull markets, rising gasoline prices have consistently preceded wage increases—particularly for lower-income workers. The K-shaped distortion visible in recent data reflects the current technology-heavy economy. As the balance shifts back toward industrial and energy production, that bottom leg of the K should rise.

"This is the precursor to rising real wages. This is not materially concerning to me," Young said. "In fact, I think it's actually quite the opposite. This is what you're looking for from a U.S. perspective."

### The Inflation Nuance

The current 3.8% headline CPI versus 2.8% core inflation reflects exactly this dynamic—energy prices pulling up the headline while core prices remain relatively contained. Young sees this as healthy.

"If you look at when the Federal Reserve blinked in 2021, they blinked, they started raising rates dramatically right as wages started to rise," Young noted. "It's the inflation that sort of the top 0.1% hate—especially the weirdly on the political left in the U.S. And so, it's sort of this almost like class warfare thing where we don't let wages on a real basis rise, unfortunately for the bottom 50%."

## Investment Strategy: Small Caps Over Supermajors

Young's portfolio positioning reflects his broader thesis. He's deliberately avoiding large integrated majors like ExxonMobil in favor of smaller producers and oilfield service companies.

### The Valuation Case

Smaller energy companies trade at dramatically lower valuations than their large-cap counterparts. Whether measured by enterprise value to EBITDA, enterprise value to free cash flow, or dollars per barrel of daily production, the discount is substantial.

But there's a strategic reason beyond mere valuation. Major multinationals have significant exposure to production in the Persian Gulf region. ExxonMobil, for example, has approximately 6% of its volume shut in due to Qatar's inability to export LNG through the affected waters.

"If 100% of your production is in Colorado or Texas or New Mexico, you're not being forced to shut in because Iran and the U.S. are blocking the Strait of Hormuz," Young explained. "You're able to sell all your oil at this high price."

### The Services Opportunity

Perhaps even more compelling is Young's preference for oilfield services—particularly onshore drilling and related services in the United States and Canada. This sector is entering a cyclical boom independent of the Strait of Hormuz situation.

"We're seeing the start of a boom, which may not be affected by the straight reopening," Young said. "It's sort of more of a cyclical thing. And those services stocks are very cheap, and there's a lot of upside."

This positioning offers a hedge: if the strait reopens and oil prices fall, U.S. service companies will still benefit from domestic drilling activity. If the strait remains closed, these same companies will benefit from heightened demand for American production capacity.

## OPEC+ and the Long-Term Energy Landscape

Despite recent headlines about the UAE leaving OPEC+, Young views the cartel as fundamentally strong. The UAE's departure, he argues, was driven by specific circumstances—tourism collapse, dollar swap concerns, and security considerations—that don't reflect a broader defection trend.

"When you look at the language on it and the signaling around it, it just doesn't read like something they're doing voluntarily," Young observed. "It reads like something they were sort of forced to do as a quid pro quo."

Angola's earlier exit offers a cautionary tale: the country promised to dramatically increase production upon leaving but has barely grown output. Young expects the UAE to remain functionally aligned with OPEC+ guidelines despite its nominal departure.

### 2030 Energy Outlook

On longer-term energy demand, Young is bullish on oil's dominance persisting. Prediction markets currently assign a 57% probability that oil remains the world's largest primary energy source in 2030—reflecting the immense difficulty of substituting liquid fuels at scale.

"Long term, I think we need to develop alternatives," Young acknowledged. "It's just a question of there being overwhelming energy demand relative to relatively constrained oil and natural gas supply."

The driving force isn't just Western consumption but the desperate need to raise living standards across India, China, and emerging markets. Achieving even a middle-class lifestyle for billions of additional people requires far more energy consumption than currently projected—making oil price increases likely to ration demand rather than eliminate it.

## The Price Forecast: $150 or Higher Before Year End

Young's price outlook is unambiguous: $150 oil is far more likely than $60, which he doesn't expect to see again until the next severe recession.

"$150 is in the cards," Young stated. "Frankly, in between now and year end, we could see oil at 200, 250. Every month that the strait remains closed, the odds of that increase dramatically."

### The Non-Linearity Problem

This isn't a linear progression. As inventories deplete toward critical levels, the market transitions from normal supply-demand dynamics to price rationing. This is the mechanism that created the gas lines of the 1970s.

"You get closer and closer to tank bottoms," Young explained. "And the reason that there's that non-linearity is as you get to tank bottoms, if you don't actually have gasoline at the gas station, you stop worrying as much about getting your gas for $5 a gallon instead of $4.90 or $5.10 from the gas station around the street, and you start worrying about getting gas so you can get to work."

Current U.S. inventories have fallen from approximately 1.6 billion barrels to 1.5 billion, then 1.4 billion. As this trajectory continues, the probability of reaching that price rationing threshold—and the associated $200-$250 oil prices—increases non-linearly.

The Strategic Petroleum Reserve is being released, providing temporary relief, but this is a finite buffer. The moment those reserves deplete and commercial inventories approach critical lows, market dynamics could shift violently.

### A Potential Bright Spot for Drivers

One counterintuitive element: gasoline prices may actually fall relative to oil prices even as crude continues rising. The Strait of Hormuz closure has disrupted approximately 10 million barrels per day of oil exports but only about 3 million barrels per day of refining capacity.

"We're going to see a flip, in my view, from ultra-high refining margins to lower margins and higher oil prices from lower oil prices," Young predicted. "You're going to see gasoline prices, at least relative to oil, fall materially."

## Key Takeaways

- **The US is now a net oil exporter**, fundamentally changing how higher prices affect the domestic economy. Unlike the 1970s, American producers now benefit from elevated oil prices, creating positive feedback loops for employment and investment.

- **Wage growth typically follows oil price increases**, particularly for lower-income workers. As energy prices rise, businesses dependent on these workers must pay more to maintain staffing—eventually raising the floor of the labor market.

- **The Strait of Hormuz situation benefits China** more than the US or allied nations. Chinese-owned tankers have preferential access to oil flows, and Beijing has strategic incentive to maintain regional instability.

- **Small-cap oil producers and oilfield services companies** offer superior risk-reward profiles compared to large integrated majors, which face production shut-ins in the Persian Gulf and trade at premium valuations.

- **Oil inventories are depleting non-linearly**, with each passing month increasing the probability of reaching price rationing thresholds where $200-$250 oil becomes reality. The current suppression through volatility and headline management is temporary.

- **Global energy demand will likely overwhelm supply** through 2030 and beyond, making oil the dominant energy source for the foreseeable future despite pressure for alternatives.

The energy paradigm is shifting. For investors and workers alike, understanding these dynamics may prove more valuable than following conventional wisdom about oil prices and economic growth.
