The numbers are staggering. The four largest companies on Earth—Microsoft, Google, Meta, and Amazon—are collectively committing $700 billion or more to artificial intelligence infrastructure in 2026, a 77% year-over-year surge that has quietly become the single largest stimulus running through the American economy. Yet as the hyperscalers pour hundreds of billions into GPU clusters, data centers, and nuclear power stations, they are simultaneously firing tens of thousands of knowledge workers while the very software companies that should be benefiting from this buildout are bleeding. Adobe is down 32% year-to-date. ServiceNow has shed 40%. Snowflake has cratered 44%. And OpenAI's CFO has privately warned colleagues the company may not be able to pay its compute bills.

This is the central paradox of Wall Street's most crowded trade: AI infrastructure spending is either the engine keeping the economy out of recession, or the most sophisticated Ponzi scheme in corporate history. The answer will determine whether 2025 marks the beginning of a new productivity boom—or the top of a generational market bubble.

## The $700 Billion Stimulus Engine No One Wants to Talk About

On the surface, markets are roaring. The NASDAQ cracked 25,000 for the first time last week, the Dow continues to flirt with 50,000, and the S&P 500 sits at or near record territory. But look beneath the indices and the picture grows murky. The iShares Expanded Tech Software ETF (IGV)—which tracks the infrastructure layer supposedly most exposed to AI adoption—is down roughly 16% year-to-date. This divergence between headline indices and the underlying software economy has left Wall Street scrambling for explanations.

Gareth Soloway, chief market strategist at Verified Investing, argues that AI capital expenditure has become the economy's invisible life support. "As long as these mega-caps are willing to spend $100 to $200 billion every year, the massive stimulus engine is going to run," Soloway told the David Lynn Report. "It really does filter through a large portion of the economy. Now, there's other parts of the economy that many would argue are already in a recession, but at least on the baseline of what will actually be classified as a recession, at most it would be at the very end of the year. But I would say you push it out more towards 2027."

Even Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell acknowledged at a recent FOMC meeting that "data centers and AI capex have been major drivers of the economy so far." The central bank itself is validating the thesis that AI infrastructure spending has become the primary economic engine replacing traditional monetary and fiscal stimulus.

The critical question, Soloway suggests, is what happens when the music stops: "The real kicker is going to be when do these institutions, these big players like Meta and Amazon, Google, and Microsoft, when do they start pulling back on their spend? That's going to be when the recession hits. Once they start, let's say they go from $200 billion each or $150 billion each even to $100, that's a massive amount of sucking out of the economy of money."

## The Casualties: Why Software Stocks Are Getting Crushed

If $700 billion in AI infrastructure spending is stimulating the economy, you'd expect software companies—the businesses selling the applications that run on all those GPUs—to be the primary beneficiaries. They are not. They are, as Jay Singh, founder of Special Situations Investing, puts it, "the casualties."

Singh walked through the IGV holdings name by name, and the numbers are uglier than most retail investors realize:

- **SAP**: Down 40% year-to-date
- **Shopify**: Down 20% year-to-date
- **Salesforce**: Down 35% year-to-date
- **Snowflake**: Down 44% year-to-date
- **Adobe**: Down 32% year-to-date
- **ServiceNow**: Down 45% year-to-date (even after announcing a $25 billion share buyback plan)

"We're talking about the best companies in the space," Singh said. "If you look at a handful of these names, it is quite concerning that these are some of the best companies in the United States and Europe. What this means is that the fear is real."

The fact that Adobe announced a massive $25 billion share buyback program yet the stock continued to fall 32% suggests something deeper than temporary market dislocation. It suggests institutional investors are pricing in permanent impairment to these companies' competitive positions—worrying that AI itself may be disintermediating the traditional software layer entirely.

## The Productivity Paradox: Where Are the Gains?

Professor Steve Hanke of Johns Hopkins University has been one of the most consistent and vocal skeptics of the AI investment narrative, and he points to data that Wall Street prefers to ignore: US productivity actually declined last year despite all the AI hype.

"Productivity in the United States was 3% per annum in 2024. In 2025 it was 2.1%," Hanke noted. "Last year it actually went down. So all of this hype around AI and people adopting it and increasing productivity and so forth, if you aggregate and look at the economy as a whole, it's not comporting with what we're getting out of Silicon Valley hype—or what I call 'Silly Valley' hype."

Hanke's reading of the situation is straightforward: the only way to justify current valuations for AI-exposed software stocks is a massive increase in free cash flow that the productivity data simply does not support. His recommendation? Short the leaders. "Those would be prime shorts. They're primed to short because there's just no way you're going to get that kind of improvement in valuation just because of adoption of AI. It's just not going to happen because the only way it could happen is to have a massive increase in free cash flow to justify that kind of increase in present value or stock price."

## Agentic AI: The One Place Productivity Gains Are Real

Not everyone sees a productivity collapse. Alexandra Shagalinska, an AI researcher at Harvard Law School, points to one sector where productivity improvements are not modest—they're spectacular. And it's the same sector that's driving the layoffs.

"For the past four months, we've seen the rapid growth in the so-called agentic AI," Shagalinska explained. "And that is much more than just simple ChatGPT or our usual experience with Anthropic Claude or any other model. It's actually a new development in artificial intelligence that allows creation of agents that are going to fulfill certain tasks end-to-end. They can make payments, they can run different loops, they can query databases, come up with results, send emails, write reports. So they can do many different things that people have been doing."

The agentic AI revolution is particularly visible in software development, where Shagalinska describes a new organizational structure: "They have their own hierarchies. So you have an agent that is an orchestrator, and that orchestrator is orchestrating the work of other smaller agents that are doing other things, reporting back. It resembles a big organization or even a society. So one coder can now use those tribes of different agents to perform multiple tasks."

In IT, Shagalinska argues, the productivity spike is "incredible" and "spectacular" because of those agents. The implication is stark: "You can clearly see that you can have four or five IT specialists who are doing the same job as 50 people have been doing before, and therefore that remaining 45 people are just not needed anymore. You don't need them."

## The Architects and the Unemployed

Michael Gedad, founder of The League Lag Report and someone who has built 70 different agentic AI automations over the past 70 weeks, has gone "all-in" on a prediction that the world will soon be divided into two camps: "The architects and the unemployed."

"The people that can actually figure out how to use AI and go all in—I think similar to what I'm doing—and everybody else that's basically going to be replaced by that output," Gedad said. "If that's the case, and I think it happens sooner than people realize or sooner than people expect, then yeah, you're going to see some nasty volatility in equities."

Gedad's thesis is not that AI will augment workers—it will replace them entirely. The thousands of layoffs at Meta, Microsoft, Coinbase, and others are not a temporary recalibration. They are a preview of a labor market transformation that will make the industrial revolution's disruption of agriculture look gradual by comparison.

## The Deception: Why Layoffs Aren't Really About AI

Clem Chambers, founder of financial news site ADVFN, offers a more cynical interpretation of the layoff wave. These companies, he argues, are using AI as cover for a much older problem: they overhired during the zero-interest-rate era when capital was free and headcount was a prestige metric.

"Look, those companies were hiring people for non-existent jobs, and they were doing it for decades," Chambers said. "And now rather than hire a load of people that do nothing because it looks good on a spreadsheet, they're having to build out things that cost real money. So they're sacking their people that are doing nothing, working from home, playing computer games all day."

But it's what Chambers heard inside Silicon Valley that gave him chills: "In the HR department, they call them stock." Employees, in the corporate lexicon of tech giants, are inventory to be managed, adjusted, and disposed of when balance sheet priorities change. "They hire people to keep them from working for the competition, because they got all the capital in the world, and now they got to build up big sheds full of vanidas and buy nuclear power stations. All of a sudden they can't afford stock. They got to turn that into cash, and they do that by getting rid of it."

This framing suggests the current layoffs are a balance sheet correction dressed up as an AI revolution—and that the narrative being sold to investors may be more spin than substance.

## The Balance Sheet Transformation Nobody Noticed

Ted Oakley, founder of Oxbow Advisors and a 40-year veteran of energy and credit cycles, points to a structural change that should concern anyone betting on the AI capex trade: the Magnificent 7 used to have the cleanest balance sheets in the stock market. They were cash machines generating mountains of free cash flow. That is no longer true.

"These companies didn't have to have a tremendous amount of bricks and mortar. I mean, it was easy money," Oakley observed. "But they spent it all, and they now put it all back into data centers and AI. Whether it pays off or not, I don't know. But I do know this: they've changed the structure of their balance sheets from what was healthy to what I think is unhealthy."

Microsoft, Oakley notes, now trades at 14-15 times sales. These are extraordinarily expensive companies that have converted pristine balance sheets into leverage, betting the future returns from AI infrastructure will exceed the cost of capital. That's a bet that requires both AI adoption to materialize at scale and the companies to successfully monetize their investments—neither of which is guaranteed.

## The Technical Picture: Generational Top or Continuation?

Not everyone is convinced the end is near. Christopher Mulan of TheTechnicalTraders.com watches price, momentum, money flows, and sentiment—and what he sees is not a bubble warning but rather underlying strength that should be respected.

"We like the markets here. I really do like the equities market still," Mulan said 12 hours after Meta's capex bombshell dropped the stock 8%. "Looking at all the different asset classes, equities is the only one that's actually standing out. Hence the reason we are long it."

Mulan's team remains long the S&P 500 and NASDAQ through QQQ, approaching a 10% gain target. His framework is simple: "Buy the rumor, sell the news." Markets have been running ahead of fundamentals on the expectation of good earnings, and when those earnings arrive, the smart money takes profits. That doesn't mean the trend is broken—it means the market is healthy and volatile.

"I think you need to be long equities," Mulan concluded. Even with all the uncertainty, equities remain the best asset class relative to alternatives.

## The $700 Billion Question

So where does this leave investors? The bull case is real: Alphabet ripped 7% last week on 63% cloud growth. Apple hit a record on iPhone strength and a $100 billion buyback. These are genuine businesses with real earnings power benefiting from the AI buildout.

But the bear case is equally compelling: most of that $700 billion in GPU spending is being sold to one money-losing startup—OpenAI—whose CFO reportedly told colleagues she is worried about paying the compute bills. The companies funding the buildout are firing tens of thousands of workers to finance it. And US productivity is declining despite three years of AI hype.

How long can the US economy carry a $700 billion concentrated bet on a handful of AI customers, financed by corporate debt and white-collar layoffs, while productivity refuses to show up in the data?

**The experts are split:**

- **Gareth Soloway** believes AI capex has replaced Fed stimulus as the economy's engine—and that the recession will arrive when the spending stops
- **Steve Hanke** sees a productivity collapse beneath the hype and recommends shorting the leading AI stocks
- **Alexandra Shagalinska** confirms the productivity gains are real, concentrated in agentic AI deployed by software developers
- **Michael Gedad** predicts a division between "architects" who master AI and "the unemployed" who don't
- **Clem Chambers** argues the layoffs are a balance sheet correction, not an AI revolution
- **Ted Oakley** warns that clean balance sheets have been converted into leverage
- **Christopher Mulan** tells traders to respect the trend and stay long equities

The market has priced in a future where AI transforms productivity across the entire economy. The data, however, shows a future where AI transforms productivity for a small number of developers while everyone else gets laid off. Whether that concentrated productivity gain justifies $700 billion in infrastructure spending—and the valuations built on top of it—is the trillion-dollar question investors will have to answer in 2025 and beyond.
