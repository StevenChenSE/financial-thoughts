## The Crash of the KOSPI and the Rise of Retail Leverage

Just three weeks ago, South Korea boasted the best-performing stock market in the world. The KOSPI had surged nearly 200% in the last 12 months, dwarfing the US S&P 500’s 24% gain. At the peak, tech giants Samsung and SK Hynix saw their stock prices rise over 500% and 1,000%, respectively. However, this rally was built on a fragile foundation: extreme concentration and massive retail leverage.

Unlike the diversified US market, where the top 10 companies make up 36% of the S&P 500, Samsung and SK Hynix alone represent over 56% of the KOSPI. This concentration was fueled by 14 million retail investors, who dubbed themselves "ants." Facing unaffordable housing and cultural pressure to build wealth, these investors borrowed heavily to buy stocks, often using leveraged ETFs that amplify market movements. When the market turned, this leverage triggered a catastrophic collapse.

## Black Tuesday: The Mechanics of the Collapse

The bubble burst in late June when US chip stocks began to sell off ahead of earnings from Micron, a key competitor to Samsung and SK Hynix. This triggered a domino effect in Korea, culminating in "Black Tuesday," where the KOSPI dropped more than 10% in a single day—one of the worst declines in Korean history.

Because the market was driven by borrowed money, the drop created a "doom loop." As stock prices fell, brokers issued margin calls to investors who couldn't deposit more cash. Consequently, over 1.2 million accounts faced forced liquidations, leading to automatic selling that drove prices down further. Leveraged ETFs exacerbated the issue by rebalancing daily, forcing sales into a falling market. In just 21 days, the KOSPI dropped 25%, erasing over $600 billion from Asian markets and prompting emergency intervention by the South Korean president.

## Record Margin Debt in the United States

The collapse in South Korea serves as a stark preview for potential risks in the US. As of June 2026, US margin debt hit roughly 4.5% of GDP, the highest level in American history. This figure surpasses debt levels seen before the dot-com bubble, the 2008 financial crisis, and the 2021 meme stock rally.

This official statistic likely underestimates the true level of leverage, as it excludes modern instruments like leveraged ETFs, options, and private credit. The US market has increasingly resemble a casino, with record volumes in zero-day options and leveraged funds on single stocks like Nvidia and Tesla. Historically, every time US margin debt reached extreme levels, a significant market correction followed shortly after.

## The AI Spending Loop and Diminishing Returns

The current US market rally is heavily supported by AI infrastructure spending from hyperscalers like Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta. These companies are projected to spend $725 billion on AI in 2026, driving revenues for chipmakers like Nvidia and memory providers like Micron. However, this spending creates a circular dependency where one company's expenditure becomes another's earnings.

Jim Chanos, a well-known short seller, warns that the returns on this spending are diminishing. Hyperscalers were generating 40 cents of operating income on every dollar invested a year and a half ago; today, that figure has dropped to 20 cents and is trending toward 10 cents. Unprofitable AI companies like OpenAI and Anthropic drive compute demand, justifying hyperscaler spending, which in turn props up chip earnings and the broader stock market.

## Warning Signs: IPOs and CapEx Cuts

A critical warning sign for the US market is the surge in Initial Public Offerings (IPOs). In 2026, IPOs have reached over 12% of GDP in post-IPO market cap, more than double the peak of the dot-com bubble in 1999. Historically, such high IPO activity indicates that insiders believe stock prices have peaked and are selling to retail investors.

The potential trigger for a US market correction could be a cut in capital expenditures (CapEx) by major hyperscalers. If a company like Microsoft announces reduced AI spending, it could cause a collapse in supplier earnings, similar to the Cisco crash in 2001 when Coca-Cola reduced router orders. Jim Chanos predicts this realization could occur in late 2026 or 2027, when companies begin questioning the ROI of trillion-dollar AI investments.

## Conclusion: Two Scenarios Ahead

There are two likely scenarios for the future. In the first, the Korean crash remains contained, memory prices stabilize, and hyperscalers continue spending through 2027, allowing the KOSPI to recover. In the second, and more dangerous scenario, Korea was the first domino. A cut in CapEx by a major US tech firm could trigger a leveraged unwind in the US market, leading to margin calls, forced selling, and potential government intervention.

While AI may ultimately transform the economy, investors are currently paying future prices for present promises. The South Korean crash highlights the dangers of excessive leverage and concentration. As margin debt reaches historic highs, investors should exercise caution, recognizing that the market's confidence may be as fragile as the "unsinkable" Titanic after hitting the iceberg.
