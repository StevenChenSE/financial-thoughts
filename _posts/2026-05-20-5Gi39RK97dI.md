The convergence of rising silver prices, climbing oil costs, and surging interest rates paints a troubling picture for American consumers—and according to legendary economist Peter Schiff, this worst-of-all-worlds scenario is just the beginning. Speaking on Capital Controls on May 11, 2026, Schiff delivered a stark assessment: as the Iran conflict drags on, energy prices will remain elevated, the dollar will weaken, and precious metals will continue their march higher while traditional markets face mounting pressure.

"The markets are moving into gold and silver as a hedge against higher inflation," Schiff explained. "I think investors are just starting to accept that higher oil prices are here to stay—that they're not going down."

The analysis touched on everything from midterm election implications to copper's record-setting rally, China emerging as an unexpected geopolitical winner, and why mining stocks may represent the most compelling opportunity in this new market environment.

## Silver Breaks Out as Metals and Energy Move in Tandem

Silver captured headlines by surging to $86.58—knocking on the door of $87—representing a 2-month high with a remarkable 7% single-day gain. What makes this move particularly significant is its correlation with oil prices, which also jumped 3% in WTI crude on the same day.

"For the last two months or so of the Iran war, metals and energy have been inversely correlated with one another," Schiff noted. "One moves up, the other moves down, and vice versa. But that's because everything was inversely correlated to oil. Oil went up, pretty much everything went down."

That dynamic has now shifted. Bond yields are climbing toward highs, gold and silver are rising alongside oil prices, and the stock market continues making all-time highs even as energy costs increase. This unusual simultaneous movement suggests investors are increasingly pricing in persistent inflation rather than treating energy spikes as temporary disruptions.

### The Parabolic Run and Sustainable Recovery

Silver's recent moves trace a dramatic pattern. The metal exploded from below $70 to reach $125—a near-tripling in price—before pulling back. Financial media declared the bull market over when that correction occurred.

"The media looked at that big pullback to declare the whole bull market over," Schiff said. "A lot of people might have been scared out of the market when what they should have been doing is buying the hell out of that dip."

Schiff believes $125 will eventually become the support level for silver, similar to how $50 transitioned from resistance to foundation. Gold, meanwhile, pulled back to around $4,100 before recovering toward $4,800—still closer to its highs than silver.

His targets remain ambitious: silver finishing the year above $100, potentially reaching $150 in a straight-up run, while gold targets above $6,000 by year-end. Though gold has traditionally led precious metals rallies, Schiff sees silver as potentially taking the lead this time around.

"Silver could have a run and go straight to 150," he said. "Who the hell knows? If you're bullish on the AI build-out, just buy silver. I think it's a much safer way to play it."

## Copper Sets Records While Inflation Remains Hidden

Copper, often called "Dr. Copper" for its role as an economic health indicator, traded at all-time record highs as of the interview date. The red metal's performance tells a compelling story about underlying inflationary pressures:

- **Year-to-date:** Up 13%
- **One-year return:** Up 38%
- **Three-year return:** Up 73%
- **Ten-year return:** Up 212%

A rising copper price typically signals economic strength, but Schiff interprets the current surge differently. "That means normally a rising copper price is supposed to show the economy is in good shape," he observed. "I don't think that's what it's showing here. I just think it's evidencing a lot of inflation."

The worldwide demand for copper—particularly for infrastructure, renewable energy, and technology applications—transcends U.S. borders, making this price action a global inflation indicator rather than a domestic economic health measure.

## Dollar Weakness and Commodity Implications

The U.S. Dollar Index has surrendered all gains made when the Iran conflict began, falling back to pre-war levels. The implications are significant:

- Dollar down 1% this month
- Dollar down 1.6% over six months
- Dollar trending downward over the past year

"We're at about 10 to 15% of the world's energy supply locked up through the Strait of Hormuz," Schiff noted. "The dollar has lost 100% of its Iran war gains."

If the dollar continues weakening—Schiff believes a breakdown is already inching closer—it will put upward pressure on commodities across the board. This relationship creates a self-reinforcing dynamic: dollar weakness drives commodity prices higher, which in turn pressures the dollar further, while simultaneously eroding American purchasing power.

## Interest Rates: The Coming Breakout

The 10-year Treasury yield tells a concerning story across multiple timeframes. Looking at the chart presented during the interview, yields have risen across nearly all periods except the past year. Schiff believes the current multi-year consolidation pattern will eventually break decisively upward.

"Wait until we break out of this multi-year continuation pattern," he warned. "You have that big rise that preceded it, and then we have another big move up where yields rise up to maybe 7% range. That is going to be a big game-changer because it's going to put tremendous pressure on the economy, on U.S. government finances as the cost of servicing our massive debt rises precipitously."

The historical analog most correlated to current market action is 2022—the year Russia invaded Ukraine. Both were midterm election years, and both featured bond yields spiking as the conflict unfolded. That pattern suggests voters may face significantly higher mortgage rates and borrowing costs heading into November.

## The Gas Tax Suspension: A Policy Backfire

The Trump administration's proposal to suspend the federal gas tax represents exactly the wrong response, according to Schiff.

"That's just going to put more pressure upwards on oil prices because it increases demand when you reduce the price somewhat," he explained. "But also it increases budget deficits, which are already way too high, because if the government loses that tax revenue, that means it just has to borrow that money, which puts more upward pressure on interest rates and ultimately downward pressure on the dollar—which puts upward pressure on oil prices."

The entire policy, Schiff argued, will backfire and end up making oil prices even higher than they would have been had the gas tax remained in place.

## Oil Prices: Higher for Longer

Regardless of how the Iran conflict resolves, Schiff expects oil prices to remain elevated through year's end. Several factors support this view:

**Supply-side constraints:**
- Two months or more of backlogged supply through the Strait of Hormuz
- Even if the strait reopens, restarting production takes significant time
- Uncertainty premium will persist as long as conflict continues

**Demand-side pressures:**
- Countries will likely stockpile oil to build reserves against future disruptions
- Post-conflict demand surge as economies rush to rebuild inventories

"The markets are pricing in a big drop," Schiff observed. "Trump is out there saying that as soon as this is over, oil is going to drop like a rock. A, I don't know how soon this is going to be over. But B, even if it is over, I don't expect that kind of collapse in oil prices. How could it?"

If the war escalates, $150 per barrel becomes a realistic possibility—a 50% increase from current levels.

## Geopolitical Missteps and China's Rise

Schiff offered a blunt assessment of the Iran campaign's strategic outcomes.

"We've changed nothing about the regime that's in power," he stated. "They are still in power. They are every bit as hostile to America as they were before, if not more so. The current crop of Iranian leaders may be even more hardline than the ones that we killed."

The Islamic Republic's economic and regional influence has arguably strengthened despite military losses. Meanwhile, the U.S. position in the Middle East has been compromised.

To the extent Iran was pursuing nuclear capability, Schiff suggested the conflict may have accelerated rather than delayed that timeline. "If they do develop a nuclear weapon, they now have more of a reason to use it—assuming they could get it to one of our bases or something—because they're going to feel that we've been attacked and we need to defend ourselves."

Most troubling from a geopolitical perspective, China emerges as a major beneficiary of American overextension.

"China is one of the winners of that war," Schiff declared. "They're not fighting it, but their global reputation is being enhanced. They're not launching missiles and blowing up bombs—they're doing that. So now we're going to have to spend a lot of money to replace what we no longer have, whereas the Chinese will have everything. They haven't used any of their bombs or missiles."

Beyond the military dimension, China gained intelligence on American capabilities while preserving its own arsenal intact. Countries worldwide now have additional reasons to seek partnerships with Beijing rather than Washington.

"There's more reasons for countries to want to work with China because they can't trust the United States," Schiff said. "Our prestige is on the line. If we can't beat Iran, if we can't get the regime change that we said was our goal, that makes us look weak—and that exposes us as more of a paper tiger."

## Midterm Elections and the Democratic Resurgence

Schiff's political forecast carries significant implications for market participants. Rising interest rates and oil prices will create a political liability for Republicans heading into November.

"Republicans own that," Schiff said flatly. "Voters are going to be very upset because when they voted Republican in 2024, they voted for lower prices, not for higher prices. And they voted to end wars, not to start them."

He predicts a potential electoral wave: "I think the voters are going to feel betrayed and they're going to want to throw out the betrayers. I think they're going to get a lot of votes by people who are very frustrated having been sold out by Trump and the Republicans. I think it could be one of the biggest losses in any midterm."

Schiff expects Democrats to potentially capture both chambers of Congress—not just the House but the Senate as well. While a split government might limit immediate legislative impact (Trump has historically supported spending increases regardless of political persuasion), the real significance lies in forecasting 2028.

"When the Democrats also regain the White House and then they have the trifecta—Congress and the White House—then it can get really bad," Schiff warned.

## Mining Stocks: The Smart Money Play

Beyond the metals themselves, Schiff sees mining stocks as a compelling opportunity. The SILJ ETF (Silver Miners ETF) and GDX (Gold Miners ETF) have shown signs of bottoming after pulling back from their initial breakouts.

"Smart money investors tend to flow into the riskier at the front and run the metal by positioning themselves in mining stocks," Schiff explained. "We've seen it at countless times before where the mining stocks got ahead of the metal and then the metal ran right after."

The mining sector also benefits from its own momentum. Even after oil spiked from $60 to $100 per barrel—a major input cost for mining operations—mining stocks have held relatively strong.

"The gold companies never really got much of a boost out of the falling oil prices," Schiff noted, criticizing market inefficiency. "Oil is the cheapest it's ever been relative to gold, and this is not being factored into these valuations because this is obviously bringing down costs. So some of that gain has been lost now with the price of oil moving up 50% from where it was. But on a relative basis, they're still killing it—even with $100 oil."

Major producers like Newmont demonstrate extraordinary economics: production costs around $1,000 per ounce while gold trades near $5,000 per ounce. Smaller juniors represent even more explosive potential, particularly as acquisition targets for cash-rich major miners seeking to build their exploration pipelines.
