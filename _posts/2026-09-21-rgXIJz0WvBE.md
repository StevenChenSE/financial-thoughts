Joel Mokyr, a professor of economics and history at Northwestern University and co-winner of the 2025 Nobel Prize in Economics, recently sat down to discuss the engines of economic growth, the trajectory of the United States and China, and the societal implications of artificial intelligence. Alongside Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt, Mokyr was awarded the prize for explaining how innovation drives growth. His work explores a central question in economics: why did humanity experience sustained economic growth only after thousands of years of stagnation?

## The Transience of Technological Leadership

Mokyr begins by examining "Cardwell’s law," an empirical regularity observed by historian Donald Cardwell, which suggests that no society remains technologically creative for very long. The United States, which dethroned Europe as the world’s technological leader for most of the 20th century, is currently facing clouds on the horizon. While the U.S. remains a leader in digital and digital technologies, Mokyr notes a historical pattern: Britain replaced the Netherlands, Europe replaced Britain, and now China is pushing into advanced technologies.

Mokyr argues that competition is healthy and necessary. He rejects the idea that technology is a "horse race" where the first to the finish line wins, a view he attributes to a misunderstanding of economics. Instead, he posits that the U.S. will likely have to share leadership with China, potentially ceding the lead depending on policy choices in both nations.

## The Real Risks of AI: Inequality, Not Extinction

A significant portion of the interview addresses the rapid evolution of AI. Mokyr notes that AI is developing faster than institutions can adapt. Unlike previous technological shifts, which allowed time for political and social adjustment, AI’s pace is creating a gap between capability and regulatory mechanism.

While some researchers, such as those at Anthropic, have warned that AI could pose an existential threat to humanity by 2030, Mokyr dismisses this as "ridiculous." He argues that the true danger of AI is not a rogue robot or a virus, but the sharpening of income distribution. AI could lead to an enormous concentration of wealth and power in the hands of a few, potentially creating "trillionaires." He draws a parallel to the late 19th-century railroad barons, such as Cornelius Vanderbilt and Cornelius Vanderbilt, whose political influence was eventually constrained by the Progressive movement and antitrust policies.

Mokyr advocates for institutional adaptation, including the possibility of wealth taxes or caps on wealth accumulation to prevent the political power of the super-rich from undermining democracy. He argues that while individual innovation is driven by personal incentive, there is a point beyond which wealth becomes unspendable and is instead used to buy political influence, a dynamic that is unhealthy for a democratic society.

## Why Europe and Europe’s Decline

Mokyr explains the "Great Divergence" by highlighting the unique European belief in "progress." During the Renaissance, Europeans rejected the "intellectual ancestor master worship" that characterized other civilizations, including China and Islamic civilization. They decided that the present generation could be smarter than their predecessors, leading to the invention of the patent system to incentivize innovation.

Regarding Europe’s current status, Mokyr notes that while Europe may be losing its position as the primary innovation hub, its quality of life remains high, with life expectancy significantly higher than in the U.S. Europe’s decline relative to the U.S. is attributed to two major disasters: the two World Wars, which devastated European human and physical capital, and the subsequent migration of European scientists and intellectuals to America.

## China’s State-Driven Market Economy

Mokyr challenges the notion that China is a command economy. He describes China as a "non-democratic, unfree market economy," a unique experiment in history. Unlike the Soviet Union, which failed after abandoning its New Economic Policy, China has maintained a market system where entrepreneurs and firms operate, but the state directs massive resources toward research and development.

Driven by a desire to restore the nation’s historical glory and avenge 19th-century humiliations by the West, the Chinese government has aggressively pushed the technological envelope. China now files millions of patents a year and produces a large number of high-impact scientific papers. Mokyr sees this state-driven ambition as a powerful engine for growth that the West may not be able to easily replicate.

## The Future of Cognition and Education

In the final segment, Mokyr addresses the debate over whether millennials and Generation Z are less intelligent than previous generations. He points to evidence that improved nutrition over the last century has led to taller, healthier, and cognitively more capable populations. However, he acknowledges that digital screens may be impeding the depth of learning in some contexts.

Mokyr’s solution is AI itself. He argues that AI’s greatest triumph will be the personalization of education. By allowing educators to tailor instruction to the specific strengths, weaknesses, and interests of each student, AI can revolutionize learning in a way that "one-size-fits-all" education never could.

## Final Verdict on Superpowers

When asked to predict the dominant superpower of the next 30 to 50 years, Mokyr gives a "much greater weight" to China. However, he refuses to count out the United States, arguing that American institutions are strong enough to weather current political turbulence and that the U.S. will likely bounce back to share global leadership. He bets against Russia, viewing it as too corrupt and misgoverned to be a superpower, and suggests that other regions, such as Latin America and Africa, may also pull themselves up by their bootstraps.

Mokyr’s insights underscore a central theme: innovation is the engine of progress, but its benefits and distribution are shaped by culture, institutions, and policy. The future of global power will depend not just on who invents the next breakthrough, but on how societies choose to manage the transformative forces of technology.
