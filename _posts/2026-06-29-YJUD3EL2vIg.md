The strategic shift from military dominance to economic coercion reshaping global power dynamics

---

On a Thursday evening, Iran struck a cargo vessel transiting the Strait of Hormuz with a drone projectile—an act that drew immediate condemnation from President Trump, who called it a "foolish violation" of recent ceasefire agreements. The attack, attributed to Iran's Revolutionary Guard, occurred despite ongoing negotiations aimed at de-escalating tensions between Washington and Tehran. Yet this incident represents far more than a simple ceasefire breach. It reveals a fundamental shift in how nations wield economic leverage, and more critically, how the world's most powerful military has been effectively neutralized by a relatively inexpensive arsenal of drones.

Edward Fishman, author of the 2025 best-selling book *Chokepoints: American Power in the Age of Economic Warfare* and senior fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations, argues that the United States has fundamentally transformed the global economy into a weapon of war—only to discover that other nations have learned to use the same tactics against Washington. The Strait of Hormuz incident exemplifies this new paradigm: a choke point that the U.S. military could once guarantee has become a liability that even the world's most sophisticated armed forces cannot resolve without unacceptable costs.

## The Strait of Hormuz: The World's Most Critical Energy Chokepoint

The Strait of Hormuz represents the single most important maritime passage for global energy markets. Approximately 20 percent of the world's oil flows through this narrow waterway, connecting the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and ultimately the open ocean. Any disruption to shipping through this corridor sends shockwaves through global commodity markets, affecting everything from gasoline prices to industrial manufacturing costs worldwide.

Before the current conflict, roughly 130 vessels transited the Strait of Hormuz daily. The assumption among American military planners was clear: even if Iran threatened to close the strait, the U.S. Navy could quickly restore the flow of commerce through naval escorts and overwhelming force. This assumption, according to Fishman, proved catastrophically wrong.

What's emerged instead is a new form of economic warfare that Fishman describes as "selective closure." Rather than completely blocking the strait—which would be suicidal for Iran's own oil exports—Iran has developed the capability to close the waterway for everyone except itself. They've created what amounts to a tollbooth on one of the world's most vital shipping lanes, and they're collecting revenue from nations desperate to keep their oil flowing to global markets.

## Iran's Strategic Masterstroke: Selective Control

The mechanism behind Iran's control is deceptively simple and devastatingly effective. Within the first week of the conflict, Iranian forces didn't deploy the thousands of sea mines they had stockpiled—a tactic everyone anticipated. Instead, they simply broadcast on radio frequencies used by all vessels in the strait: "The Strait of Hormuz is closed. It's not safe to go through."

Ships that didn't heed this warning faced strikes from anti-ship missiles and drones. Though only about a dozen vessels were hit—seemingly minor in the context of over 130 daily transits—the psychological and commercial impact was immediate and total. Shipping crews refused to risk their lives regardless of insurance coverage. Commercial carriers rerouted or sat idle. The Strait of Hormuz became, effectively, inaccessible to global commerce despite remaining technically open.

Iran established the Persian Gulf Strait Authority (PGSA), an entity with its own Twitter account, requiring vessels to register and pay service fees before passage. The recent Singaporean-flagged tanker that was struck had apparently bypassed this protocol and paid the price. International law may not support Iran's claims, but as Fishman observes, "If they're willing to go there, there's not much that the world can do about it."

This capability—controlling who passes and who doesn't—is fundamentally different from a complete closure. Iran can now allow Chinese vessels through while blocking Israeli, American, or Saudi ships. They can monetize passage selectively, creating an entirely new revenue stream and geopolitical lever that costs relatively little to maintain.

## The American Miscalculation

Fishman identifies a critical analytical error made not just by the Trump administration but by military strategists and intelligence analysts across the Western world. Everyone recognized the Strait of Hormuz as essential and understood Iran possessed thousands of sea mines capable of closing it. The assumption was that Iran wouldn't deploy those mines because doing so would be "economically suicidal"—a complete closure would prevent Iran's own oil exports from reaching market.

What analysts failed to foresee was that Iran never needed to deploy sea mines. The selective approach—keeping the strait open for themselves while threatening everyone else—achieves Iran's strategic objectives without self-inflicted economic damage. Iran has essentially weaponized one of the world's most critical chokepoints while maintaining their own ability to export oil and generate revenue.

The implications for American power are profound. The traditional response to threats against international shipping lanes has been naval presence and military deterrence. Yet deploying warships through the Strait of Hormuz now means placing "exquisite, expensive naval warships at risk of Iranian drones and missiles," as Fishman explains. The asymmetry is stark: Iranian drones costing $20,000 to $30,000 each can be manufactured in facilities the size of an office, while the American warships they threaten represent billions of dollars of investment and carry hundreds of sailors.

Eliminating Iran's drone capability would require not merely striking launch sites along the coastline. With drone ranges exceeding 150 kilometers, a meaningful buffer zone would necessitate occupying a substantial portion of coastal Iran—potentially requiring hundreds of thousands of American ground troops. Fishman compares the prospect to "a 2003 style Iraq war," noting there is "literally no political support" in the United States for such an operation.

## The New Architecture of Economic Coercion

The framework for understanding this new era of economic warfare extends well beyond the Strait of Hormuz. Fishman's research identifies multiple critical chokepoints through which the United States has projected power: financial systems centered on dollar clearing and the SWIFT messaging network, technology supply chains involving advanced semiconductors and chip-making equipment, energy infrastructure, maritime shipping routes, and critical mineral supplies.

The 2015 Iran nuclear deal illustrated this dynamic in reverse. The United States and allied powers weaponized America's financial chokepoint—primarily the dollar system—to impose severe economic pressure on Iran, successfully compelling nuclear concessions. The current situation represents a mirror image: Iran weaponizing a physical chokepoint to impose economic pressure on the United States, compelling the very economic relief that has defined the recent ceasefire agreement.

Trump himself inadvertently confirmed this dynamic when he stated at the G7 summit that his Iran negotiations had "avoided a global depression." Fishman interprets this as a revealing admission: the ceasefire agreement is fundamentally about paying Iran to keep the Strait of Hormuz open, not about preventing nuclear proliferation. The Memorandum of Understanding represents the Trump administration swallowing what Fishman describes as "quite a humiliating deal," providing Iran with "front-loaded economic relief in exchange for no nuclear concessions whatsoever."

## Market Implications and the Future of Shipping

The market's reaction to recent events suggests investors are pricing in a new reality. Despite the tanker strike and ongoing tensions, oil prices (WTI remaining above $70 per barrel) have not spiked dramatically, and markets appear to expect relative stability rather than significant escalation. This calm reflects a broader recognition that neither Washington nor Tehran has appetite for returning to active hostilities.

Yet the Strait of Hormuz will not return to its pre-war status quo. Pre-conflict traffic of approximately 130 daily vessel crossings has already fallen to roughly 70. Fishman suggests a recovery to 100 or 110 crossings is possible, but a complete return to previous levels seems unlikely. More significantly, Iran will continue monetizing the strait through various mechanisms: service fees, environmental fees, and proprietary insurance schemes that shippers must purchase to transit safely.

For regional oil producers like Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates, the calculus is straightforward. Even if payments to Iran for strait passage total billions of dollars annually, this represents a small fraction of the value delivered by ensuring their tankers can reach global markets. As Fishman observes, paying Iran for safe passage is "well worth it" compared to the alternative.

## Summary

The attack on vessels in the Strait of Hormuz represents not an aberration but a preview of the emerging architecture of global power. Economic chokepoints—whether financial systems, technology supply chains, or critical maritime passages—have become the primary domains through which nations project and contest power. The United States pioneered this approach but now faces rivals who have learned to exploit the same vulnerabilities.

Iran's victory in securing effective control over the Strait of Hormuz demonstrates that expensive military capabilities can be neutralized by inexpensive alternatives when deployed intelligently. The ceasefire agreement effectively acknowledges this new reality through its structure: front-loaded economic concessions to Iran in exchange for keeping open a passage that American military power could never guarantee by force.

For global markets and policymakers, the message is clear. The rules governing international commerce have fundamentally changed. Military supremacy no longer translates directly into economic leverage or security of supply chains. The nations and companies that adapt to this new reality—recognizing that chokepoints can flow in either direction—will be best positioned to navigate an era where economic warfare has replaced military confrontation as the primary instrument of geopolitical competition.
