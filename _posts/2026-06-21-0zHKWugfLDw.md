## Veteran Macro Analyst Predicts Explosive Move Higher as 44-Year Bull Market Nears Its Spectacular Finale

The markets are poised to break out of their months-long consolidation phase in dramatic fashion—and the move could surprise even the most optimistic investors. That's according to David Hunter, longtime macro analyst and returning guest on Metals and Miners, who recently joined host Gary Bone to discuss the intersection of Federal Reserve policy, macroeconomic conditions, and market expectations.

"This is a historic period," Hunter told viewers. "I call it the end of a 44-year secular bull market. And I think this is going to end in pretty spectacular fashion—meaning a parabolic runup into a top."

### The Federal Reserve's New Direction

The discussion turned immediately to the Federal Reserve, where Kevin Worsh has assumed leadership, aligned with the Trump administration's policy priorities. Hunter noted that while Jerome Powell has remained as a voting governor until his term expires January 31st, 2028, this arrangement is unlikely to create significant friction.

"Worsh is well respected in the industry and within the Federal Reserve," Hunter explained. "He's a calming voice. He's not somebody that creates conflict. I think Powell respects the position of chairman enough that he'll be a voting member. I don't think he's going to get into big conflicts with Worsh or try to run the committee from a second spot."

Hunter expects Worsh will have an easier time cutting rates than the current market consensus suggests, particularly as inflation begins to roll back over in the coming months.

"A lot of what we've had here is basically the result of the conflict in Iran, the runup in oil prices and other commodity prices," he said. "Now that we're going the other way on oil and going the other way in terms of the conflict—at least seemingly—you're going to see inflation get back to the downtrend that it was in before the conflict started."

Hunter anticipates one to two rate cuts before year-end, potentially surprising a market currently pricing in no cuts at all.

### The Bond Market Will Lead, Not Follow

Perhaps most provocative was Hunter's assertion about the relationship between the bond market and Federal Reserve policy. "I am also a bull on the bond market and have said forever: the bond market sets rates, not the Fed," he stated.

In Hunter's view, the bond market is bottoming, with rates topping out. As the economy shows more obvious signs of slowing and oil prices head toward $60, investors will witness a significant bond rally with rates coming down. Crucially, the Fed will be following rates down rather than leading them.

Hunter acknowledged the challenges facing Worsh's desire to shrink the Fed's balance sheet back to normalized levels. "That's not going to be easy to do given the macro situation we have, because I think the economy is slowing," he said. "I probably said this when we met long ago, but I still think we're heading for a global bust—and which means it's not just a recession, it's something much worse."

The analyst emphasized that macro conditions will ultimately trump any intention to shrink the balance sheet. "You might be able to do small amounts of it at times, but between now and that bust—and I think a bust is next year—once that bust hits, expanding the balance sheet is going to be the story, not shrinking it."

### Why the Economy Can Slow While Markets Surge

Hunter's outlook presents an apparent paradox: he's bearish on the broader economy yet bullish on markets. He addressed this apparent contradiction directly.

"You know, I'm talking negative on the economy, probably more negative than anybody out there in terms of my expectations over the next 12 to 18 months," he admitted. "However, you're still in that kind of sweet spot where inflation can start rolling over, rates can start coming down, and the economy can still seem like it's holding its own. During that period, I think you're going to see the momentum of the tape pull more and more people in."

Hunter sees several catalysts converging: the resolution of the Iran conflict removing a geopolitical headwind, the Fed appearing to "win" its battle against inflation, and the psychological relief of avoiding what many feared was an imminent recession.

"The Fed has a win at its back," he explained. "If inflation comes down because of oil, if the economy holds together enough to believe it's still intact, all of a sudden it's: 'Yeah, we got to win our back. We've been so skeptical and defensive. We've got to get back on board.'"

The result, according to Hunter, could be a concentrated buying spree that pushes markets sharply higher.

### Specific Price Targets: A 30% Move in Just Months

Hunter laid out ambitious targets across major indices, with most representing approximately 30% upside from current levels:

- **S&P 500**: 10,000 (potentially by end of summer, beginning of fall)
- **NASDAQ**: 36,000
- **Dow Jones**: 67,000
- **Russell 2000**: 4,000

"That's what I call a meltup," Hunter noted. "My current target on the S&P is 10,000—so I'm way beyond anybody else's expectations."

When asked about timing, Hunter was emphatic: "This year for sure. And it could be by the end of the summer, beginning of fall."

### Metals and Miners: Even More Explosive Potential

Hunter's most enthusiastic commentary focused on the precious metals sector, where he sees even more dramatic moves ahead.

"Silver at $200 and gold at $7,000," he said. "I think you know we had that huge runup in December, January into that top and we've spent the last several months consolidating that in several steps. I think the lows we saw last week should be your lows for this period, and we come out of that consolidation in a very steep way this summer and into the fall."

Crucially, Hunter expects miners to significantly outperform the metals themselves. His specific targets include:

- **SIJ**: 90
- **SIL**: 220
- **GDX**: 180
- **GDXJ**: 250

"Do the math on those numbers for the gold miners—it's well over a double," he explained. "And for the silver, it's a triple or more."

The analyst sees the recent consolidation as healthy, creating the conditions for what he called "a wall of worry" that prices can overcome and run "pretty hot."

### The AI-Dot-Com Comparison: Similarities and Differences

Hunter was asked about an analysis comparing the current AI cycle to the dot-com era, suggesting markets are at roughly a 1998 equivalent with potentially two years remaining in the bull market.

"I didn't see it. I'm not big on overlays, but I get it," Hunter replied. "My sense is what I've said for a long time: when we get to the top, you're going to have all kinds of narrative out there telling you this thing has years to run—maybe two years, maybe three years to run—that the Fed's just beginning an easing cycle, that AI is in its infancy. Those are the narratives that I think are going to make it hard to get out at the top, because it's going to be convincing."

Hunter views such analyses as fitting into the narrative machine that accompanies market tops, while believing the actual timeline is much shorter. "I think we're only months away from a top," he said.

He did acknowledge important similarities between the AI and dot-com buildouts—both featuring dramatic capital requirements and exciting technological narratives. However, he noted a crucial difference: "You also get a rapid kind of transition into adopting AI in lots of companies. You're seeing productivity gains. I think some of the easy transition took place pretty quickly, amazingly quickly."

Hunter questioned whether investors have frontloaded much of AI's potential benefit this time around, unlike the dot-com era where much was "on the come" that would take years to materialize. "I'm not sure we're factoring in how we frontloaded a lot this time," he said. "I think there may be a kind of a first surge and then a leveling off, and yet you still have AI companies having to compete with each other and jockey for market share."

### A Consumer Economy Under Pressure

Underlying Hunter's market optimism is a deeply skeptical view of the consumer-driven economy. While recent retail sales have appeared strong, he urged viewers to look beneath the surface.

"We incomes, although jobs are okay, incomes are not going to be able to prop up spending like that for very long," he warned. "Savings has been drawn down. We've talked about forever a have and have not economy—that continues to be the case. Half the economy is really struggling just to get by month-to-month while the other half is kind of propping things up."

Hunter sees the AI infrastructure buildout—data centers, energy expansion—as offsetting what would normally be a consumer-driven economy showing signs of fatigue. But he believes this offset won't last forever, particularly given the extraordinary leverage embedded in the financial system.

"More leverage than we've ever had going into a downturn," he emphasized. "That's going to be the reason I say this is going to be so much bigger than just a normal recession."

### The Bottom Line: Prepare for the Final Act

David Hunter's thesis is clear: investors should prepare for a powerful summer and fall rally that will push markets dramatically higher—a parabolic surge marking the end of a 44-year secular bull market. The conditions are aligning: inflation rolling over, rates set to fall, geopolitical tensions easing, and a psychological shift among investors who have been too defensive.

Yet Hunter wants viewers to understand what comes after this finale. "It's going to be hard to want to be out at the top or to want to go the other way, because you don't get to those kind of levels easily," he said. "So there's another side of the mountain coming—but for the next few months, I think it's up and away."

The message is one of cautious participation in what could be a historic move higher, with eyes wide open to what follows. Hunter sees a "global bust" arriving next year—something far worse than a typical recession—making the coming months both an opportunity and a warning for investors positioning their portfolios accordingly.
