Judy Shelton: Rothbard’s Legacy and the Future of Sound Money

November 28, 2026 1782 words misesmedia
Watch Source Video
100 Years of Rothbard: His Bequest for the Future | Judy Shelton · misesmedia

Introduction

In a keynote address at the Mises Institute’s annual summit, economist and current Counselor to the U.S. Treasury Secretary, Dr. Judy Shelton, reflected on the centennial of Murray N. Rothbard’s birth. Shelton explored how Rothbard’s ideas are increasingly influencing global economic policy, from Argentina to Europe, while navigating her own transition from academic advocacy to government service. Her talk offered a candid look at the tension between ideological purity and political reality, highlighting the enduring relevance of sound money principles in an era of fiat currency debasement.

From Academia to the Treasury

Shelton began by acknowledging the unique circumstances of her appearance. Having recently joined the U.S. Department of the Treasury as Counselor to the Secretary, she operates under strict ethical guidelines that prevent her from discussing official duties. Despite this constraint, she emphasized her excitement about moving from theoretical debate to the “crucible of policy decision-making.”

Her career has spanned significant institutions:

  • The Hoover Institution: Where she worked alongside figures like Milton Friedman and Condoleezza Rice, authoring The Coming Soviet Crash and Money Meltdown.
  • Atlas Network (formerly Atlas Economic Research Foundation): Where she co-directed the Sound Money Project.
  • Government Service: Serving as the U.S. Executive Director of the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development in London.

Shelton also recalled her controversial 2019 nomination to the Federal Reserve Board of Governors by President Trump, a nomination that was ultimately rescinded by the Biden administration in 2021. She noted with amusement the hostile online reactions to her potential appointment, which often characterized her advocacy for the gold standard as “dangerous” or rooted in misunderstanding of fiat currency benefits.

The Moral and Intellectual Compass

Shelton described the Mises Institute as her “conscience.” She noted that although Treasury’s ethics lawyers had cleared her to accept the speaking honorarium, she was declining it: “Please consider it my personal contribution to your fine organization and your important work.” She has three Mises Institute books in her new office:

  1. Money, Sound and Unsound by Joseph T. Salerno.
  2. Murray Rothbard, The Making of an Austrian Economist by Dr. Salerno and Dr. Patrick Newman.
  3. A compilation of Rothbard’s works, including What Has Government Done to Our Money? and The Case for a 100% Gold Dollar.

She used the third book as a metaphor for the subtle pressures of public service. The first two, she said, sit on prominent display in her office “among an array of selected works flanked by heavy bookends, so everyone can see the sort of things I like to read.” The volume carrying Rothbard’s “incendiary” titles, by contrast, she keeps “locked away in my cabinet” — before backpedaling: “No, I’m just kidding. Well, sort of.” The joke was her way of illuminating what changes when an economist moves from being carefree about putting ideas out there to feeling directly connected to the consequences of them.

Jefferson, Rothbard, and the Definition of Money

Shelton drew a direct line from Thomas Jefferson’s foundational monetary philosophy to Murray Rothbard’s modern critiques. She highlighted Jefferson’s Notes on the Establishment of a Money Unit and of a Coinage for the United States, written in March 1784, and quoted him: “If we determine that a dollar shall be our unit, we must then say with precision what a dollar is.” Jefferson’s argument, she said, was that the nation’s money unit had to work for everyone — straightforward, simple and trustworthy — rather than serving those lobbying for a more sophisticated approach to money and finance. The Coinage Act that followed on Jefferson’s recommendations duly defined the dollar as 371.25 grains of pure silver or 24.75 grains of pure gold. While at Atlas, Shelton’s Sound Money Project worked with the Library of Congress to publish high-resolution images of Jefferson’s handwritten pages alongside a matching typewritten text. That founding instinct — fixing the standard of weights and measures in the same breath as fixing the monetary standard — is enshrined in Article I, Section 8 of the U.S. Constitution, which grants Congress the power to coin money, regulate the value thereof and of foreign coin, and fix the standard of weights and measures, all in the same sentence.

Shelton contrasted this original intent with the current Federal Reserve policy, which explicitly targets a 2% annual inflation rate. She criticized the Fed’s redefinition of “stable prices” as “stable inflation,” calling it an oxymoron that deliberately debases the currency.

However, Shelton acknowledged the practical limitations of Rothbard’s proposal for a 100% gold-backed dollar. She cited the arithmetic she said she is “forced to acknowledge”: outstanding Federal Reserve Bank notes — U.S. currency in circulation — stood at $2.4 trillion as of a few days before the speech, against official gold reserves held by Treasury on its balance sheet worth just over $1 trillion, a figure that only holds at a spot price of roughly $4,000 per troy ounce. More than half of those paper notes circulate outside the United States, the $100 bill being a particular favorite. “We’re in no position to make even our current paper money convertible into gold,” she said, and she does not see 100% gold redeemability happening soon. Could gold nevertheless serve as a “monetary anchor,” exerting monetary discipline if linked to the issuance of a gold-convertible Treasury security? That is the idea she has been pushing for years under the name Treasury Trust Bonds — a proposal she was careful to label her own long-standing personal view, not official policy.

The Global Resurgence of Austrian Economics

Shelton argued that Rothbard’s ideas are no longer confined to libertarian think tanks but are gaining traction in mainstream political circles worldwide.

  • Argentina: She cited President Javier Milei as a prime example of Rothbard’s influence. Milei, who proudly proclaims himself an anarcho-capitalist and even named one of his cloned dogs Murray, credits Rothbard’s writings on monopoly and competition — together with Man, Economy, and State — with making him throw out his formal training in neoclassical economic theory. Speaking in April about Argentina’s deficit reduction, Milei declared: “We are fulfilling Rothbard’s dream.”
  • Europe: Shelton was careful not to pronounce judgment on the mass public protests in France or on whether Marine Le Pen’s National Rally is responding appropriately to them, nor on the rise of the AfD — the party typically labelled far-right in the media. “I’m not saying this is good or bad,” she said. What interested her was the substance of the monetary ideas circulating around those movements, which she had heard discussed at a conference in Germany. One adviser floated a new national D-Mark with partial gold backing to establish trust. Another called for German official gold held abroad to be repatriated so it could underwrite future credibility. One economist proposed that cash and deposits be 100% backed by gold using existing Eurosystem reserves — provided gold were revalued at €35,000 per ounce — and noted that Germany holds substantially more gold than needed to back its share of the money supply and could lend the surplus to countries short of reserves. A further adviser favoured Hayek-style currency competition, including privately issued and potentially gold-backed money, letting citizens choose the better currency. The common denominator, Shelton said: less monetary discretion, stronger protection of German gold and savings, resistance to the digital euro, and a substantially harder monetary system than the euro architecture of today.

Shelton observed that the through-line running from Buenos Aires to Berlin is a demand for less monetary discretion and harder protection of savings against state-driven devaluation.

The Greenspan Paradox

To illustrate the difficulty of maintaining free-market convictions in positions of power, Shelton shared a long-running story about Alan Greenspan, whom she had known for decades. An acolyte of Ayn Rand, Greenspan wrote in Gold and Economic Freedom that “gold and economic freedom are inseparable,” that “in the absence of the gold standard, there’s no way to protect savings from confiscation through inflation,” and that gold “stands as a protector of property rights.”

When Shelton was writing Money Meltdown, she learned that Greenspan personally held the trademark for his essay Gold and Economic Freedom — published in Ayn Rand’s compendium Capitalism: The Unknown Ideal — and was not willing to grant permission to quote from it. He impressed on her that his earlier writings, now that he headed the U.S. central bank, could carry potential financial implications. Shelton worked out the workaround herself: she told him she would paraphrase his prior statements in the main text but give exact references in the chapter notes, so anyone could look them up and confirm his words. She also offered to hand-deliver the first copy of Money Meltdown to him at the Fed. He said thank you — and then asked her to please cover it with brown paper. No kidding, she noted, to laughter.

Shelton contrasted that with a much more recent exchange. In July 2019, the week after Trump tweeted his intention to nominate her for the Fed Board, a Washington Post opinion piece headlined “Judy Shelton is a dangerous pick for the Fed Board” accused her of using “sound money” and “dependable dollar” as code words for a crank right-wing fringe. That morning she emailed Greenspan, echoing his own earlier sentence back at him: “Dear Alan, I’m noting an almost hysterical antagonism toward the gold standard these days.” He wrote right back: “Dear Judy, interesting times. If gold is such a worthless metal, then why does the US government and all other major governments hold so much of it in storage? Best, Allen.” Why indeed, Shelton added — and why are central banks buying even more of it these days?

Conclusion: Rothbard’s Bequest

Shelton concluded by reframing Murray Rothbard not as a historical figure but as a live source of intellectual energy. What she wanted to celebrate on what would have been his 100th birthday, she said, was not only his legacy but what that legacy might mean for the global economic and political future — “I think of Rothbard’s legacy more in terms of Rothbard’s bequest. His gift.” Through “his keen analytical logic, his compelling books and articles, and his unbowing intellectual commitment and integrity,” she argued, Rothbard has bequeathed the world a set of views that are even now finding new adherents.

Whether viewed through a leftist lens (taxation as theft) or a rightist one (free-market infrastructure), Rothbard’s ideas defy simple categorization but resonate across the political spectrum. As global financial systems face increasing strain, Shelton suggested that we are on the cusp of a pivotal moment where these ideas may reshape the proper role of the state and the future of money.
