Oil Plunges 20%: Is $60 Or $125 Next? | Doomberg · David Lin

Introduction

In late November, crude oil prices experienced a significant correction, with WTI crude dropping approximately 20% from its recent highs of $104 to around $89. This decline coincided with the U.S. midterm elections and shifting geopolitical dynamics in the Middle East. David Lin, host of Doomberg, joined by head writer David (referred to as “David” in the transcript context), dissected the drivers behind this volatility. The discussion moved beyond simple supply and demand, exploring how market prices reflect a complex web of shipping frictions, potential de-escalation in Iran, and the broader implications for global inflation and the war in Ukraine.

Market Equilibrium and Geopolitical Risk

The core thesis presented by Doomberg is that the current oil price reflects an “unstable freeze” rather than true stability. The market has priced out the immediate risk of a third wave of air strikes or a full-scale restart of kinetic warfare between the U.S./Israel and Iran. Instead, the prevailing outlook is one of economic war, blockades, and low-level friction in the Strait of Hormuz.

According to Doomberg’s modeling:

  • Current Equilibrium ($85–$95 WTI): This range represents the “all-in” price under current conditions. It includes not just the commodity cost but also significant premiums for shipping risks, insurance, and the use of shadow fleets to circumvent sanctions and conflict zones.
  • De-escalation Scenario ($56–$60): If full peace were to break out in the Middle East, removing all logistical frictions and sanctions, prices would collapse toward this lower band. The brief dip in July following a temporary agreement suggested this level is the true underlying market clearing price without geopolitical premiums.
  • Escalation Scenario ($125+): In the event of catastrophic war escalation, prices could spike to $125 or higher before global demand destruction kicks in, potentially leading to an economic collapse and a subsequent crash back to $60.

The Role of Shipping Frictions

A critical factor often overlooked is that oil prices are not just about extraction costs but also transportation. Doomberg highlighted that the current price includes a premium for navigating high-friction environments. Even though actual flow through the Strait of Hormuz has remained relatively consistent, the cost of moving oil—via shadow tankers and middlemen operating at night to avoid detection—remains elevated.

If these frictions were removed through a genuine peace deal or effective de-escalation, the “premium” baked into the price would vanish, causing a sharp drop. Conversely, as long as the region remains in a state of high tension without full-scale war, prices will likely hover in the $80–$90 range, which Doomberg notes is actually a sweet spot for producers in regions like the Permian Basin.

Midterm Elections and Domestic Policy Impacts

The U.S. midterm elections have unlocked new certainty for global actors. With Democrats gaining control of Congress, the geopolitical calculus has shifted:

  • Iran: Likely calculating that there is less political will in the current Congress for aggressive military action against Iran compared to the previous administration.
  • Ukraine: President Zelensky and Kyiv have cheered the results, anticipating continued or increased support from Democratic senators like Richard Blumenthal. However, Doomberg notes that opposition to funding Ukraine was more of a Trump-specific issue than a broader Congressional one.
  • Russia: Moscow is not thrilled but remains pragmatic, continuing its strategy of attrition.

Permitting Reform and Energy Demand

Contrary to the narrative that Democratic victories will stifle energy development, Doomberg predicts a major legislative breakthrough in the upcoming lame-duck session: comprehensive permitting reform. This law could accelerate construction for data centers, oil and gas drilling, and renewables by bypassing environmental hurdles.

  • Data Centers: Despite voters rejecting initiatives opposing data centers in local ballots, federal permitting reform may lead to a boom in construction.
  • Natural Gas Dominance: Data centers require immediate power availability, not just the lowest cost. Natural gas wins this race over nuclear or hydro due to speed of deployment. With permitting reform, U.S. energy dominance could leap forward, increasing demand for natural gas (e.g., EQT in Appalachia) and potentially impacting long-term oil demand dynamics through increased economic activity.

Inflation Expectations and Diesel Crack Spreads

The bond market is signaling a shift in inflation expectations. The 10-year break-even inflation rate has been declining, correlating with falling oil prices. Doomberg emphasizes that diesel is a direct input into global inflation, as it powers long-haul shipping and logistics.

  • Diesel Crack Spreads: These spreads (the difference between crude oil price and refined diesel price) hit all-time highs of $100–$115 per barrel but have begun to soften. A year ago, they were $20–$30.
  • The Putin Diesel Deal: The administration attempted a deal with Russia to supply 300,000–500,000 tons of diesel to global markets to lower prices. However, this deal has largely fallen apart due to Ukrainian attacks on Russian refineries and local opposition in the U.S. to receiving Russian fuel. Nevertheless, even partial flows or the anticipation of them help stabilize prices.
  • Fed Policy: Lower diesel costs could provide relief for the Federal Reserve, potentially influencing interest rate decisions. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has argued against hiking rates into an inflation shock, a stance that aligns with the market’s hope for softer energy prices.

The Ukraine-Iran Connection and World War III Risks

Doomberg draws a stark connection between the war in Iran and the conflict in Ukraine, warning of a potential “World War III” scenario. The central argument is that the West is losing an attrition war against an industrial coalition of Iran, Russia, China, and North Korea, which are producing missiles at a rate far exceeding Western capacity.

  • Attrition Pocket: Hundreds of billions in Western aid have flowed into Ukraine, but weapons are being trapped east of the Dnieper River as Russia blows bridges and advances.
  • Missile Shortages: The U.S. is running out of Patriot missiles and long-range standoff munitions. When the conflict with Iran escalated, limited Western inventories had to be split across two fronts, leaving Ukraine vulnerable.
  • Russian Strategy: Russia is not seeking a ceasefire because it believes it can win through attrition. Doomberg argues that if Putin were losing, he would be asking for peace talks. Instead, Russia is holding firm with maximalist demands.
  • The Danger: If the West fails to recognize its industrial disadvantage, a push into Kyiv or further escalation could occur without an effective Western response, potentially triggering a broader global conflict. Doomberg concludes that all wars are failures of diplomacy and rackets, criticizing the U.S. for tearing up the Obama-era Iran deal (JCPOA), which has led to an emboldened regime more anxious to acquire nuclear weapons than ever before.

Conclusion

The recent 20% drop in oil prices reflects a market betting on de-escalation and the removal of extreme war risks, settling into a “high-friction” equilibrium of $85–$95. However, this stability is fragile. The interplay between domestic U.S. policy (permitting reform), global inflation (diesel crack spreads), and geopolitical attrition wars (Ukraine/Iran) creates a complex landscape. Doomberg warns that without addressing the industrial base disparities with Russia, China, Iran, and North Korea, the West risks losing not just economic battles but potentially facing catastrophic military outcomes in Ukraine.
