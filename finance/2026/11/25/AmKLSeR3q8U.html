Subtitle: Why experienced investors are ignoring the Nvidia hype and buying unloved assets in offshore oil, fintech, and tankers.

Introduction

In a recent episode of Capital Cause, host Danny interviewed Brad McFadden, a key figure at the Capitalist Exploits Insider newsletter alongside Chris Minton. The discussion centered on the dramatic market volatility seen in late November 2026, specifically following news regarding Nvidia CEO Jensen Wong and President Trump. While the S&P 500 and NASDAQ saw massive single-day rallies—with Nvidia surging over 31%—McFadden offered a sobering perspective: these moves may not signal a recovery but rather the chaotic nature of a bear market beginning to take shape.

The core thesis presented by McFadden is one of disciplined value investing. Rather than chasing high-flying AI stocks or trying to time the market bottom, he advocates for finding “unloved” assets with strong fundamentals and holding them through the volatility. This article breaks down his analysis of the current market environment, the parallels to past bubbles, and the specific contrarian positions he is taking right now.

The Illusion of Recovery: Bear Market Kickbacks

The immediate catalyst for the recent market surge was the resolution of allegations against Jensen Wong, leading to a 31.7% jump in Nvidia’s stock price and significant gains across the NASDAQ (up 6.5%) and S&P 500 (up 4.7%). For many retail investors, this felt like the “all clear” signal they had been waiting for. However, McFadden cautioned against interpreting this as a definitive end to market stress.

Drawing on his extensive experience managing funds since the mid-1990s, including the 1987 crash, the Asian Tiger crisis, and the Dot-com (TMT) bubble, McFadden identified these sharp rallies as classic “bear market kickbacks.” He pointed to historical data from the early 2000s, where the NASDAQ experienced massive intra-bear-market rallies of 30-40% before continuing its downward trend.

“Bear markets are just ugly, messy, and very confusing… You have these kickbacks, these bear market rallies, which can be absolutely huge.” — Brad McFadden

The key takeaway is that volatility does not equal safety. Investors who sell during a dip and wait for the market to “settle down” often fail because they cannot objectively define what “settling down” looks like. Instead of trying to predict the bottom, McFadden suggests focusing on asset quality.

AI Valuations: Hope, Hype, and Circularity

A central theme of the interview was skepticism toward the current AI trade. McFadden stated clearly that Capitalist Exploits has been avoiding major AI-related companies for years because the valuations are driven by “hope and hype” rather than sustainable earnings growth. He noted that a significant portion of the market cap in indices like the NASDAQ is now priced on expectations that may not materialize.

McFadden also highlighted structural risks within the AI sector, such as circular financing models where major tech companies effectively hold up each other’s valuations. In this context, the recent Nvidia rally was viewed not as a fundamental revaluation but as an “illusion” or a knee-jerk reaction to news headlines. The underlying issues of overvaluation and speculative fervor remain unchanged.

Bitcoin as a Leading Indicator

The discussion extended to cryptocurrency, specifically Bitcoin. Danny noted that while the NASDAQ rallied significantly, Bitcoin remained flat. Historically, Bitcoin has correlated with liquidity and often acted as a leading indicator for tech stocks. McFadden agreed that this divergence is telling. If Bitcoin, which shares similar “hope and hype” drivers with AI stocks, is not participating in the rally, it suggests the broader market move lacks underlying strength.

McFadden dismissed Bitcoin as an investment (“it’s rubbish”), citing Charlie Munger’s famous skepticism. However, he acknowledged that the correlation between crypto sentiment and tech stock sentiment remains strong. The flatlining of Bitcoin while stocks rise could indicate that this is a “bull trap” rather than a genuine recovery in liquidity.

The Contrarian Playbook: Buying What Others Hate

If the market is entering or already in a bear phase, how should investors position themselves? McFadden’s strategy is simple but psychologically difficult: Buy unloved assets.

He emphasized that his firm does not try to time the market with precision. Instead, they look for sectors and companies where the fundamental thesis has not changed, but the price has fallen due to broader market fear or sector rotation. Currently, their portfolio focuses on cyclical, out-of-favor industries.

Key Positions and Switches

McFadden detailed several recent moves made by his team:

  1. Tanker Stocks (Profit Taking):
    • The firm had held significant positions in tanker stocks like International Seaways, Frontline, and DHT.
    • These holdings performed exceptionally well, with Frontline seeing returns of approximately 8x when dividends were reinvested since their entry in April 2020.
    • As these stocks ran their course, the firm took profits to redeploy capital into cheaper opportunities.
  2. Offshore Oil Services (The Core Thesis):
    • SLB (Schlumberger): The firm switched out of Frontline and into SLB.
    • The Rationale: There is a massive lack of “sustaining capital” investment in the oil and gas sector, particularly outside of US shale. Shale production is topping out (especially in the Permian Basin), forcing a return to conventional offshore exploration.
    • Supply Constraint: No new drill ships or semi-submersibles will be built before 2035 because shipyards are reluctant to take on such capital-intensive projects after previous cycles bankrupted operators.
    • Demand Increase: Energy security concerns (heightened by geopolitical events like the Iran situation) will drive a boom in capex for exploration and development, with roughly two-thirds of this spending going offshore.
    • Conclusion: With supply fixed and demand rising, offshore drillers and service providers are poised for significant upside over the next 5-10 years.
  3. Kaspi (KSPI): The Oddball Fintech Play:
    • Overview: A Kazakhstan-based fintech company trading on US exchanges (NYSE/NASDAQ).
    • Valuation: Trading at a P/E ratio of roughly 6-7x with a dividend yield around 9%.
    • Opportunity: The stock fell from $110 to $85, allowing the firm to buy back in at prices similar to their original entry point.
    • Diversification: This asset provides exposure to different geographies (Central Asia/Turkey) and cash flow drivers, diversifying risk away from traditional Western industrial sectors.

The Psychology of Holding Through Volatility

The hardest part of value investing is not finding the assets, but holding them when everyone else is screaming that the market is going to zero. McFadden warned that bear markets “shake you to the core.” Fear is a more potent emotion than greed, leading investors to sell good assets at low prices.

He referenced the famous John Templeton maxim: “Bull markets are born on pessimism, grow on skepticism, mature on optimism, and die on euphoria.” The current phase appears to be one of dying euphoria for AI stocks, but potentially early pessimism for value sectors like oil services.

Advice for Individual Investors

McFadden highlighted a distinct advantage individual investors have over institutional fund managers: Time Horizon.

  • Fund managers are pressured by quarterly performance benchmarks and may be forced to sell unloved assets to avoid underperforming the S&P 500 in the short term.
  • Individuals do not have this constraint. They can hold a thesis for 5-10 years without worrying about relative performance metrics.

The strategy is to find value, hold on to it, and don’t let the volatility interrupt the compounding machine. If you own a fractional interest in a business with real cash flows (like SLB or Kaspi), ignore the noise surrounding AI stocks and index movements. Look at supply, demand, and earnings reports. If those fundamentals are intact, stay invested.

Conclusion

The recent market rally driven by Nvidia news is likely a temporary relief valve in a more complex bearish environment. Brad McFadden’s approach offers a counter-intuitive path: ignore the hype, sell overextended winners like tanker stocks, and buy unloved, cash-flow-positive assets in offshore energy and emerging market fintech.

By focusing on long-term fundamental dynamics—such as the impending shortage of offshore drilling capacity—and maintaining emotional discipline, investors can navigate the chaos of bear markets. The key is not to guess where the bottom is, but to ensure you own assets that are cheap enough to survive it and strong enough to thrive when the cycle turns.
