Stock & Silver RESET: The SEC Rule Change Behind the 2027 Playbook

November 27, 2026 1122 words Felix Nikolas Prehn
Watch Source Video
Stock & Silver RESET Is Here - Here's How I'm Investing In 2027 · Felix Nikolas Prehn

A regulatory shift just went through in US markets that changes how stocks can be sold outside regular hours. Felix Prehn says he and his co-host Winston have not seen a move like this: Wall Street asked the SEC for the permission, and it was granted in one business day. It lands at the same moment the big US names are moving toward near-continuous trading. The two changes together are what he means by a reset — and the rules of 2027 depend on them.

What the SEC Actually Changed

There is a screenshot of the order: “an order granting temporary exemptive relief pursuant to…” — the rest of the sentence is too long for his brain, as he puts it. What matters is the mechanics.

To short a stock you must first find shares to borrow: “the first thing you have to do is you have to find shares that you can borrow… it’s called the locate rule, and it’s been there for years for a very, very good reason. It stops people selling shares that don’t exist. Because if you can sell shares that don’t exist, you can push a price down as far as you like… you could sell more shares than there are out there and that would crash the stock.”

That rule has now been switched off — but only outside the normal session. “It’s still on during the normal trading day when you’re awake, but outside of it, in the hours before the market opens and after it closes, the big players no longer have to find the shares first. They can short and go looking for the shares to borrow later.”

The process is what he flags: “they asked for this and they got it approved in one business day. There was no consultation, no public debate, no comment period — nothing.” And the relief “is going to run until the end of 2027. So we got a full year.”

Why the Hours Matter

The rule change lines up with the expansion of trading hours: “from early December, you can trade those big US names 23 hours a day, 5 days a week. And you put those two things next to each other…”

The vulnerable periods are the thin, quiet hours, when liquidity is low because most investors are asleep. The evidence is already on the tape: “there have been several stocks this year that moved 1,000% in a minute. And those thousand% moves always happened pre-market. So not when most people are awake, but before, in those thin quiet hours. Up like a rocket and then obviously back.”

With the locate rule off in exactly those hours, a large player can hit a thin book with aggressive shorts and no borrowing friction. The result is a price that looks terrifying on screen — “you wake up, you see it’s down some insane amount overnight, and you don’t understand why, and you sell in fear, and then it might recover without you. And that’s the trap this reset sets. It’s a trap for your analysis. It’s a trap for your nerves.”

The Psychology of a Reset

By “reset” he does not mean the end of the world — he means the underlying rules changed, and rule changes tend to favor whoever has the most capital. Old instincts, like reacting to an overnight gap, become liabilities in the new regime. The best traders he has known were not the smartest; they were the ones who did not flinch, because they had a written plan and stuck to it.

Silver: Paper vs. Physical

On the shiny stuff: “this silver shake-out we’re seeing here — we’re presently down 65% from the high, it’s about the biggest on record, and your instinct when you hear that is ‘oh no, silver’s broken, stay away.’”

His counter is to separate the two markets. There is paper silver, “what gets traded on screens,” and physical silver — “which is more likely what you probably own, unless you own an index fund, which is a different story. Mostly they move together, but every once in a while they actually pull apart.”

For the long-term crowd he points at the moving average on the chart — the caption drops its length, so read the number off his screen rather than treating it as a stated figure — and notes how far price sits below it: “there was one here in 2020… that was the COVID crash. You saw what happened afterwards. And then there was one [more, year dropped by the caption].” Spikes in selling volume during a decline, on his reading, mark proximity to a bottom rather than the start of a collapse.

Markets Bottom on Bad News

“Markets bottom on bad news, not on good news.” His example is current: good news like “Jensen Huang the almighty is reinstated” is never the market bottom — “it’s the opposite of what feels true. When everything looks great and the news is wonderful, that is usually closer to the top.” Right now, “the news is awful… ghastly.”

The AI trade has become the crowded one: chips, data centers, infrastructure. For 2027 he expects a rotation away from pure hardware toward the companies that monetize AI — “just your good old… your Meta, your Google, your Microsoft… I’m like an Amazon here,” while “I’d be cautious on Oracle, quite frankly.” These are the businesses with the sales teams and the customer bases to fold AI into products people already pay for.

Three Rules for 2027

  1. Own assets, not cash. In an era of money printing, cash loses value; put money to work consistently.
  2. Diversify properly. A standard index fund no longer diversifies, because the indices are heavily weighted to the same crowded AI trade. Spread across industries and asset classes.
  3. Follow the money, but demand quality. Wall Street has largely abandoned value investing because short-term performance metrics punish managers who hold undervalued names that dip first. That does not make “cheap” equal “value”: he uses PayPal as the example of a stock people call a value play and calls it not one — its quality score for them “is not very good,” which points to deteriorating fundamentals rather than a bargain.

Bottom Line

Near-continuous trading plus a suspended locate rule outside market hours is a liquidity-extraction machine aimed at the emotional investor. The defense is mechanical: understand that thin-hour moves are engineered to be scary, distinguish paper prices from physical markets, and tilt toward the companies that monetize AI rather than those building its infrastructure.
