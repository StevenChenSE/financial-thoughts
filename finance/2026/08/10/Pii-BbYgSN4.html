Technical Analysis: Stripping Out the Noise

At first glance, the silver charts from 2011–2014 and late 2025–2026 appear strikingly similar. However, relying solely on visual resemblance can be misleading. Soloway correctly notes that cycles between gold and silver corrections are tightening, a sign that financial interventions are becoming less effective. Yet, the analysis falls short by excluding the 2008 correction as a benchmark.

Using the indexing method to strip out volatility and compare monthly closing prices reveals a different picture:

  • The 30% Convergence: In the sixth month following the pullback, the 2008, 2011, and 2026 corrections all converge at approximately 30% lower than their monthly benchmark closing levels. This indicates that 2011 is not unique.
  • Downward Trajectory: Silver’s price action in 2008 and 2026 shows a quicker, deeper correction in the first three to four months compared to 2011. This suggests 2008 is a better technical fit.

To confirm this, we must look at gold, which historically leads silver. In both 2008 and 2026, gold experienced a sharper correction than in 2011. By the sixth month of the pullback:

  • 2011: Gold was still negative relative to the benchmark.
  • 2008 and 2026: Gold was positive, signaling a quicker recovery phase.

This divergence strongly suggests that the current market is not in a 2011-style topping pattern but rather a 2008-style accumulation phase.

The Macro Picture: Cracks in the Financial System

Technical data alone does not tell the whole story. The macroeconomic environment in 2026 bears a far greater resemblance to the early days of the 2008 debt crisis than the post-resolution landscape of 2011.

In 2008, the world was in the early stages of a debt crisis and bailout cycle, beginning with the U.S. housing market and spreading to the European sovereign debt crisis of 2009–2011. This period was followed by a coordinated global effort to save the U.S. dollar system, including China’s massive purchases of U.S. debt between 2006 and 2011. This coordination suppressed precious metals prices after the 2011 blow-off top.

Today, we are seeing similar cracks form, particularly with Japan’s debt situation and the crashing value of the yen. Recently, the U.S. Treasury intervened by purchasing Japanese yen. This was not merely a bailout of Japan; it was a bailout of U.S. bonds and the dollar. By propping up the yen, officials like Scott Bessent are effectively threatening currency speculators to prevent Japan from selling its U.S. bond holdings, which could crash the U.S. bond market.

However, this intervention highlights a critical shift:

  1. Loss of Sovereignty: The U.S. is telling major central banks when and how they can sell U.S. debt, even when it is in their best interest to do so.
  2. Acceleration of Gold Buying: This coercive approach is likely accelerating central banks’ efforts to replace U.S. debt with gold on their balance sheets.
  3. No Global Coordination: Unlike 2011, there is no unified global effort to save the dollar. Instead, rising powers like China may now undermine the system, making a major correction in the U.S. dollar and stock market likely.
