The Silent Killer: Why $1 Million Won’t Be Worth $231,000 in 30 Years

Let me start with a post from Dividendology that should concern everyone: “Here’s what $1 million will be worth in 30 years.”

  • $552,000 at 2% inflation
  • $412,000 at 3%
  • $308,000 at 4%
  • $231,000 at 5%

“I think we have inflation greater than 5%,” I say. “So, $1 million over 30 years at an inflation rate greater than 5%—say 6, 7, 8, 9, 10%—you are losing a ton of purchasing power.”

Here’s what I find troubling about this: “This is what I don’t like about our system. They force everyone to speculate in the markets. We don’t have an option. Because if you keep your cash, you’re going to get absolutely wiped out over a long period of time.”

Andy’s conclusion is stark: “So, yes, you have to invest or yes, you have to speculate on something in order to keep your purchasing power.”

The Case for Precious Metals

One of my favorite strategies for preserving wealth is holding precious metals. “Save in gold, save in silver, save in other precious metals. Find what’s cheap, buy what’s cheap, and just sit in it. And then you can sell it off if you need money as you go.”

His harshest assessment is reserved for cash itself: “But if you stay in cash, you’re basically screwed. I mean, that is the one asset class that we know will go down in value, period. I don’t even know if it’s an asset class. I don’t know what you want to call it. It’s like a… liability.”

The Mortgage Paradox: Why You Might Want to Keep That Low Rate

Here’s a perspective most financial advisors won’t share: if you locked in a 2.75% mortgage when rates were low, you might actually benefit from not paying it off early.

“Think of it this way. I bought a house and I have a 2.75% mortgage. If $1 million in 30 years, think of that as like a mortgage, at 5% is only worth $231,000, then hypothetically, I don’t want to pay off my mortgage as soon as possible if you have a low interest rate.”

The logic is elegant: “You want to throw it out as far as you possibly can into the future because you’ll be paying it back with less valuable dollars.”

Andy explains the mechanism: “As long as your mortgage rate is lower than the inflation rate, you’re paying the mortgage back in more depreciated currency.”

This is where most people fail to do the math. “And that’s what a lot of people—they don’t take that into consideration. They only look at the today: ‘Do I pay more today to buy or do I pay less if I rent?’ But if you’re paying a house back with depreciating currency over a long period of time, your actual cost may be lower than what you think.”

The hypothetical example: “Let’s just say you take out a $100,000 mortgage, and if it’s over a 30-year time frame and you pay it back, you may pay it back with 60 or 50 or 40,000 dollars in purchasing power currency.”

“That’s what people need to understand—is that net present value of the mortgage. And when you take that mortgage out, do you think inflation is going to increase or decrease?”

The uncomfortable conclusion: “They made us all into speculators. That’s what they did.”

Reading the Macro Tea Leaves: Oil, Stocks, and Yields

The observation about bond vigilantes is particularly troubling: “I don’t know what the Fed’s forward guidance will be from here, but it seems less and less likely that we’ll see rate cuts, especially since bond vigilantes appear to have decided that in the current environment of uncertainty, 4.3% on the 10-year is not enough.”

The Distinction That Matters: Money Printing vs. Supply Shocks

Andy interjects with what he considers a critical distinction: “What drove this back here in 2022 was a bunch of money printing. Money printing coming into the system, aka inflation, puts everything into a shortage. That’s what inflation is. It’s money being printed faster than the supply of goods and services.”

A supply shock from oil is fundamentally different: “So, the money is in the system, but you get a shock because there’s a gap in oil. So, it’s a weird situation because in order for that to be in balance, you have to take currency out of the system somehow. If you want to match up GDP and money in the system.”

The reality, he argues, is less comforting: “But they’re not going to do that. So, you’re going to get increasing prices of things that are in a shortage and then other things, whatever’s in the system, will have to go down in price. Money’s going to have to flow to where the problems are.”

The implication is clear: “So, we’re seeing potentially a rollover in the S&P because oil’s broken out. And 10-year yields have also broken out. So, how much longer is this going to last? I’m not exactly sure. Are they in there trying to hold yields down? I don’t know. Very well could be.”

The Timing Problem: Why Buying Bottoms Is Harder Than It Sounds

A valuable observation about market mechanics: “Stocks bottom 9 months before earnings do. Don’t avoid buying stocks just because a recession is coming.”

This challenges conventional wisdom: “The markets are forward-looking and they price things before they actually happen. So, this is all priced into the stock price, these declining earnings. And they are anticipating increase of earnings.”

The hard truth for those waiting for perfect entry points: “So, a lot of people will say, ‘Yeah, but they’re going to earn less this year.’ Well, maybe not. It’s already priced in, usually. So, it’s hard to buy bottoms because you can’t really use logic, news, earnings—you can’t use any of that.”

“People are looking ahead and saying, ‘Yeah, it’s going to bottom here in about 9 months.’ Stocks bottom 9 months before earnings. So, you have to buy it 9 months before the projected earnings bottom 9 months from now. Which makes it really difficult.”

The Charts Don’t Lie: Global Exports in Freefall

JP Morgan and Kepler showed what Andy calls “the scale of this crisis in one chart”:

  • Global crude exports are down 9 million barrels per day
  • Global product exports are down 4 million barrels per day
  • “The red line, which is 2026, every other year is the normal range. 2026 is now off the charts, literally. This has never happened before, not in 2022, not in 2020, not in 2008.”

Jeff Currie’s warning is stark: “Mid to late April is when inventories run out.”

“This here being off a cliff, so to speak, is going to eat through inventories rapidly around the world. And on the right here, this is global product exports. So, product exports are rapidly declining. And crude global exports are rapidly declining. It’s a one-two punch here.”

The compounding problem: “You’re sucking it down the back end for, say, gasoline and diesel, and then you’re also sucking it down on your feedstock of oil for those global products. And it’s coming, guys. It’s coming.”

The Question That Baffles Analysts

Andy admits puzzlement: “How is the market pricing this the way that they are? They must know that something like… not even a ceasefire, but they must know that this is going to end relatively quickly. Or maybe they don’t. Maybe the market has it wrong. I don’t know. But this is coming.”

The timeline for recovery is longer than most expect: “These global crude exports and global product exports, this gap is going to flow through the system. And even if we were to stop today, this still is going to be like this for a while, at least for a few months.”

Even getting production back online faces obstacles: “What if that takes 5, 6 months? What if this is pretty persistent on these gaps for 2, 3 months? And then they draw it down, and then production starts to come back, and it may take a couple of months for production to come back on top of clearing out the inventory in that particular area.”

“Because now we’ve got all these dislocations of where inventory is and where it isn’t. And things need to flow. And that’s if it gets back up and running soon.”

Fundamentals Over Forecasts: Warren Buffett’s Wisdom Applied

Andy reveals his investment philosophy with a Warren Buffett quote: “Fundamentals over forecasts. I make no attempt to forecast the general market. My efforts are devoted to finding undervalued securities.”

This mirrors his own approach: “That’s exactly what I do on the website. I don’t try to make forecasts. And people ask me all the time, ‘Well, where do you think… what is the price objective? What’s… where’s this going to go in the next month?’ I don’t know, guys.”

The harder truth: “What you do is you buy undervalued securities, and then you wait for them to get repriced. What you are asking is, ‘When are other people going to buy my stock?’ That’s what you’re ultimately asking me. ‘What day are they going to buy my stock?’ Nobody knows that. Come on, get serious.”

The better approach: “The better thing is, what is undervalued in relationship to history? That’s what the ratios are. You find what sectors are down, and you buy the undervalued securities from a fundamentally fundamental perspective. Then you wait for the market conditions to change and people to come in and revalue those assets.”

“The market prediction stuff is dumb. It doesn’t work that well, if at all. Doing what I’m doing, it’s worked out quite well.”

The Signal to Watch: When to Exit Oil

Andy shares a framework for timing crude oil trades: “If you want to know when it’s time to get bearish oil, just watch refining margins.”

The indicators that mattered in COVID:

  1. Floating storage
  2. Tanker rates
  3. Contango
  4. Refining margins

“After refining margins, which were negative in early May, started to rebound. Refining margins only rebounded because product storage started to drain. Refineries at the time were in a precarious place. OPEC+ had just reduced production by 10 million barrels a day. Mandatory lockdowns destroyed oil demand. So, refining margins had to go up in order for crude oil to rally.”

“When it finally did, that was when crude started to rally with a bit more leg.”

The reverse scenario: “This time around, a similar playbook will happen, but in reverse. Refining margins will likely be a key indicator for when the global economy cannot handle higher prices any longer. We are already seeing some of that in Southeast Asia, but it’s not widespread yet.”

“Once you hear about how refineries are forced to cut throughput and can no longer afford crude, that will be the signal to sell your crude oil longs—in my opinion.”

Andy’s conclusion: “If they are manipulating the oil market, if they are, and I would suspect they are, but that’s just an opinion and I don’t know for a fact, that means that the oil equities are one heck of a deal because they’re pricing in the futures market.”

“And if the futures market is wrong and the physical market’s going to overpower the futures market at some point, we would see a big move in the future. So, I would take that bet. I’m betting that they’re in there jacking around with stuff.”

“So, yeah, I’m going to stay long oil and wait for the physical price to force the futures prices up.”

The Liquidity Trap: Kevin O’Leary’s Definition of Wealth

Kevin O’Leary made waves with his definition of true wealth: “‘You’d be amazed how many wealthy people that say they’re rich do not have liquidity. Liquidity is defined by the ability at any moment to have liquid cash. You’re not rich if it’s all tied up in real estate. You’re not rich if it’s in jewelry and in cars and in boats and all that stuff. Sure, it’s very hard to get $5 million liquid because in this market that makes you $250,000 a year pre-tax. You have a family of four and poo-poo hits the fan in your world and everybody loses their job, you can sustain a family of $250,000 pre-tax. That’s why it’s the magic number.’“

Andy offers a counterpoint: “I don’t know if what he says is that big of a deal. It depends on the situation and each person and their expenses.”

His framework for resilience: “Resiliency is a function of low costs and a lot of wealth and or income.”

“If your income gets hit, you can obviously sell some of your things. In a very bad market, you probably don’t want to sell things because you’re not going to get top dollar for them.”

His personal strategy: “The one thing that I try to do myself is to diversify income streams. That’s one thing. And reduce your liabilities or your expenses. Reduce them as low as you can.”

“So, if you do have a lot of money that’s tied up in your house, but you own your house, well, if you own your house, that means that you have less expenses. And that could be a good thing. That doesn’t mean that it’s bad that your liquidity is tied up in your house.”

“You can be very rich and have low liquidity and still live a fantastic life. And you can be rich with a lot of liquidity. And you could be living a horrible life, although all the things you’re doing take a lot of money.”

“So, I don’t measure someone’s happiness by how much money they spend. I think that’s a poor measurement of health and wealth.”
