Interview with Andy: Oil Price Bullish Breakout vs. Bearish Predictions

April 03, 2026 1819 words

In a recent episode of his ‘Finding Value’ channel, financial analyst Andy dives into the volatile world of commodities, specifically focusing on oil prices. The discussion, which features interactions with community members like Fernando, Fred, and others, explores a stark divide between bearish predictions and Andy’s bullish technical and fundamental analysis.

The Bearish Case: Technical Patterns and Geopolitical Fears

The conversation begins with a bearish outlook from a community member named Fernando. He argues that oil prices are “very bearish” and presents a technical setup involving a “1 2 3 4 5 falling wedge in an ABC correction.” Fernando suggests that if the Strait of Hormuz were shut, oil prices could plummet to $30 a barrel, describing the current resistance levels as a temporary barrier before a significant drop.

Fernando’s bearish sentiment is echoed by others who point to supply-demand dynamics. One observer notes that while physical markets are screaming a shortage, paper markets are “still pretending,” creating a gap that keeps panic at bay for now. Another contributor highlights that within the next 12 months, the U.S. must refinance $10 trillion in debt. With an average interest rate on outstanding U.S. debt at 3.4%, every 1% increase in rates increases interest expenses by $100 billion. This creates a scenario where higher oil prices could exacerbate debt servicing issues, potentially forcing rate hikes that might dampen demand.

Additionally, some analysts look to historical parallels. For instance, comparing the current situation to the 1970s, gold rose 20x while the S&P 500 plunged 50%. Similarly, in the Great Depression, gold stocks returned 500% as the S&P plunged 80%. However, Andy questions the direct applicability of these historical patterns to current mining stocks, noting that in the 1930s, gold was forcibly repriced from $20 to $35 an ounce, which benefited mining companies like Homestake Mining.

Andy’s Bullish Thesis: The “Big Squeeze”

Contrasting sharply with the bearish views, Andy presents a compelling bullish case for oil, rooted in both technical chart patterns and macroeconomic fundamentals.

Technical Analysis: Breaking Resistance

Andy analyzes the long-term chart of oil, identifying a massive consolidation period. “We’ve got this resistance, this is what I drew in there. You draw that down a little bit, right on the resistance. We’re right underneath that. We’re about to break that.” He describes the recent movement as a “big squeeze” and the bottom of “wave two,” suggesting that what follows could be “wave one” of a new upward trend.

Zooming in on the charts, Andy observes: “This is zoomed way in. So, I think he’s in my opinion, off on his count.” He points out that the market is currently “trying to chop sideways” right under a significant resistance level. “If it were me, um I just wouldn’t do anything. I’d just sit in it and wait.”

Andy predicts a breakout: “And we’ve broken that, and I think we’re going to like six or $700 a barrel. I’ll describe how we get there.” He envisions a scenario where oil outperforms the S&P 500 significantly. “Indeed, the oil to S&P 500 ratio is still .8 standard deviations below its long-term trend, while it has historically topped far higher, up to about five standard deviations above it in 1970s.” He calculates that reaching just one standard deviation above the mean could imply “a 500% plus outperformance of oil relative to the stock index.”

The Role of Money Printing and Supply Constraints

A core pillar of Andy’s argument is the relationship between money printing and oil demand. He challenges the notion that money printing doesn’t drive oil demand, stating: “When you print money, it is literally demand for oil.”

He explains the mechanism clearly: “If we’re at war, we are burning fuel. We are We have people eating, and eating is burning fuel to create the food. The fossil fuels are in the fertilizers.” Thus, any stimulus package or money printing intended to boost demand ultimately fuels the supply chain, which is heavily dependent on oil. “And people will tell me how wrong I am. ‘Andy, that’s not right. That doesn’t make any sense. That’s not, you know, when money printing is not demand for oil.’ Okay, well, tell me what money printing is going to purchase.”

Andy illustrates this with a historical parallel to the 1970s oil embargo: “This one here was money was coming into the system. It was coming through bank loans. And the bank loans expands the money supply. And that money supply expanding said, ‘Feed me more things.’ We hit peak conventional oil in America in the 1970. And that’s what that big move is. We had an oil embargo and all this other things. It was money chasing a supply uh shortage of oil.”

Looking ahead, Andy suggests a similar pattern is emerging: “Now, this circle here is going to be a bunch of money printing that occurs with a shortage on the supply of world production. They’re going to print money at a faster pace than oil production can go up. If there is any. And if it goes down, it’s going to amplify the move.”

The Strait of Hormuz and Supply Disruption

The potential closure of the Strait of Hormuz is another critical factor. While some fear it will crash prices, Andy argues it could be the catalyst for the ultimate surge. “The Strait of Hormuz is shut. It is shut. The only way you get this type of movement, he’s got it going down to $30 I don’t know. It’s got to be an April Fool’s joke.”

He elaborates on the supply constraints: “Now, what started this whole thing off is the war. But it was baked into this pattern that something was coming irrespective of the war occurring.” He notes that even if the Strait reopens, recovery will be slow: “They said they’re saying if it reopens, uh it’ll take about two months for port activity to fully normalize.”

Furthermore, Andy highlights the lack of spare capacity in shale oil production. “Shabam says, ‘Permian rigs are down 17% in the last year and flatlined over the last month, despite WTI averaging 91 barrel $91 a barrel in March.’” He explains that companies are reluctant to ramp up drilling: “They want to milk their fields for everything they are worth. They’re I don’t think they’re going to ramp the rig count at prices this low. I think prices need to get way up there. Then they’re going to milk it.”

Nuclear Energy and the Future of Energy

Andy also touches on the transition to nuclear energy, arguing that it is inevitable given the constraints of fossil fuels. “Fossil fuels are tired, guys. They’re They’re not as… This isn’t a young market where we’re just plopping in holes and oil’s just squirting out.” He posits: “We’re going all over the place. We’re going to need as much nuclear as we can put in. And we better do it soon.”

He uses an analogy to illustrate the necessity of energy solutions: “Yellow Bull says, ‘For those who need to hear this, using Chernobyl as an argument against nuclear power is like using the Hindenburg as an argument against commercial aviation, or using a horse-drawn carriage fire as a reason to ban the Tesla Model 3.’”

Wealth Building and Investment Strategy

Beyond oil, Andy shares insights on wealth building and investment strategies, drawing from the wisdom of Warren Buffett. “Warren Buffett’s greatest lesson, never chase. I’m I’m reading this again because this is I think the biggest problem that most people have. In the market, you have time. If an exceptional company trades at insane prices, don’t buy it. Wait for an exceptional company at a reasonable price. When you find it, invest heavily. If you don’t, wait. If there’s no opportunities, just sit in cash.”

This philosophy aligns with his approach to oil: “I save money up find an opportunity, and then I’m quote broke because I buy cash flowing assets. Exactly what I do.”

The Link Between Wealth, Health, and Intelligence

The discussion also touches on the relationship between wealth and health. One contributor notes: “US life expectancy based off level of wealth. Bottom 20% 21 and you know, you can see the life expectancy going up depending on where you’re at in the level of wealth. He says, ‘Health is wealth, literally.’”

Andy offers a different perspective, attributing disparities to decision-making capabilities rather than just statistical averages. “I think stupid people are more prevalent in the bottom 20% in terms of IQ and they make worse decisions. They smoke, they drink, they are not able to make money in the current society.” He concludes: “So really, the bottom 20% if you were to take it on a statistical average, uh they make poor decisions which impacts their health, their wealth, uh on on all accounts. And as you move up into the top 10% and above, uh the top 10% make better decisions on average. So this is just a statistical probability bet. So smarter people make smarter decisions about their health. They also make smarter decisions across the full line of everything. So they are also wealthier. So they’re healthier and wealthier based off of being.”

Conclusion

Andy’s analysis paints a picture of an oil market poised for a dramatic breakout, driven by the interplay of money printing, supply constraints, and the fundamental role of oil in the global economy. “Oil is the most important commodity in the world. Bar none, there is no other master commodity. It is the most important. It is used in the most amount of things. It is everywhere. All wealth came from oil almost.”

As the market navigates these complex dynamics, Andy advises patience and careful observation: “Come on up, you get another one there, and boom, we’ve broken out of all this. And we’re right underneath that big resistance, this guy. So, if it were me, um I just wouldn’t do anything. I’d just sit in it and wait.”
